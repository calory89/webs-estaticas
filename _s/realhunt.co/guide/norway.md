## How to buy a home in Norway as a foreigner

The steps, papers and taxes, in plain words. General information, not legal advice: use a local lawyer before you sign.

### Can foreigners buy?

Yes. Anyone with the funds can buy property in Norway, and a built plot of up to 2 decares normally needs no concession (konsesjon) or self-declaration; owning property gives no right to live there.

### The steps

- Find a home and get loan approval: Search listings on Finn.no and get pre-approval if you need a mortgage; expect to put down 15 to 25%.
- Bid through the agent: Offers go through the seller's agent and bidding rounds can close within 24 hours, so inspect the home first.
- Sign the contract: The agent draws up the purchase contract; have a local lawyer review it.
- Pay at settlement: The price is paid through an escrow account at closing, and the deed is signed.
- Get a D-number if needed: Send Kartverket's D-number application with the deed and a certified passport copy no more than 3 months old.
- Register the deed: The deed (skjøte) is registered with Kartverket and the 2.5% document duty and fee are paid.

### Costs on top of the price

About 2.5-3%: Document duty (dokumentavgift) of 2.5% of market value, a registration fee (tinglysingsgebyr) of NOK 545 for the deed and NOK 545 for any mortgage document; a flat in a housing cooperative (borettslag) has no document duty; the seller normally pays the agent.

### Every year

Expect municipal property tax where it applies, municipal fees, insurance and upkeep. Costs such as municipal fees and property tax are deductible against rental income.

Yearly property tax: Some municipalities charge property tax (eiendomsskatt), at most 4 per mille of the value after a reduction of at least 30%, and some also give a basic deduction; many charge nothing.

Tax on rental income: Rental profit is normally taxed at 22%, and the Tax Administration says its rental rules also apply to owners who are tax resident abroad.
