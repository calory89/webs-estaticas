Papers, checks and who signs

The papers that prove who owns a house and what it owes, country by country
Every country keeps a public record of who owns a property and which debts sit on it, and in most of them you can read it before you pay anything. In Spain, Italy, Portugal and England a buyer can get the extract directly; in Germany you need to show a legitimate interest. Ask for it before the deposit, not after.
Who inspects the house before you buy it, country by country
It depends on who the country makes responsible. France obliges the seller to give you a file of technical surveys. In the Netherlands, Sweden, Germany and the United States no report comes with the house: an inspection is something the buyer orders and pays for. In Denmark the buyer's lawyer reviews a condition report before the contract becomes final.
Buying from a distance: power of attorney, who signs and what still needs you
Yes, in several countries the deed can be signed by someone holding your power of attorney: our files say so for Italy, France, Germany and Greece. A power signed in your own country normally needs an apostille to be accepted in another. Tax numbers can often be obtained at a consulate or through a representative, and in Japan the legal explanation can be given by video call.
Do I need a lawyer to buy a house abroad? Notary or lawyer, country by country
A notary is not your lawyer. In Germany, France, Italy, Spain, Portugal and the Netherlands the sale is signed before a notary, but in Germany the notary is expressly neutral. A lawyer of your own is optional in most countries and strongly recommended in Greece, Denmark, Bulgaria and Mexico. Our files put the fee between about 0.5% and 2% of the price.

Money: paying, running costs and rent

Opening a bank account and paying for a home in another country
If you live legally in an EU country, a bank in any EU country cannot refuse you a basic payment account just because you live elsewhere. Outside that right, each bank decides. Spain, Sweden and the Netherlands in practice expect a local account or one that can pay the notary on time; the price itself almost always travels by bank transfer to the notary or the seller, never in cash.
Renting out your home when you are not there: licence and tax, country by country
Before you count on any rent, check two things in the country of the house: whether a holiday let needs a licence or a registration number, and how rent paid to a non-resident is taxed. Spain, Italy and Portugal all require registration for tourist lets, and some cities have stopped giving new licences. We promise no income: the table below is about rules, not returns.
After the purchase: what a home abroad costs every year, in 20 countries
The yearly bill has three parts: the local property tax, the running costs of any house, and a few costs that only an owner from abroad meets, such as a tax representative in Japan, a bank trust fee in Mexico or a company in Bulgaria. The property tax ranges from a fixed fee in Sweden to about 1% of the value, on average, in large US cities.

Tax

Tax residency and the 183-day rule: how four countries really count
Owning a house does not make you a tax resident; living in it for long enough can. There is no single 183-day rule: Spain counts more than 183 days in the calendar year and also looks at where your economic interests are, the United Kingdom has several tests, the United States adds up three years and Germany looks at a continuous stay of more than six months.
For Americans: what to report to the IRS when you own a home or an account abroad
A home abroad that you hold directly is not reportable on the FBAR or on Form 8938, according to the IRS. The foreign bank account you open for it is another matter: the FBAR applies when your foreign accounts together exceed $10,000 at any time in the year. Rent from the house is part of your worldwide income, which US citizens report wherever they live.
Spain's wealth tax for owners who live abroad: from what amount it applies
A non-resident owner pays Spanish wealth tax only on the assets located in Spain, and the first €700,000 is exempt. Someone whose only Spanish asset is a house worth less than that owes nothing under the state rule.
Do I pay tax in my own country too on a home abroad?
Often yes, but usually not twice in full. The country where the house stands taxes it whoever you are. If your own country taxes residents on worldwide income, as the United Kingdom and the United States do, the rent and the gain go in your return there too, with relief for the tax already paid abroad. The relief is not automatic: you have to claim it.

Living there: residency, visits and pets

Buying a home in Europe with a British passport after Brexit
Brexit did not stop British citizens buying homes in the EU: in most countries they buy on the same terms as before. What changed is everything around the house. Without a visa you can spend 90 days in every 180 in the whole Schengen area, you are taxed as a non-EU owner and your pet needs a new certificate for every trip.
Living in the house you bought: residency, registration and health cover
No: in almost every country in our files, buying a home gives no right to live there, and the property routes that existed in Spain and Portugal have closed. EU citizens can live in another EU country anyway and register after three months. Everyone else needs a visa based on income, work or family, not on the house.
Taking a dog, cat or ferret to your home in another country
For the EU the order is: microchip, rabies vaccination, then the travel document. Between EU countries that document is the European pet passport, which only owners resident in the EU can get. From Great Britain and other non-EU countries it is an animal health certificate, issued by a vet for each trip. Dogs going to Finland, Ireland, Malta, Norway or Northern Ireland also need tapeworm treatment.

Risks and what can go wrong

Buying a house together when you are not married, or between friends
The law of the country where the house stands decides how co-owners hold it, not your relationship. In England you choose between two forms with opposite results on death. In Spain shares are presumed equal unless you write otherwise, and any co-owner can ask to divide the property at any time. Write the shares, and what happens if one of you wants out, into the deed.
What happens to your home in another country when you die: the EU rules
In the EU, your whole inheritance, including a house in another country, is normally handled by the authority of the EU country where you last lived, under that country's law. You can choose the law of your nationality instead, but you have to say so clearly in a will. Denmark and Ireland do not apply these rules, and inheritance tax is a separate matter in each country.
Buying a house at a court auction in Spain and Germany: deposit, minimum bid and what you take on
A court auction can be cheaper than the market, and it moves the risk to you. In Spain a bidder first deposits 20% of the valuation and, by bidding, accepts the title as it stands and the charges that rank before the debt being enforced. In Germany the security is one tenth of the market value set by the court, and a first-round bid under half that value is refused. You bid without the checks of a normal purchase.
What if I change my mind? Cooling-off periods and what you lose, by country
Only some countries give the buyer a legal right to withdraw for free: France, 10 calendar days, and the Netherlands, 3 days. In Germany and Sweden nothing binds you until the notary's contract or the signed purchase contract. In Spain, Greece, Bulgaria and Japan, once a deposit is paid, changing your mind costs you the deposit.
Seven myths about buying a home abroad, each with its figure
Most of what people believe about buying abroad is half true at best. Foreigners can buy in almost every country we cover, a cheap house gives residency in none of them, and the price in the listing is never what you pay. Each myth below comes with the figure that corrects it.
Why are houses so cheap in Japan?
Because there are more houses than households that want them. Japan's 2023 housing survey counted 9 million vacant dwellings, 13.8% of all homes, the highest share ever recorded. Empty houses find few buyers, so prices are low, and foreigners can buy them freehold on the same terms as Japanese citizens.
Why are houses so cheap in Italy?
Because more than a quarter of Italian homes have nobody living in them all year. The 2021 census counted 9.6 million dwellings not occupied by a resident, 27.2% of the total. Homes that nobody lives in find few buyers, and prices follow. The purchase tax is charged on the cadastral value, not on the price.
Why are houses so cheap in France?
Because France has 2.9 million vacant homes, 7.6% of its housing, and they are concentrated outside the cities. The price is not the whole cost: transfer taxes of about 5.8% to 6.3% and the notary come on top, and the seller must give you a file of surveys that tells you what needs fixing.
Why are houses so cheap in Bulgaria?
Because the country has fewer people every year. The 2021 census counted 6.5 million inhabitants, almost 845,000 fewer than ten years earlier. Fewer buyers mean low prices, and yearly taxes are among the lowest in our files. The catch for buyers from outside the EU is the land: they can own a building but not the plot under it.
Why are houses so cheap in Sweden?
We have found no single official figure that explains it, so we give what we can show: the price depends on where the house is, and the list below shows where the cheap Swedish houses on RealHunt are. Buying is simple and cheap: anyone can buy, and the stamp duty is 1.5%.

Words and language

Can I buy a house without speaking the language?
Yes. Our country files list no language requirement for buyers, but the deed is drawn up in it, and the law protects you by requiring a translation. In Germany the notary must have the deed translated for a party who does not know enough German; in Spain an interpreter is required when the notary cannot explain the content personally; in Bulgaria a sworn translator signs with you.

Canada

Can foreigners buy a home in Canada?
Mostly not for now: a federal ban stops non-Canadians from buying homes in cities and larger towns until January 1, 2027, with exceptions such as work permit holders and spouses of citizens or permanent residents. The ban does not cover rural areas and small towns, and the government has said it does not plan simply to extend it, but a replacement is being studied.
How much do you pay on top of the price?
About 1.5-4%. Work it out for your price
What is the yearly property tax?
Property tax is set by each city as a share of assessed value, from about 0.3% in Vancouver to 2.12% in Saint John in 2026.

Cyprus

Can foreigners buy a home in Cyprus?
EU citizens buy without restrictions. Non-EU buyers need permission from the Council of Ministers, applied for through the District Officer, and are normally limited to one home or a plot of up to 4,014 m².
How much do you pay on top of the price?
About 2.5-3.5%. Work it out for your price
What is the yearly property tax?
There is no annual national property tax (abolished in 2017); owners pay municipal fees of about €90 to €300 a year, plus sewerage and local authority charges.

Denmark

Can foreigners buy a home in Denmark?
Without Danish domicile or 5 years of past residence you need permission from the Department of Civil Affairs (Civilstyrelsen); EU/EEA citizens who live and are registered in Denmark can buy a permanent home without permission by signing a declaration to the Land Registration Court. Holiday homes (sommerhuse) need permission, which is only given to people with particularly strong ties to Denmark, such as many years of holidays there.
How much do you pay on top of the price?
About 1.5-3%. Work it out for your price
What is the yearly property tax?
Owners pay property value tax (ejendomsværdiskat) of 0.51% on 80% of the official valuation (1.4% above DKK 9.2 million) plus municipal land tax (grundskyld) at a rate set by each municipality, capped at 30 per mille of the land value.

Dominican Republic

Can foreigners buy a home in the Dominican Republic?
Yes. Foreigners have the same rights as Dominican citizens to buy land and homes, with no restrictions.

What is the yearly property tax?
The yearly IPI tax is 1% of the part of a person's total Dominican property value, as assessed by the tax office DGII, above RD$10,695,494 in 2026, so a single cheap home is often below the threshold.

France

Can foreigners buy a home in France?
Yes. Foreigners can buy property in France without major restrictions, and non-EU buyers do not need a permit, although the paperwork can take longer.

Greece

Can foreigners buy a home in Greece?
Yes. Buyers from outside the EU and EFTA need a permit to buy in designated border areas (parts of northern Greece and eastern Aegean islands such as the Dodecanese and Rhodes). The government has announced a 15% transfer tax for non-EU buyers of homes from 1 January 2027, not yet final in September 2026.
How much do you pay on top of the price?
About 5.5-7%. Work it out for your price
What is the yearly property tax?
Owners on 1 January pay the unified property tax (ENFIA), calculated per property from its location, size, age and official zone value, with an extra charge if total Greek property exceeds €500,000.

Netherlands

Can foreigners buy a home in the Netherlands?
Yes. There are no nationality or residence rules and no surcharge for foreign buyers. Some cities, such as The Hague, restrict renting out cheaper homes after purchase without a permit (opkoopbescherming).

Owners pay municipal property tax (onroerendezaakbelasting, OZB) on the official WOZ value, at a rate each municipality sets, which in practice is a per mille figure; tenants of homes do not pay it.

Switzerland

Can foreigners buy a home in Switzerland?
Under the Lex Koller law, EU/EFTA citizens living in Switzerland and holders of a C permit buy like Swiss citizens, and non-EU holders of a B permit can buy a home they live in. People living abroad need a cantonal permit and can usually only buy one holiday home in a tourist area, within national quotas; a proposed tightening was under consultation in 2026.
How much do you pay on top of the price?
About 1-5%. Work it out for your price
What is the yearly property tax?
More than half of the cantons charge an annual property tax (Liegenschaftssteuer), usually 0.01-0.3% of the assessed value, and owners also pay cantonal wealth tax on the property value minus the mortgage.
