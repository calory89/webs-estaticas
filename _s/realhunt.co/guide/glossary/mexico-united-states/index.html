Buying a home in Mexico and the United States: the local words, explained

On one side of the border a bank may have to hold the title for you; on the other, a company insures the title and a neutral account holds the money. The first six words are Mexican, the last six American.

zona restringida restricted zone

The strip within 50 km of the coast and 100 km of a border where foreigners cannot hold title directly.

It covers places such as Tulum and Playa del Carmen. Outside it you can own in your own name.

Source: RealHunt country file: Mexico

fideicomiso bank trust

A bank trust in which a Mexican bank holds the title for a foreign buyer in the restricted zone.

The alternative is a Mexican company. The foreign ministry (SRE) permit for a trust cost MXN 21,650 in 2026, and the bank charges a yearly fee of about US$450 to US$1,000.

Source: RealHunt country file: Mexico

ejido communal farm land

Communal agricultural land, which cannot be sold in the normal way.

Our Mexico guide tells buyers to confirm the land is not ejido when they check the seller's title, liens and disputes.

Source: RealHunt country file: Mexico

escritura pública public deed

Also: notario público

The public deed, prepared and signed by a public notary.

The notary registers it in the Public Registry. A separate buyer's lawyer is recommended for an independent review, and no deposit should be wired until the documents and the title have been reviewed.

Source: RealHunt country file: Mexico

ISAI acquisition tax

Also: impuesto sobre adquisición de inmuebles

The tax on acquiring property in Mexico.

Our Mexico file gives 2% to 5%, plus notary fees of 0.5% to 1% and legal fees of 1% to 1.5%, and 0.5% to 1.5% more for setting up the trust and its permit.

Source: RealHunt country file: Mexico

predial yearly property tax

Also: impuesto predial

The yearly local property tax in Mexico.

Usually 0.05% to 1.2% of the official cadastral value, which is often well below market value.

Source: RealHunt country file: Mexico

buyer's agent agreement written agreement with your agent

The written agreement a buyer signs with an agent before being shown homes in the United States.

It sets out how much the agent is paid and who pays. Since August 2024 the seller no longer has to pay the buyer's agent, so the buyer may pay unless the seller agrees to.

Source: RealHunt country file: United States

home inspection inspection of the house

An inspection of the condition of the house, often part of the buying process.

Our United States guide pairs it with a title search, so you know the condition of the house and that the title is clean before closing.

Sources: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau · RealHunt country file: United States

title insurance insurance on the title

Insurance against problems with the title, such as someone with a legal claim on the home.

The consumer bureau explains that the lender's policy only protects the lender; to protect yourself you may want to buy an owner's policy. Title search and title insurance are part of the closing costs.

Sources: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau · RealHunt country file: United States

escrow account held by a third party

An account set up by the mortgage lender to pay property taxes and home insurance out of your monthly payment.

If your loan has no such account, or you buy without a loan, you pay those bills yourself.

Source: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau

HOA dues owners' association fees

Also: homeowners' association

The fees paid to the owners' association of a condo building or a planned community.

They vary widely and are usually paid separately from the mortgage. The consumer bureau warns that if you do not pay them you can face debt collection and even foreclosure.

Source: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau

closing the day the sale completes

Also: closing costs

The meeting at which the balance and the closing costs are paid and the sale completes.

The deed is then recorded with the county. Closing costs vary by state and typically run 2% to 5% of the loan amount. There is no federal ban on foreigners buying homes, but about 30 states have added some limits.

Sources: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau · RealHunt country file: United States

FIRPTA withholding when a foreigner sells

Also: Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act

The rule under which the buyer generally withholds 15% of the sale price when a foreign owner sells.

It does not apply when you buy, only when you later sell.

Source: RealHunt country file: United States
