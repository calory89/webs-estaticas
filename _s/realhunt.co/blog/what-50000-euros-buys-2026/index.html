What €50,000 Buys in 2026: 140 Checked Homes, Country by Country

We sorted every home on RealHunt by price. 46 of 140 cost €50,000 or less, and almost all of them are in three countries.

RealHunt Research · 2026-09-23

€50,000 is roughly what a new mid-size car costs, or a deposit on a flat in most European cities. We wanted to know what it buys if you spend it on a whole house instead, so we sorted every home currently published on RealHunt by price.

The short answer: 46 of the 140 homes on the site cost €50,000 or less. They are not spread evenly. 29 are in Italy, 9 in Japan and 6 in Sweden. France and Denmark have one each. Spain, the United States, Canada and Mexico have none under that line.

Every home here passed the same check: a person looked at the photos and the listing, and anything that needs a new roof, is a ruin, is only land or has a single photo was left out. So these are cheap homes you could sleep in, not projects.

Italy: the most homes for the money

Italy has the cheapest home on the site, a stone house in a Lazio hill town for €12,000, about 55 minutes from Rome's Ciampino airport. Half of the 39 Italian homes cost €41,000 or less. At this price you are mostly buying in small inland villages: Tuscany, Lazio, Umbria and Abruzzo come up again and again. Expect thick stone walls, a small kitchen, and a piazza five minutes away on foot.

What the price hides: Italy's buying costs are the highest on our list. For a second home, registration tax is 9% of the cadastral value (a tax value that is usually well below the price), and the notary and registry add about €2,500. Our cost estimate adds roughly 10% plus €2,500 to be safe, so a €30,000 house is closer to €35,500 all in.

Japan: bigger houses, lower running costs

Japan's cheapest homes on RealHunt start at €18,400 and the middle of the list is €51,980. 9 of 18 are under €50,000. For that money the houses tend to be larger than in Italy, often with a garden, and many are near the coast in Wakayama, Kyoto prefecture and Okayama. Foreigners can own land and houses outright in Japan, with no residency needed to buy.

Sweden: where €50,000 is the entry ticket

Sweden's median on our list is €72,000, higher than Italy or Japan, and only 6 of 41 Swedish homes are under €50,000. But what you get is often a detached wooden house with a big plot, near a lake, with a wood stove. Anyone can buy in Sweden, and the only real buying cost is a 1.5% stamp duty. There is no notary.

North America starts higher

In the United States the cheapest move-in-ready home we have published is €65,428 (a brick row house in McKees Rocks, Pennsylvania, asking $75,000). Canada starts at €55,842 and Mexico at €50,575. Those markets have plenty of cheaper listings, but at the very bottom many are sold as investor projects: boarded windows, fire damage, or a plot with a trailer on it. They do not pass our quality rule.

What we take from this

If €50,000 is your hard ceiling, Italy is where you will have the most choice by far, Japan is where you get the most house, and Sweden is where you get the most land. Add 2% (Sweden) to 10% (Italy) on top for taxes and fees, and always pay for a survey before you sign.
