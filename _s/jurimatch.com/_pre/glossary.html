    No-win-no-fee terms, explained

    What the words in a fee agreement mean, and how the main ways of paying for a claim compare. General information; the rules depend on your country.

    How the main fee models compare

    • Hourly fees

      If you lose: All your lawyer's fees

      If you win: All your lawyer's fees

      Risk: You

    • Contingency fee / cuota litis

      If you lose: No lawyer fee (expenses and adverse costs may apply)

      If you win: A share of what you recover

      Risk: Lawyer, mostly

    • Conditional fee agreement (CFA)

      If you lose: No lawyer fee (adverse costs unless insured)

      If you win: Base fees plus a success fee, partly recovered from the other side

      Risk: Lawyer, plus ATE insurer

    • Damages-based agreement (DBA)

      If you lose: No lawyer fee (adverse costs unless insured)

      If you win: A capped share of damages

      Risk: Lawyer

    • Litigation funding

      If you lose: Nothing to the funder (non-recourse)

      If you win: The funder's share of the recovery

      Risk: Funder

    Terms A to Z

    Adverse costs (loser pays) (Most countries outside the United States)
    The rule that the losing party pays part of the winner's legal costs. It is why a no-win-no-fee agreement with your own lawyer does not always mean you risk nothing.See also: After-the-event (ATE) insurance, Litigation funding (third-party funding)
    After-the-event (ATE) insurance (Mainly England and Wales)
    Insurance bought after a dispute arises that pays the other side's costs, and often your own expenses, if you lose. The premium is frequently deferred and payable only if you win.See also: Adverse costs (loser pays), Conditional fee agreement (CFA)
    American rule on costs (United States)
    The principle that each side pays its own lawyer whatever the result, unless a statute or contract says otherwise. It is one reason contingency fees work well in the US: losing usually does not mean paying the other side's lawyer.See also: Costs follow the event, Contingency fee, Adverse costs (loser pays)
    Burofax (Spain)
    A certified communication sent through the Spanish postal service with proof of content and delivery. It is commonly used to send a formal demand before a claim, and a demand received by the debtor can interrupt the limitation period.See also: Letter before action, Proceso monitorio, Limitation period
    Claims management company (CMC) (United Kingdom)
    A business that handles claims for a share of the compensation, often passing them to a law firm. In Great Britain, CMCs have been regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority since 2019. Many claims can be made directly for free, so check before signing.See also: No win, no fee, Damages-based agreement (DBA)
    Class action (United States, Canada, Australia and others)
    A single case brought by one or more representatives on behalf of a larger group with similar claims. Members are often included unless they opt out, and any settlement usually needs the court's approval. Lawyers in class actions are frequently paid on a contingency basis.See also: EU representative action, Group litigation order (GLO), Contingency fee
    Conditional fee agreement (CFA) (England and Wales)
    The lawyer charges normal fees only if you win, plus a success fee on top of those fees. The success fee cannot exceed 100% of the base costs, and in personal injury claims it is capped at 25% of certain damages.See also: Damages-based agreement (DBA), After-the-event (ATE) insurance
    Contingency fee (United States, Canada, Australia and others)
    The lawyer's fee is a percentage of the money recovered and nothing is due if there is no recovery. The percentage, and whether expenses are deducted before or after it, is set in the written agreement and is limited by local rules in some places.See also: No win, no fee, Damages-based agreement (DBA)
    Costs follow the event (England and Wales, Ireland and many other countries)
    The general principle that the losing party pays the winning party's legal costs, usually only part of them, as assessed by the court. Courts can depart from it, for example when the winner behaved unreasonably.See also: Adverse costs (loser pays), American rule on costs, After-the-event (ATE) insurance
    Cuota litis (Spain and much of Latin America)
    An agreement where the lawyer's fee depends on the result, usually a percentage of what is obtained. In Spain the professional ban on it was struck down by the Supreme Court in 2008, so it can be agreed freely in writing.See also: Success fee, Contingency fee
    Damages-based agreement (DBA) (England and Wales)
    The lawyer takes a percentage of the damages you recover instead of hourly fees. The cap, including VAT, is 25% in personal injury claims, 35% in employment claims and 50% in other claims.See also: Conditional fee agreement (CFA), Contingency fee
    Disbursements (General)
    Expenses a lawyer pays to third parties while running your case, such as court fees, expert reports, medical records and translations. Some no-win-no-fee agreements cover them if you lose; others make you pay them whatever the result. The agreement should say which.See also: No win, no fee, After-the-event (ATE) insurance
    Disclosure of documents (England and Wales; called discovery in the United States)
    The stage of a case in which each side must reveal relevant documents, including some that harm its own case. It can be costly and time-consuming, which is one reason lawyers and funders weigh it when deciding whether to take a case.See also: Litigation funding (third-party funding)
    Erfolgshonorar (Germany)
    A fee that depends on success. It is generally not allowed for lawyers, with exceptions: for example claims of up to 2,000 euros, certain debt collection work, and cases where the client could not otherwise afford to bring the claim.See also: Success fee, Litigation funding (third-party funding)
    EU representative action (European Union)
    A collective action brought by an approved organisation (a "qualified entity") on behalf of consumers, under Directive (EU) 2020/1828, to stop an unlawful practice or obtain redress. Each member state sets its own details, including whether consumers opt in or opt out.See also: Class action, Litigation funding (third-party funding)
    EU261 flight compensation (European Union, and flights covered by the UK version)
    Fixed compensation under Regulation (EC) No 261/2004 of €250, €400 or €600 depending on flight distance, for long delays at arrival, late cancellations and denied boarding, unless the airline proves extraordinary circumstances. The UK version pays similar amounts in pounds.See also: Small claims procedure, Limitation period
    European order for payment (European Union (except Denmark))
    A procedure under Regulation (EC) No 1896/2006 to recover uncontested money claims across EU borders using standard forms. If the debtor does not object, the order becomes enforceable in the other member states.See also: Proceso monitorio, European Small Claims Procedure
    European Small Claims Procedure (European Union (except Denmark))
    A written procedure for cross-border claims of up to €5,000 under Regulation (EC) No 861/2007, using standard forms and usually without a hearing. A lawyer is not required.See also: Small claims procedure, European order for payment
    Funding multiple (General)
    A way of setting a litigation funder's return as a multiple of the money it invests in the case, often compared with, or combined with, a percentage of the recovery. The funding agreement should state how the return is calculated and in what order the proceeds are paid out.See also: Litigation funding (third-party funding), Non-recourse funding
    Group litigation order (GLO) (England and Wales)
    A court order that manages many separate claims raising common issues together. Claimants usually join by being entered on a group register, and decisions on the common issues bind the claims on the register.See also: Class action, Litigation funding (third-party funding)
    Honoraire de résultat (France)
    A bonus linked to the result. A fee based only on the result (pacte de quota litis) is not allowed, but a result-based fee can be added to a fixed or hourly fee if it is agreed in writing.See also: Success fee
    Honorários de êxito (Brazil)
    A success fee agreed between client and lawyer, often alongside a smaller fixed fee. It is separate from the honorários de sucumbência, the costs a court orders the losing side to pay to the winner's lawyer.See also: Success fee, Contingency fee, Adverse costs (loser pays)
    Late payment interest (European Union and United Kingdom)
    Statutory interest a business can charge on late payment by another business. Under the EU Late Payment Directive the rate is at least 8 percentage points above the reference rate, plus a fixed €40 compensation per invoice; the UK has its own similar rules. Our free calculator works it out.See also: Proceso monitorio, Letter before action
    Legal expenses insurance (BTE) (General, common in Germany, Austria, Scandinavia and the UK)
    Insurance bought before any dispute arises ("before the event"), often included in home, car or bank policies, that pays legal costs if a covered dispute comes up. Check your policies before agreeing anything else: the insurer may have to approve the lawyer and the costs.See also: After-the-event (ATE) insurance, Adverse costs (loser pays)
    Letter before action (England and Wales, and similar formal demand letters elsewhere)
    A formal letter to the other side setting out the claim, what you want and a deadline to respond, sent before starting court proceedings. Courts expect it, and skipping it can affect who pays the costs. Our free claim letter tool helps you draft one.See also: Limitation period, Burofax
    Limitation period (General)
    The deadline for starting a claim. Once it has passed the claim is usually barred, so check it early; periods differ by country and type of claim and can be paused or restarted in some situations.See also: No win, no fee
    Litigation funding (third-party funding) (General)
    A company that is not a party to the case pays the legal costs in exchange for a share of the money recovered. Funding is usually non-recourse: if the case is lost, you do not repay the funder.See also: Adverse costs (loser pays), No win, no fee
    No foil, no fee (Ireland)
    The Irish form of no-win-no-fee: the solicitor charges no fee if the case is lost. Irish solicitors may not charge a percentage of the damages, and you may still face the other side's costs if you lose.See also: No win, no fee, Adverse costs (loser pays)
    No win, no fee (General)
    An arrangement where you do not pay your own lawyer's fees if the case is lost. If you win, the lawyer is paid from the result or recovers fees from the other side. Court fees, expert costs and the other side's costs may still apply, so read the agreement.See also: Contingency fee, Conditional fee agreement (CFA), After-the-event (ATE) insurance
    Non-recourse funding (General)
    Funding that is repaid only out of what the case recovers. If the case is lost, you owe the funder nothing. Most third-party litigation funding works this way, but check whether the agreement also covers the other side's costs.See also: Litigation funding (third-party funding), Funding multiple, Adverse costs (loser pays)
    Out-of-court settlement (General)
    An agreement that ends a dispute without a court judgment, usually in exchange for a payment. Most claims settle. On a no-win-no-fee agreement, a settlement normally counts as a win, so the lawyer's success fee or percentage applies to the amount agreed.See also: No win, no fee, Success fee
    Pactum de quota litis (Civil-law countries)
    Latin for an agreement under which a lawyer's fee is only a share of what the client wins. Many civil-law countries forbid it while allowing a success bonus on top of a base fee; Spain has allowed it since 2008.See also: Cuota litis, Honoraire de résultat, Erfolgshonorar, Patto di quota lite
    Patto di quota lite (Italy)
    An agreement giving the lawyer a share of the asset or sum in dispute. Since 2012 Italian law forbids it again, but a fee calculated as a percentage of the value of the case, agreed in writing in advance, is allowed.See also: Success fee, Cuota litis
    Proceso monitorio (Spain)
    A fast Spanish court procedure to recover a money debt backed by documents, such as invoices or a signed contract. The initial application can be filed without a lawyer. If the debtor does not pay or object, the court can order enforcement.See also: Burofax, European order for payment, Cuota litis
    Prozessfinanzierung (Germany, Austria and Switzerland)
    The German term for third-party litigation funding: a funder pays the court and lawyer costs, and the other side's costs if the case is lost, in return for a share of the proceeds agreed in the contract. Funders usually look for larger claims with good prospects.See also: Litigation funding (third-party funding), Erfolgshonorar, Non-recourse funding
    Qualified one-way costs shifting (QOCS) (England and Wales)
    A rule for most personal injury claims under which a claimant who loses is generally not ordered to pay the defendant's costs out of their own pocket. Costs orders can still be set off against damages, and the protection can be lost, for example if the claim is found to be fundamentally dishonest.See also: Adverse costs (loser pays), Conditional fee agreement (CFA)
    Security for costs (England and Wales, Ireland, Australia and other common-law systems)
    An order requiring a claimant to pay money into court or give a guarantee to cover the defendant's costs if the claim fails, for example when the claimant lives abroad or is a company that may not be able to pay. Funders and ATE insurers can sometimes provide it.See also: Adverse costs (loser pays), After-the-event (ATE) insurance, Litigation funding (third-party funding)
    Small claims procedure (General)
    A simpler and cheaper court procedure for low-value claims, designed so that people can bring a claim without a lawyer. The value limit and the costs rules differ by country; in England and Wales the small claims track generally covers claims up to £10,000.See also: European Small Claims Procedure, Adverse costs (loser pays)
    Statute of limitations (United States)
    The US name for the law that sets the deadline for filing a lawsuit. Each state sets its own periods by type of claim, and federal claims have their own, so the same claim can have different deadlines in different states.See also: Limitation period
    Success fee (General)
    Any fee, or part of a fee, that is payable only if the case succeeds. How it is calculated, and whether it is allowed at all, depends on the country.See also: Conditional fee agreement (CFA), Cuota litis, Erfolgshonorar
