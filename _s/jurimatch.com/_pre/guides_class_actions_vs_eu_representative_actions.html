    US class actions vs EU representative actions

    Last updated: October 31, 2026 · Editorial policy

    When many people suffer the same harm from the same company, collective procedures let them claim together. The United States has a long tradition of class actions. The European Union took a different route: Directive (EU) 2020/1828 on representative actions, which Member States had to apply from June 2023. This guide compares the two. It is general information, not legal advice.

    Key points

    • US class actions let one or more representatives sue for a whole group, and members are usually included unless they opt out.
    • In the EU, Directive (EU) 2020/1828 lets approved organisations bring representative actions for consumers, applied by Member States from June 2023.
    • Each Member State sets key details itself, such as whether consumers opt in or opt out.

    Reviewed on October 31, 2026

    At a glance

    • Who brings the claim

      US: One or more individuals (class representatives) on behalf of everyone in a defined class, through their lawyers.

      EU: A qualified entity, typically a consumer organisation designated by a Member State. Individuals cannot start one themselves.

    • Main legal basis

      US: Rule 23 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure in federal courts; similar state rules.

      EU: Directive (EU) 2020/1828, transposed into each Member State's national law.

    • Scope

      US: Any area of law where the class requirements are met: consumer, securities, antitrust, employment, product liability and more.

      EU: Breaches of the EU laws listed in the Directive's annex, which include consumer protection, data protection, financial services, travel and passenger rights, among others.

    • Opt-in or opt-out

      US: Damages classes are normally opt-out: class members are included unless they exclude themselves.

      EU: Each Member State chooses opt-in, opt-out or a mix. Consumers not habitually resident in that Member State must opt in to redress.

    • Lawyers' fees

      US: Contingency fees are common and are approved by the court from any settlement or judgment.

      EU: Depends on national rules. The Directive does not introduce contingency fees.

    • Costs if the claim fails

      US: Each side generally pays its own lawyers (the American rule).

      EU: The loser-pays principle applies under national law; individual consumers are generally not liable for the costs.

    • Damages

      US: Compensatory damages; punitive or statutory damages are possible under some laws.

      EU: Compensation for the harm suffered. The Directive excludes punitive damages.

    • Third-party funding

      US: Used, with disclosure rules varying between courts.

      EU: Allowed where national law permits it, with safeguards against conflicts of interest and undue influence by funders.

    • Court approval of settlements

      US: Required; class members are notified and can object.

      EU: Required; the court or authority checks the settlement is fair, and individuals may be able to accept or refuse it depending on national rules.

    US class actions in brief

    In federal court a class can be certified only if the class is so numerous that joining everyone is impractical, there are common questions of law or fact, the representatives' claims are typical of the class, and the representatives and their lawyers will protect the class adequately. For damages classes, common questions must predominate and a class action must be the superior way to decide the dispute. Certification is often the decisive stage. Broad pre-trial discovery, contingency fees and the rule that each side pays its own lawyers make the system accessible to claimants, while defendants face significant exposure.

    EU representative actions in brief

    The Directive requires every Member State to have at least one procedure through which qualified entities can seek injunctions and redress (such as compensation, repair, replacement or refunds) for consumers harmed by breaches of listed EU laws. Entities that bring cross-border actions must meet common criteria, including being non-profit and independent. Member States keep their own procedures, so the details vary: for example, the Netherlands and Germany have dedicated collective redress laws, and some Member States transposed the Directive late.

    The Directive keeps the loser-pays principle, excludes punitive damages, and allows third-party funding where national law permits it, with rules to prevent conflicts of interest. See how to choose a litigation funder.

    What it means for you

    • In the US, you are usually included automatically in a damages class; watch for a notice and the deadline to opt out or file a claim.
    • In the EU, check whether a consumer organisation has announced an action and whether you need to register (opt in).
    • Joining a collective action may affect your right to sue individually. Read the terms before you register or opt out.
    • Deadlines still matter. The Directive requires that a pending representative action suspends or interrupts limitation periods for the consumers concerned, and US law has its own tolling rules, but how this applies to you depends on the country and the case.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I start an EU representative action myself?
    No. Under Directive (EU) 2020/1828 only qualified entities designated by a Member State, usually consumer organisations, can bring a representative action. You can contact such an organisation, or join an action once it is announced.
    Is the UK covered by the EU Directive?
    No. The UK left the EU before the Directive applied. England and Wales has its own collective routes, including opt-out collective proceedings for competition claims before the Competition Appeal Tribunal, group litigation orders and representative actions.
    Are EU representative actions opt-out like US class actions?
    Some are. The Directive lets each Member State choose an opt-in system, an opt-out system or a combination, so the answer depends on the country where the action is brought. Consumers from other Member States must opt in to benefit from redress.
    Do I pay anything to be part of a class or representative action?
    Usually not upfront. In the US, lawyers are typically paid from the recovery with court approval. In the EU, it depends on national rules and on how the qualified entity is funded; check the terms before you register.
