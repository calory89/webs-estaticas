    After-the-event (ATE) insurance in the UK, explained

    Last updated: October 31, 2026 · Editorial policy

    In England and Wales the losing party is usually ordered to pay a large part of the winner's legal costs. A no-win-no-fee agreement with your own lawyer does not protect you from that. After-the-event (ATE) insurance does: it is bought after a dispute has arisen and pays the other side's costs, and often your own expenses, if you lose. This guide explains how it works. It is general information, not legal or insurance advice.

    Key points

    • In England and Wales the loser is usually ordered to pay a large part of the winner's costs, and a no-win-no-fee agreement with your own lawyer does not cover that.
    • After-the-event (ATE) insurance, bought once a dispute has arisen, pays the other side's costs and often your own expenses if you lose.
    • Premiums are often deferred and payable only if you win; since April 2013 they generally come out of your damages, not from the losing side.

    Reviewed on October 31, 2026

    What ATE insurance usually covers

    • Adverse costs: the other side's legal costs you are ordered to pay if you lose, up to the limit of indemnity.
    • Your own disbursements: expert fees, court fees and other expenses, if the policy includes them.

    It does not normally cover your own lawyer's fees under a conditional fee agreement (those are waived if you lose) or any damages awarded against you.

    How premiums work

    • Deferred: you do not pay when the policy starts; the premium is due at the end of the case.
    • Contingent: the premium is payable only if you win, so nothing is owed if you lose. Many policies are both deferred and contingent.
    • Staged: the premium increases as the case progresses, so an early settlement costs less.

    Since 1 April 2013, ATE premiums can generally no longer be recovered from the losing side, apart from a limited exception for expert reports in clinical negligence claims. In practice, the premium usually comes out of your damages. Ask for the premium in writing at each stage and include it when you compare offers with the case value calculator.

    When you may need it, and when you may not

    • Commercial and consumer claims, professional negligence, group actions: adverse costs can be significant, so ATE insurance is common, often arranged by your lawyer or required by a litigation funder.
    • Most personal injury claims: qualified one-way costs shifting (QOCS) generally protects claimants from adverse costs, so ATE is used mainly for disbursements.
    • Small claims track: the costs that can be recovered are very limited, so ATE is rarely needed. See suing without a lawyer.
    • Lower-value claims under fixed recoverable costs: the costs the winner can recover are capped, which reduces, but does not remove, adverse-costs risk.

    Before buying ATE, check whether your home, car or other insurance includes legal expenses cover taken out before the dispute (before-the-event, or BTE, insurance).

    Conditions to read carefully

    • Duties to follow your lawyer's advice and to keep the insurer informed.
    • What happens if you reject a settlement offer your lawyers consider reasonable.
    • Whether the insurer can cancel cover, and whether it is then backdated.
    • Anti-avoidance endorsements, which some defendants or courts ask for when ATE is used as security for costs.
    • The limit of indemnity and whether it is enough for the likely costs of the other side.
    • The insurer's financial strength, especially for large cases.

    Frequently asked questions

    Do I need ATE insurance for a personal injury claim?
    Often less than in other claims. In most personal injury cases in England and Wales, qualified one-way costs shifting (QOCS) generally protects a losing claimant from paying the defendant's costs, subject to exceptions. ATE may still be used to cover your own expenses, such as expert reports.
    Can I recover the ATE premium from the other side if I win?
    Generally not for agreements made since 1 April 2013, when the rules changed. The main exception is part of the premium for expert reports in clinical negligence cases. Otherwise the premium is usually paid out of your damages.
    What if I lose and the premium was deferred?
    With a deferred and contingent (sometimes called self-insured) premium, the premium is payable only if you win, so you would not pay it if you lose. Check the policy wording to confirm.
    Can the insurer cancel my policy?
    Yes, in certain situations: typically if you do not follow your lawyer's advice, reject a settlement your lawyers consider reasonable, or gave inaccurate information. Read these conditions before you sign.

    General information about England and Wales, not legal or insurance advice. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own rules.
