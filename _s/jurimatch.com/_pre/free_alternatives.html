    Free ways to resolve a complaint before hiring a lawyer

    Last updated: October 31, 2026

    Many disputes with banks, insurers, airlines and shops can be resolved for free, without a lawyer. Ombudsmen, regulators and conciliation bodies exist for exactly this. They are worth trying first for smaller claims, and a no-win-no-fee lawyer will often ask whether you have used them. This page lists the main official routes in selected countries. It is general information, not legal advice.

    Key points

    • Many disputes with banks, insurers, airlines and shops can be resolved for free through ombudsmen, regulators and conciliation bodies.
    • These routes are worth trying first for smaller claims, and no-win-no-fee lawyers often ask whether you have used them.
    • Each body has its own rules and time limits, so check them on its official site before you rely on it.

    Before you start

    1. Complain to the business in writing. Our free complaint letter template helps.
    2. Keep copies of everything: contracts, receipts, emails and the reply.
    3. Check your deadline with the limitation checker: a free route may not stop the clock.

    United Kingdom

    Complain to the business first. Financial firms have up to eight weeks to send a final response; after that, or once you have the final response, you can go to the ombudsman.

    • Financial Ombudsman Service (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free for consumers. Handles complaints about banks, lenders, insurers, investments and payment services. You generally have six months from the firm's final response to refer your complaint.

    • Civil Aviation Authority: passenger complaints (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Explains your flight rights and, for airlines that are not members of an approved dispute resolution scheme, can help with complaints.

    Spain

    Complain in writing to the company's customer service (Servicio de Atención al Cliente) first, and keep proof. Since April 2025 most civil claims also require a prior attempt to settle, which a documented complaint can help show.

    • Banco de España: complaints service (Portal del Cliente Bancario) (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free. For complaints about banks and credit institutions once the bank has rejected your complaint or not replied in time. Its reports are not binding on the bank.

    • CNMV: investor complaints (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free. For complaints about investment firms and products, after complaining to the firm.

    • Consumer arbitration (Sistema Arbitral de Consumo) (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free and voluntary for both sides. The decision is binding, but the business must have agreed to arbitration (look for the arbitration badge, or ask).

    • AESA: air passenger complaints (official site, opens in a new tab)

      For delays, cancellations and denied boarding on flights departing from Spain, after complaining to the airline.

    Germany

    Many sectors have conciliation bodies (Schlichtungsstellen) that are free or nearly free for consumers. Their proposals are usually not binding on you, so you can still go to court.

    • Versicherungsombudsmann (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free for consumers. For disputes with insurers that are members; up to a certain value its decisions bind the insurer.

    • Ombudsmann der privaten Banken (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free for consumers. For disputes with private banks that are members. Savings banks and cooperative banks have their own ombudsman schemes.

    • söp: Schlichtungsstelle für den öffentlichen Personenverkehr (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free for passengers. For disputes with member airlines, rail and bus companies, such as delays and cancellations.

    • Universalschlichtungsstelle des Bundes (official site, opens in a new tab)

      General consumer conciliation body for disputes where no specialised body is responsible.

    Ireland

    Complain to the provider first and follow its internal complaints process.

    • Financial Services and Pensions Ombudsman (FSPO) (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free. Handles complaints about banks, insurers, investment firms and pension providers. Its decisions are legally binding on both parties, subject to appeal to the High Court.

    United States

    Federal and state agencies accept complaints about financial products and businesses. They are not courts and do not award damages directly, but they forward complaints and can prompt a response.

    • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB): submit a complaint (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free. Forwards complaints about credit cards, mortgages, bank accounts, loans, debt collection and credit reports to the company, which is expected to respond.

    • Your state attorney general's consumer protection office (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free. Accepts consumer complaints and can mediate some disputes with local businesses.

    Cross-border in the EU

    If you bought from a trader in another EU country, Iceland or Norway, a European Consumer Centre can help for free. The EU's online dispute resolution (ODR) platform closed in July 2025.

    • European Consumer Centres Network (ECC-Net) (official site, opens in a new tab)

      Free advice and help with cross-border consumer disputes, including contacting the trader on your behalf. Contact the centre in your own country.

    • European Small Claims Procedure (official site, opens in a new tab)

      If the free routes fail, a simplified written court procedure for cross-border claims of lower value. See our guide to suing without a lawyer.

    When a lawyer makes sense

    Free routes work best for clear, lower-value complaints. For larger or complex claims, group claims, or when a free route has failed, a lawyer can help, often on a no-win-no-fee basis. See suing without a lawyer in small claims courts, browse no-win-no-fee lawyers, or read the company guides, for example Ryanair or Santander.

    Frequently asked questions

    Do I need to complain to the company before going to an ombudsman?
    Almost always. Ombudsmen and complaint services usually require you to complain to the business first and give it time to respond. Keep a copy of your complaint and the reply.
    Is an ombudsman decision binding?
    It depends. Some bodies, such as the UK Financial Ombudsman Service (if you accept its decision) and the Irish FSPO, issue decisions that bind the firm. Others, such as the Banco de España complaints service, issue reports the business does not have to follow.
    Does using a free route stop the limitation clock?
    Not always. Some procedures suspend limitation periods and others do not. If your deadline is close, check it with the limitation checker and speak to a lawyer before relying on a complaint route.

    General information, not legal advice. Check each body's own website for its current rules, time limits and eligibility.
