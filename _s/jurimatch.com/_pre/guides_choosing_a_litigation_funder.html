    How to choose a litigation funder

    Last updated: October 31, 2026 · Editorial policy

    A litigation funder is a company that pays some or all of the costs of a legal claim in exchange for a share of any money recovered. Funding can make a strong claim possible when you cannot or do not want to pay legal costs yourself. This guide explains how funders decide, how they are paid and what to compare. It is general information, not legal or financial advice.

    Key points

    • A litigation funder pays some or all of the costs of a claim in exchange for a share of any money recovered.
    • Funding is usually non-recourse: if the case is lost, you do not repay the funder.
    • Funders look for strong merits, a likely recovery several times the budget and a defendant who can actually pay.

    Reviewed on October 31, 2026

    What a funder pays for

    • Your lawyers' fees, or the part not covered by a no-win-no-fee arrangement.
    • Court fees, expert reports and other expenses (disbursements).
    • In "loser pays" countries, often the premium for after-the-event (ATE) insurance or security for the other side's costs.

    Funding is usually non-recourse: if the case is lost, you do not repay the funder.

    What funders look for

    • Strong merits. Most funders want a lawyer's written assessment showing good prospects on liability.
    • Value against budget. The likely recovery needs to be several times the budget, so that the funder's return and your share both make sense.
    • A defendant who can pay. Winning is not enough; the money must be collectable.
    • A clear budget and timetable, prepared by experienced lawyers.

    Many funders only consider commercial claims or group actions above a minimum size. Smaller individual claims are more often handled by no-win-no-fee lawyers.

    How funders are paid

    Common structures are a multiple of the money invested, a percentage of the recovery, or the greater of the two. Many increase the longer the case runs. Ask the funder to show, in a table, what you would receive if the case settles early, settles late, or wins at trial for different amounts. Our case value calculator helps you compare.

    Check the order of payment from any recovery (the "waterfall"): who is paid first, and whether lawyers' deferred fees, the funder's return and insurance premiums all come before you.

    Terms to read closely

    • Control and settlement. Who decides whether to accept an offer, and what happens if you and the funder disagree. Some agreements refer disputes to an independent lawyer.
    • Termination. When the funder can stop funding (for example if prospects drop) and what you then owe.
    • Adverse costs. Whether the funder covers the other side's costs if you lose, or whether you need insurance.
    • Budget overruns. What happens if the case costs more than planned.
    • Confidentiality and disclosure. Whether the existence of funding must be disclosed to the court or the other side.

    Checking the funder

    • Ask about its capital: does it have the money committed to see your case through, and from whom?
    • In England and Wales, check whether it is a member of the Association of Litigation Funders, which has a code of conduct, including capital adequacy rules.
    • Ask for references from lawyers who have worked with it, and how many cases it has funded to the end.
    • Be wary of anyone asking you for an upfront fee to "assess" or "secure" funding.

    Rules differ by country

    Litigation funding is well established in England and Wales, Australia and the United States, and growing in Europe. In England and Wales, a 2023 Supreme Court decision (known as PACCAR) affected agreements under which funders take a percentage of damages, and reform to reverse its effect has been proposed: check the current position with your lawyer. There is no EU-wide regulation of funders yet. In collective actions under the EU Representative Actions Directive, Member States must prevent funders from exercising undue influence. See our guide to class and representative actions.

    Frequently asked questions

    Do I have to repay a litigation funder if I lose?
    Usually not. Most litigation funding is non-recourse: if the case fails, the funder loses its investment and you owe it nothing. Check the agreement for exceptions, for example if you breach its terms or abandon the case without the funder's consent.
    How much does litigation funding cost?
    There is no standard price. Funders are commonly paid a multiple of the money they invested, a percentage of the recovery, or the greater of the two, often rising over time. The return reflects the risk and how long the case may take, so compare offers on what you would keep in realistic scenarios.
    Will the funder control my case?
    It should not. Reputable funders agree not to take control of litigation or settlement decisions, though they usually receive information and may have rights if you reject a settlement they consider reasonable. Read those clauses carefully.
    Is litigation funding the same as a no-win-no-fee lawyer?
    No. A no-win-no-fee lawyer defers or waives their own fees. A funder is a separate company that pays costs, such as lawyers' fees, experts and sometimes adverse-costs insurance, in exchange for a share of the result. The two are often combined.
