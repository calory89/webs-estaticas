How to check a Japanese building confirmation for an off-plan condominium

In Japan the plans of a new building must be reviewed against the Building Standards Act in a building confirmation (kenchiku kakunin), and the finished works must pass a completion inspection. The Building Standards Act lets anyone view the building plan summary, which records the building’s outline and its confirmation and inspections. This guide uses pages of the Tokyo Metropolitan Government and Minato City, read on 27 November 2026.

Step by step

  1. Ask the developer for the building confirmation number and date shown on the confirmation certificate (kakunin-zumisho), the lot number (chimei chiban) of the site and the name of the building owner (kenchikunushi) who applied.
  2. Work out who holds the file. The Tokyo page says the summary can be viewed at the prefecture or at a city with a building official. In Tokyo’s 23 wards, the metropolitan government holds buildings over 10,000 square metres of total floor area from fiscal 2000; Minato City, for example, holds those up to 10,000 square metres, including ones confirmed by designated private inspection bodies.
  3. Prepare the three items Tokyo asks for: the lot number at the time of building (not the residential address), the building owner’s name at the time, and the date and number of the confirmation or inspection certificate (not the ward’s reception number). Floor areas, floors, structure, name and use help identify the building.
  4. View the summary. At the Tokyo and Minato counters viewing is free and a printed copy costs 10 yen a page. Since March 2024 Tokyo also offers an online viewing system, but it needs an account with an uploaded identity document, and the online copy leaves out the location map, the site plan, seals and private owners’ addresses.
  5. Read what it records. The Tokyo page says the summary holds the building’s outline and the records of building confirmation, interim inspection and completion inspection, so you can learn whether those were carried out. Compare the building owner, floors, structure, use and floor area with the sales material.
  6. If you need proof on paper, ask for a certificate of ledger entries (kisai jiko shomeisho), which certifies that a confirmation or inspection certificate was issued. Minato charges 300 yen a copy; Tokyo’s online application costs 400 yen each, up to five per application, plus 140 yen postage, by credit card only.

What it does not prove

  • It does not show who owns the land or the building today, or any mortgage. The building owner named may not be the current owner; ownership is in the Legal Affairs Bureau registry.
  • A confirmation shows that the plans were checked against the law, not that the building will be completed or pass its completion inspection.
  • It says nothing about the developer’s finances or how your deposit is protected.

Red flags

  • The developer cannot give a building confirmation number and date, or the authority holding the file has no summary matching them.
  • The floors, use or floor area in the summary differ from the brochure.
  • The building owner named is unrelated to the company selling to you and nobody can explain the link.
  • At handover there is no completion inspection certificate and no record of a completion inspection.

How it ties to Kolaboro’s six checks

  • Permit or licence number: the building confirmation number and date.
  • Construction progress: the interim and completion inspection records.
  • Developer track record: the building owner’s name, to look up in the company and licence registers.
  • Floor or site plans: Tokyo shows the location map and site plan only at its counter, not online. Deposit protection and no guaranteed returns: not in this record.
