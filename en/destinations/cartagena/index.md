# Cartagena in 4 Days: Walls, Islands and Street Food

> Cartagena travel guide by Happy Low Cost, 4-day itinerary. Original page: https://www.happylowcost.com/en/destinations/cartagena/
> Updated: 2026-11-21

Cartagena is the prettiest colonial city in the Caribbean, and it knows it. Inside the walls you get bougainvillea spilling off wooden balconies, ochre and mango-coloured houses, and plazas that fill up with music after dark. Step outside and it becomes a hot, loud, real Colombian port city with high-rise hotels, traffic and a market that will rearrange your senses. The heat is serious all year, prices in the old town are close to European levels, and you will be offered hats, cigars, boat trips and much more every few metres.

It suits couples, food lovers, photographers and anyone who wants Caribbean colour with history attached. It suits beach purists less: the city beaches are average, and the good sand is a boat ride away. **Four days is the right amount**: two for the walled city and Getsemaní, one for the islands, and one for the fortress, the market and a day trip or slow afternoon.

## What to see in Cartagena: the places worth your time

### The Walled City (Ciudad Amurallada)

The old town inside 11 km of stone walls is the reason you came, and walking it is free. Split it in two: the Centro, with the grand churches and mansions, and San Diego, quieter and more residential, with better small cafés. Give it at least a full morning plus an evening, because it looks like two different places by day and by night. Our tip: walk it between 7 and 10am, when the light is soft and the vendors are still setting up.

### Getsemaní

The former working-class neighbourhood just outside the walls is now the most enjoyable part of town. Calle de la Sierpe and Callejón Angosto are covered in murals, with umbrellas and flags strung overhead, and Plaza de la Trinidad becomes the city's living room after 7pm, with street food, breakdancers and locals on the church steps. Allow two hours by day and come back at night. It is free, and drinks here cost half what they do in the Centro.

### Castillo San Felipe de Barajas

The biggest Spanish fortress in the Americas sits on a hill 15 minutes' walk from Getsemaní, and its tunnels and ramparts are the best history lesson in town. Entry is about €8 (roughly COP 35,000) for foreigners; budget 1.5 to 2 hours. Go when it opens, because by 11am the stone radiates heat like an oven and there is almost no shade. Skip the pushy guides at the gate unless you want one; the audio guide or a printed map does the job.

### The walls at sunset

Climbing onto the ramparts for sunset is free and the best ritual in the city. The famous spot is Café del Mar on the Baluarte de Santo Domingo, where a cocktail costs €12-15 and tables fill early. What we would do: buy a beer from a street vendor and sit on the wall a few bastions along towards San Diego, with the same sunset and a fifth of the bill.

### Palacio de la Inquisición

This baroque palace on Plaza de Bolívar was the Inquisition's seat in the region, and the museum inside covers its trials alongside the city's colonial history. The torture instruments get the attention, but the city maps and models are more useful for understanding Cartagena. Allow an hour; entry is around €5. Combine it with a rest in Plaza de Bolívar, one of the few shady squares in the Centro.

### Iglesia de San Pedro Claver

The church and cloister honour a Jesuit who ministered to enslaved Africans arriving in the port, and it is the most moving stop in the old town. The cloister is lovely, and you can climb up to the choir for views over the domes. Entry is roughly €5-6 and an hour is enough. Read a little on the slave trade before you go; it gives the visit real weight.

### Plaza de Santo Domingo

Home to Botero's reclining bronze Gertrudis and the city's oldest church, this is the most photographed square in Cartagena. The terraces are overpriced and touristy, so admire it and move on. Ten minutes is plenty by day; it looks better at night when the church is lit.

### Torre del Reloj and Plaza de los Coches

The yellow clock tower is the main gate into the walled city and your natural starting point. Plaza de los Coches, right behind it, was once the slave market; now its arcade, El Portal de los Dulces, sells traditional sweets like cocadas and tamarind balls for about €0.50 each. Five minutes to look, longer if you snack.

### Museo del Oro Zenú

A small free museum on Plaza de Bolívar run by Colombia's central bank, with pre-Columbian gold work from the Zenú people. It takes 30-40 minutes and, crucially, it is air-conditioned. Perfect for the 2pm heat slump.

### Cerro de la Popa

The hill crowned by a 17th-century convent gives the only real panoramic view over the city, the bay and Bocagrande's towers. Go by taxi (about €6-8 each way, agree the price or ask the driver to wait), never on foot, as the neighbourhoods on the slopes are not safe for walking. Entry to the convent is about €4; allow an hour.

### Mercado de Bazurto

The city's main market is chaotic, muddy, loud and the opposite of the postcard Cartagena, which is why it is worth it. Fish, fruit, meat stalls and tiny kitchens frying fish and rice all morning. Go before 10am with only small cash, leave valuables at the hotel, and consider a guided food tour (around €40-60) if crowds stress you. One to two hours.

### Las Bóvedas

Twenty-three vaults built into the wall at the north end of San Diego, once dungeons and storage, now craft shops. Prices are high and quality varies, but the yellow arcade is photogenic and the walls above have a good sea view. Thirty minutes, free to wander.

## Things to do in Cartagena

Once you have seen the monuments, the best things to do in Cartagena are about food, music and getting on the water.

- **Dance salsa or champeta.** Café Havana in Getsemaní is the classic live salsa bar; expect a cover charge at weekends and a sweaty, happy crowd. Several schools in Getsemaní offer group classes for about €10-15.

- **Take a street food crawl in Getsemaní.** Arepas de huevo, butifarra sausages, fruit cups and fresh juices around Plaza de la Trinidad will feed you for under €5.

- **Spend a day on the Rosario Islands.** Coral reefs, clear water and island beach clubs, covered in the day trips section.

- **Ride a bike around the walls.** Early morning bike tours or rentals (about €15-25) cover the Centro, Getsemaní and the waterfront before the heat.

- **Take a cooking class.** Several outfits run classes that start with a market visit; expect €50-80 including lunch.

- **Watch the sunset from a rooftop.** Many boutique hotels in the Centro and Getsemaní have rooftop bars open to non-guests; a cocktail runs €8-12.

- **Go to a baseball or football game.** Baseball is big on the Caribbean coast, and a game is a cheap, very local night out. Check schedules locally.

## Cartagena for free (or almost)

The walled city itself is free, and so is most of what makes Cartagena special. Spend your money on boats and food, not on entry tickets.

- Walk the walls end to end, from Las Bóvedas to the Baluarte de Santo Domingo.

- Getsemaní's murals and Plaza de la Trinidad at night.

- Museo del Oro Zenú, free and cool inside.

- Plaza de Bolívar, Plaza de San Diego and Plaza Fernández de Madrid for people-watching.

- The Parque del Centenario between the old town and Getsemaní, where you may spot sloths and iguanas in the trees.

- A Transcaribe bus ride to Bocagrande for a cheap look at the modern city and a sea swim (about €0.70).

## What is overrated (and what to do instead)

- **Playa Blanca on Barú.** The sand is lovely, but on weekends and holidays it is packed, with relentless vendors and massage sellers. Instead, book a quieter beach club on the Rosario Islands or go on a weekday.

- **Bocagrande's beach.** Grey sand, murky water and hawkers. Fine for a dip, not worth planning around. Use the islands for beach time.

- **Café del Mar.** Lovely sunset, inflated bill. Sit on the wall nearby with a street beer.

- **Horse-drawn carriage rides.** Pricey (€40+), slow in traffic, and the horses work in brutal heat. Walk or rent a bike.

## When to visit Cartagena

The best time is **December to April**, the dry season, when breezes take the edge off the heat. It is also the most expensive and crowded, especially from mid-December to mid-January and during Easter week, when hotel prices can double. Temperatures sit around 30-32°C all year with high humidity, so there is no cool season.

May to August brings some showers but is quieter and cheaper, with a short drier spell around June and July. September to November is the rainiest stretch, with heavy afternoon downpours and the occasional flooded street, but also the lowest prices. Mid-November has the Independence festivities, lively but busy.

## Where to stay in Cartagena: best areas

- **Centro (inside the walls):** For first-timers and couples who want to step out the door into the postcard. Boutique hotels in colonial houses, often with plunge pools. About €90-250+ per night.

- **San Diego:** The quieter, more residential corner of the walled city. Best for couples who want charm without the night noise. About €80-200.

- **Getsemaní:** Best value and best atmosphere, with hostels, guesthouses and boutique stays. Ideal for budget travellers and anyone who likes being near nightlife. Dorms €12-20, private rooms €40-110.

- **Bocagrande:** High-rise hotels with sea views and pools, popular with Colombian families. Good for big chain hotels and beach access, less charm. About €60-150, a 10-minute taxi from the old town.

Our tip: wherever you stay, check for air conditioning and, if you are a light sleeper, ask for a room away from the street.

## What to eat in Cartagena

Cartagena's food is Caribbean: coconut, fried fish, plantain and lime. Eat the street food, it is often better than the restaurant version.

- **Arepa de huevo:** A corn arepa fried with an egg inside, sometimes with mince. The city's signature snack, about €1.

- **Pescado frito con arroz con coco y patacones:** Whole fried fish with coconut rice and plantain fritters. The classic lunch, €8-15.

- **Ceviche:** Cartagena-style comes with a sweeter, tomato-based sauce. Street carts sell shrimp versions in cups for €2-4.

- **Posta negra cartagenera:** Beef slow-cooked in a dark, sweet sauce of panela and spices.

- **Mote de queso:** A thick yam and cheese soup from the region, comforting despite the heat.

- **Limonada de coco:** A frozen coconut lemonade. Order it at every lunch.

- **Cocadas and fresh fruit:** Coconut sweets from the Portal de los Dulces, and fruit cups of mango, papaya and watermelon from street sellers.

A **menú del día** (soup, main, juice) in a local spot outside the touristy streets costs €4-6. A mid-range dinner in the old town runs €20-35 per person with a drink, and the well-known places such as La Cevichería or Celele cost more and need booking. Avoid the restaurants with menu touts on Plaza Santo Domingo; you pay for the view.

## Airport to city centre and getting around

Rafael Núñez International Airport is only about 3 km from the walled city, so arriving is easy. A taxi from the official rank takes 10-15 minutes and costs about €4-6 (roughly COP 15,000-25,000) to the Centro or Getsemaní, with a small surcharge at night. Check the fare posted at the taxi desk and confirm it before getting in. Uber and similar apps work but sit in a legal grey area, so pickups at the airport can be awkward.

In town, you walk. The Centro, San Diego and Getsemaní are all within 15-20 minutes of each other. Taxis have no meters, so agree the price first; short hops cost €2-4. The **Transcaribe** BRT bus connects the old town with Bocagrande and the outer city for about €0.70 a ride with a rechargeable card; there is no tourist pass worth buying.

## How much a trip to Cartagena costs

Cartagena is the most expensive city in Colombia, but it is still affordable if you eat local and stay in Getsemaní.

- **Shoestring:** €35-50 per day. Dorm bed (€12-20), street food and menú del día, free sights, one paid museum.

- **Mid-range:** €100-160 per day. Boutique or private room (€60-110), restaurant dinners, cocktails, San Felipe and a guided tour.

- **Extras:** Rosario Islands day trip €40-90; Volcán del Totumo tour €30-45; salsa class €10-15.

## Day trips from Cartagena

- **Rosario Islands:** Boats leave the Muelle de la Bodeguita; 45-60 minutes by speedboat. Tours cost €40-90 depending on lunch and beach club. Pick one that includes snorkelling and avoids just shuttling you between crowded stops.

- **Playa Blanca (Barú):** About an hour by road or boat. Go midweek and arrive early for the best of it.

- **Volcán del Totumo:** A small mud volcano about an hour north where you float in warm mud, then wash in a lagoon. Half day, €30-45 by tour; tip the helpers (they will ask).

- **San Basilio de Palenque:** The first free town of formerly enslaved Africans in the Americas, a UNESCO-recognised culture with its own language and music. About 1.5 hours by car; go with a guided tour for context.

- **Bocachica and the Fuerte de San Fernando:** A fishing village and colonial fort guarding the bay entrance, about 40 minutes by boat. Rougher round the edges, but very little tourism.

## Common mistakes and scams

- **Photos with palenqueras.** The women in colourful dresses with fruit bowls charge for photos (a few euros). Fair enough, but agree before you click.

- **Beach prices.** On Playa Blanca and the islands, always ask the price of fish, drinks, massages and loungers before accepting. Unasked-for "free" massages or oysters end with a big bill.

- **Taxis without a fixed price.** Agree the fare before getting in, every time.

- **Cheap island tours from street touts.** They often mean packed boats and hidden fees, including the port tax. Book through your hotel or a known agency and ask exactly what is included.

- **Drug offers.** You will be offered cocaine. Say no and walk on: it is illegal, and set-ups involving fake police are a known risk.

- **Card skimming and ATMs.** Use ATMs inside banks or malls, and pay attention at bars.

- **Underestimating the sun.** Heatstroke is the most common way to lose a day. Sightsee early and late, rest at midday.

## Cartagena FAQ

### How many days do you need in Cartagena?

Four days is ideal: two for the walled city and Getsemaní, one for the Rosario Islands and one for San Felipe, the market and La Popa. With three days, drop the market or the island trip.

### Is Cartagena safe for tourists?

The Centro, San Diego, Getsemaní and Bocagrande are generally safe, with plenty of police, though petty theft happens. Avoid walking on La Popa's slopes and outlying neighbourhoods at night, and use registered taxis.

### What is the best area to stay in Cartagena?

Getsemaní for value and atmosphere, the Centro or San Diego for colonial charm. Bocagrande suits those who want a pool and a modern hotel.

### Can you swim at the beaches in Cartagena?

Yes, but city beaches like Bocagrande are average. For clear water, take a boat to the Rosario Islands.

### Do I need cash in Cartagena?

Yes. Cards work in hotels and most restaurants, but street food, taxis, beach vendors and small shops are cash only in pesos.

### Is Cartagena expensive?

It is the priciest city in Colombia, with old-town restaurants close to European prices. Street food, set lunches and Getsemaní keep costs down.

### What is the best time of year to visit Cartagena?

December to April is dry and breezy but busy and expensive. May and June offer a good balance of decent weather and lower prices.

### Is Cartagena worth visiting?

For the traveller who wrote this guide, yes: Cartagena is the prettiest colonial city in the Caribbean, and it knows it. 4 days are enough for the essentials. Budget: Dorm bed (€12-20), street food and menú del día, free sights, one paid museum.

### What to see in Cartagena in one day?

If you only have one day, follow day 1 of this itinerary: The walled city. Morning: Enter through the Torre del Reloj at 7.30am, then walk to Plaza de Santo Domingo, Plaza de Bolívar and the Palacio de la Inquisición.

### What to see in Cartagena in two days?
