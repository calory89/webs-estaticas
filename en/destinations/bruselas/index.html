Brussels travel guide: what to see, eat and how long to stay

🔥 8 deals to Brussels🏨 Hotels in Brussels

Why go to Brussels

Brussels has a reputation for being the grey capital of Europe, a place with a nice square and a lot of men in lanyards. That undersells it. It is a compact, slightly scruffy, very funny city where Art Nouveau townhouses sit next to comic-strip murals, and where a Tuesday-night beer can turn into a four-hour conversation in a café with no music. It is also one of the easiest European capitals to reach on a cheap flight, and it works well as a base for Bruges, Ghent and Antwerp.

What to see in Brussels

Grand-Place

Yes, it is crowded. Go anyway, and go early, before 8am, when the guild houses are lit by low sun and the cobbles are almost empty. Come back after dark for the gilded facades. Look up at the Town Hall spire rather than at your phone.

Royal Galleries of Saint-Hubert

One of the oldest covered arcades in Europe, a few minutes from the Grand-Place. The chocolate shops are the draw, but the glass roof and the old bookshop are worth a slow walk even if you buy nothing.

Manneken Pis and Jeanneke Pis

The little fountain statue is smaller than you expect, and that is part of the joke. Jeanneke Pis hides in a gated alley near Impasse de la Fidélité and gets a fraction of the visitors.

Sablon and the Marolles

Sablon is the polished side: antique dealers, the Gothic Notre-Dame du Sablon church, and chocolatiers such as Wittamer. Walk downhill to the Marolles, the old working-class quarter, and to Place du Jeu de Balle, where the daily flea market is at its best in the morning. Stop at the Place Poelaert terrace beside the enormous Palais de Justice for a wide view over the city.

Magritte Museum and the Royal Museums of Fine Arts

The Magritte Museum is the best single-artist museum in town, and the old masters, including a good Bruegel collection, are in the same complex. Allow half a day if you like art, ninety minutes if you do not.

Belgian Comic Strip Center

Housed in a Victor Horta building, it covers Tintin, the Smurfs and Lucky Luke, and it is good even if you have never read a comic. Then hunt for the comic murals on the street, since the city has dozens.

Atomium

A giant iron crystal built for the 1958 World's Fair, on the Heysel plateau in the north of the city. It looks better from outside than the exhibition inside, but the view from the top sphere is decent. Mini-Europe is next door, mainly for families.

Cinquantenaire Park and the European Quarter

The triumphal arch and the large lawns are pleasant for a break. The Parlamentarium, free and clear about how the EU works, is a good rainy-day stop nearby.

Horta Museum, Saint-Gilles

Victor Horta's own house is one of the finest Art Nouveau interiors anywhere: stairwell, light, ironwork. It is small and tickets are limited, so book ahead.

Things to do

  • Drink properly. Try À la Mort Subite for old-school atmosphere, Moeder Lambic for a serious beer list, and Delirium Café for the sheer length of its menu (it is touristy, but fun once).
  • Visit Cantillon in Anderlecht, a working brewery making gueuze and lambic. It is sour, funky and nothing like a lager.
  • Browse the Sunday market at Gare du Midi, huge and loud, with food from everywhere.
  • Do a day trip. Bruges, Ghent, Antwerp and Leuven are all roughly 30 to 70 minutes by direct train.
  • Visit the Musical Instruments Museum (MIM), where the headphones play the instruments as you walk by. The roof café has a good view too.

How many days and a suggested route

Two days covers the centre. Three is better, because it lets you add a museum, a neighbourhood like Ixelles or Saint-Gilles, and a slow evening. If you want a day trip to Bruges or Ghent, make it four.

Day 1: Grand-Place at opening hours, the Galleries, Manneken Pis, lunch of frites, then Sablon and the Marolles, finishing with beers around Saint-Géry.

Day 2: Magritte Museum and Fine Arts in the morning, the Comic Strip Center after lunch, then walk through Dansaert and the canal area.

Day 3: Horta Museum, a long lunch in Saint-Gilles or Ixelles, then Flagey and the ponds, or Cinquantenaire and the EU Quarter if you prefer.

When to go

Brussels is grey and drizzly often, in every season, so pack a light waterproof whatever the month. Spring (April to June) is the sweet spot, with long evenings and terraces opening. Summer is mild, usually 20 to 24°C, with the occasional hot spell. In even-numbered years, the Flower Carpet covers the Grand-Place for a few days in August. September brings a beer weekend on the main square. Winter is cold and dark, around 2 to 6°C, but the Christmas market and lights are lovely, and hotel prices outside the holidays are low.

Where to stay

  • Centre and Grand-Place: the most convenient for a short visit, noisy at weekends. Good for first-timers, from busy hostels to design hotels.
  • Saint-Géry and Dansaert: lively, with bars, independent shops and the old canal. A good all-round pick.
  • Sablon and Marolles: quieter, elegant, close to museums. Prices lean higher.
  • Ixelles (Châtelain, Flagey): local feel, plenty of cafés and brunch spots. Better if you like neighbourhoods more than monuments.
  • Saint-Gilles: Art Nouveau streets, good restaurants and a more relaxed pace, near Gare du Midi.
  • European Quarter: practical and often cheaper at weekends, but empty in the evenings.

As a rough guide, a hostel bed costs about €30 to 50, a mid-range hotel €90 to 150 and a high-end hotel €200 or more. Prices rise during EU summits and big events.

What to eat and where

Frites come in a paper cone with a sauce, and mayonnaise is the classic. Maison Antoine on Place Jourdan has long queues for a reason, and Fritland near the Bourse is a convenient central alternative. Moules-frites is the typical sit-down meal, best in months with an "r" in them, from September to April. Carbonnade flamande, a beef stew cooked in beer, and waterzooi, a creamy chicken or fish stew, are the comfort dishes. Stoemp, mashed potato with vegetables and sausage, is simple and good.

For sweet things, the Brussels waffle is light and rectangular, dusted with sugar, while the Liège waffle is denser and caramelised. Avoid the tourist stands piled with cream and strawberries unless you really want that. For chocolate, Neuhaus, Leonidas, Mary and Pierre Marcolini are all reliable, and speculoos biscuits are an easy souvenir.

Getting from the airport and getting around

From Brussels Airport (Zaventem), trains run to Bruxelles-Central, Midi and Nord in about 15 to 20 minutes for roughly €10 to 12. It is the simplest option. If you fly low-cost into Charleroi, expect a shuttle bus to Bruxelles-Midi in roughly an hour, around €10 to 15 each way, so allow time for traffic.

The centre is walkable. For anything further, the STIB network of metro, tram and bus is straightforward, and you can tap in with a contactless card or buy a short-term ticket. Trams are often the better way to see the city. Trains from Central, Midi and Nord reach the other Belgian cities frequently.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: about €60 to 80 with a hostel, frites, supermarket lunches and one or two museums.
  • Mid-range: about €120 to 180 with a decent hotel, restaurant dinners and a few beers.
  • Comfortable: €250 or more for a good hotel, nice meals and day trips.

FAQs

Is Brussels worth visiting for a weekend?

Yes. Two days is enough for the centre, the main museums, the beer and the food, and flights are often cheap.

Is Brussels safe?

Mostly, yes. Pickpockets work around the Grand-Place and Gare du Midi, so keep your bag zipped, and take care in quiet streets near Midi and Nord late at night.

Do I need to speak French or Dutch?

No. French is the main language, Dutch is also official, and English is widely understood in hotels, restaurants and shops. A "bonjour" or "dag" is polite.

Is Brussels expensive?

Moderate for Western Europe. Beer and frites are cheap, restaurants and hotels sit in the middle, and the city is cheaper than Paris or Amsterdam.

Can I do Bruges as a day trip?

Yes. Direct trains take about an hour, and it is better on a weekday morning, when day-trippers are fewer.
