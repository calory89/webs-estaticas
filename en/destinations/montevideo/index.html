Montevideo is the calmest capital in South America, and that is both its charm and the reason some visitors leave underwhelmed. There is no single blockbuster sight. Instead you get a long seafront promenade, a faded Art Deco old town, excellent beef, wine nobody outside Uruguay talks about, and a population that seems to carry a thermos of hot water for mate everywhere it goes. It is safe by regional standards, easy to navigate and much less frantic than Buenos Aires across the river.

It suits travellers who like walking, eating and watching city life more than ticking off monuments. It also works well as a gentle first stop in South America or as a base for Colonia and Punta del Este. Three days is the right amount: two for the city itself and one for a day trip or a slow Sunday at the market and the Rambla. Be warned that Uruguay is the priciest country in the region, so budget closer to southern Europe than to Bolivia.

What to see in Montevideo: the places worth your time

Ciudad Vieja (Old Town)

The old town is where most of what to see in Montevideo is concentrated: a grid of colonial and early 20th-century buildings on a small peninsula, some beautifully restored, some crumbling. Walk the pedestrianised Peatonal Sarandí from the Puerta de la Ciudadela towards the port, detouring into Plaza Matriz (Plaza Constitución) with its cathedral and fountain. Give it half a day, and it costs nothing. Our tip: go on a weekday morning, when the streets are alive with office workers; on Sunday afternoons and late at night it empties out and feels a bit desolate.

Plaza Independencia and Palacio Salvo

This square is the hinge between the old town and the modern Centro, with a statue of independence hero José Artigas and his mausoleum underneath (free, worth five minutes). Towering over it is the Palacio Salvo, an eccentric 1920s skyscraper that was once the tallest building in South America. Guided tours run on certain days and include the views from the top (about €10, roughly UYU 450); check the schedule in advance as they sell out.

Teatro Solís

Uruguay's main theatre, opened in 1856, sits just off Plaza Independencia and is lovely inside: red velvet, gilded boxes, a proper old-world opera house. Guided tours take about 45 minutes and cost a few euros, with some free days. The better move is to buy a ticket for a performance; seats for the national ballet or orchestra can be surprisingly cheap (€10-30).

Mercado del Puerto

A 19th-century iron market hall near the port, now packed with parrillas grilling beef over wood embers. The smoke, the noise and the cooks working the grills make it a great spectacle. It is also the most touristy place to eat in the city, with prices to match (more on that below). Go at lunchtime, never at night, when it is mostly closed. Order a medio y medio (half sparkling, half white wine), the drink that was invented around here.

The Rambla

The Rambla is a seafront promenade of over 20 km that wraps around the city along the Río de la Plata, and it is the soul of Montevideo. Locals jog, fish, sunbathe and drink mate on it at all hours. The best stretch for visitors runs from Parque Rodó through Punta Carretas to Pocitos. Free, and best around sunset, when half the city comes out to watch.

Museo Andes 1972

A small, privately run museum in the old town about the 1972 plane crash in the Andes, when Uruguayan rugby players survived 72 days in the snow (the story behind the film "Society of the Snow"). It is personal, moving and full of objects and testimony from survivors and families. Allow an hour; entry is around €10 (UYU 450). Easily the most memorable museum in the city.

Museo Torres García

Joaquín Torres García is Uruguay's best-known modern artist, and this compact museum on Peatonal Sarandí shows his constructivist grids and symbols, plus the famous upside-down map of South America. An hour is plenty and entry costs a few euros. Worth it if you like 20th-century art; skip it if you don't.

Palacio Legislativo

Uruguay's parliament is a heavy marble palace at the end of Avenida de las Leyes, and it is far grander than such a small country seems to need. Guided tours of the interior (free or very cheap, usually on weekdays) show off the marble halls and stained glass. It is a 10-minute bus ride north of Centro; combine it with lunch nearby.

Feria de Tristán Narvaja

Montevideo's Sunday flea market sprawls along Tristán Narvaja street in Cordón from morning to early afternoon. Expect antiques, old books, vinyl, vegetables, mate gourds, pets and plenty of pure junk. Free to wander; go before noon and keep your phone in a front pocket. Grab a torta frita (fried dough) from a stall.

Estadio Centenario and the Museo del Fútbol

The stadium that hosted the first World Cup final in 1930 is a pilgrimage site for football fans, and the museum inside covers Uruguay's two World Cup wins and the Maracanazo. It takes about an hour and costs around €5. If there is a Peñarol or Nacional derby on, the atmosphere is intense, but buy tickets through official channels.

Punta Carretas lighthouse

A white lighthouse on a rocky point at the end of the Punta Carretas peninsula, with wide views over the water and the city skyline. You can sometimes climb it for a small fee. It is a good turnaround point on a Rambla walk; arrive for sunset.

Museo del Carnaval

Near the port and the Mercado del Puerto, this museum explains Uruguayan carnival, from the murga choirs to the candombe drums, with costumes and masks. It takes 45 minutes and costs around €5. Visit if you are not in town during carnival itself, which is the real thing.

Parque Rodó

A leafy park in the neighbourhood of the same name, with a lake, a small funfair, the national fine arts museum (Museo Nacional de Artes Visuales, free) and a beach across the road. On Sundays there is a large street market nearby. Good for a slow afternoon, about two hours.

Things to do in Montevideo

The best things to do in Montevideo happen outdoors and around food, more than inside museums.

  • Follow a candombe drum line. On many weekend evenings, groups of drummers (comparsas) parade through Barrio Sur and Palermo, and in the weeks before carnival rehearsals happen most nights. Ask your accommodation where to stand; it is free and electrifying.
  • Drink mate like a local. Buy a gourd, a bombilla (metal straw) and yerba at any supermarket for €10-15. Locals may offer you a sip on the Rambla; it is a gesture of friendship.
  • Taste Tannat. Uruguay's signature red grape is tannic and dark. Try it by the glass in a Ciudad Vieja wine bar or visit a winery in Canelones (see day trips).
  • Catch the sunset on the Rambla in Pocitos with a beer from a kiosk, then grab dinner in the area.
  • See a murga or carnival show at a tablado. From late January to early March, open-air stages around the city host satirical choirs and drumming. The Teatro de Verano is the main venue, with tickets from about €10.
  • Swim at Playa Pocitos or Playa Ramírez in summer. The water is the brownish estuary of the Río de la Plata, not ocean, but it is clean enough on most days; check the flag and local water-quality notices.
  • Watch a football match at the Centenario or the Gran Parque Central, the home of Nacional.
  • Have a drink at a historic bar. Café Brasilero in the old town has been open since 1877, and Bar Fun Fun near the Mercado Central is famous for tango and its uvita, a sweet wine drink.

Montevideo for free (or almost)

Montevideo is expensive to eat and sleep in, but many of its best experiences cost nothing.

  • Walk the Rambla, the city's best free attraction, ideally from Parque Rodó to Pocitos (about 1.5 hours).
  • Wander Ciudad Vieja and Peatonal Sarandí, including the cathedral and Plaza Matriz.
  • Visit the Museo Nacional de Artes Visuales in Parque Rodó and the Artigas mausoleum: both free.
  • Browse the Feria de Tristán Narvaja on Sunday morning.
  • Follow the candombe drums in Barrio Sur on weekend evenings.
  • Join a tip-based free walking tour of the old town, which usually starts from Plaza Independencia.
  • Check Teatro Solís for free tour days and cheap concerts.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Dinner at the Mercado del Puerto. The setting is great, but prices are inflated, waiters push big orders and quality varies. Do it once for lunch with a shared plate, or eat steak at a neighbourhood parrilla in Pocitos or Cordón for less.
  • Ciudad Vieja at night. Outside a few bars, it is dark and empty. Go out in Pocitos, Punta Carretas or Cordón instead.
  • The city beaches if you expect ocean. They are fine for an afternoon, but this is a river estuary. For real Atlantic waves, go to Punta del Este or further east.
  • Shopping malls. Punta Carretas Shopping sits in a former prison, which is a fun fact, but the shops are pricier than at home. The flea market is more interesting.

When to visit Montevideo

The best time to visit Montevideo is March to April or October to November, when it is mild (roughly 18-24°C), sunny and quieter. Summer (December to February) is hot, around 28°C, and great for the Rambla, but in January many locals leave for the coast and some restaurants close. Carnival runs for about 40 days from late January, the longest in the world, and is the strongest reason to come in summer.

Winter (June to August) is cold, damp and windy, around 10-15°C, with grey days, but flights and hotels are at their cheapest. Avoid Semana de Turismo (Easter week) if you want quiet: the whole country is on holiday and buses and coastal hotels fill up.

Where to stay in Montevideo: best areas

  • Ciudad Vieja: ideal for first-timers who want to walk to the main sights. Characterful hotels and hostels, but very quiet at night. Hostels €20-30, mid-range hotels €70-120.
  • Centro: around Avenida 18 de Julio, central and well connected by bus, with good-value hotels. A bit gritty after dark. €50-100.
  • Pocitos: our pick for most travellers. Residential, safe, on the beach, packed with restaurants and cafés. €70-150.
  • Punta Carretas: leafy and upmarket, near the lighthouse and the Rambla. Good for couples and families. €80-160.
  • Cordón and Parque Rodó: student-friendly, with bars and cheaper apartments, between Centro and the beach. €40-80.

What to eat in Montevideo

Montevideo is all about beef, and the dish you have to try is the chivito: a steak sandwich loaded with ham, cheese, egg, bacon, lettuce and tomato, usually served with fries. Order it al plato (on a plate, without the bread) if you are very hungry. A proper asado at a parrilla means grilled cuts like asado de tira (short ribs), vacío (flank) and entraña (skirt), plus morcilla (blood sausage) and provoleta (grilled cheese).

Look out for these as well:

  • Milanesa: breaded beef cutlet, often "a la napolitana" with ham, cheese and tomato sauce.
  • Pizza al tacho and fainá: thin cheese pizza with a chickpea flatbread on top. Order "pizza con fainá".
  • Empanadas: baked turnovers, around €2 each.
  • Torta frita: fried dough, a rainy-day classic.
  • Dulce de leche: in everything, especially alfajores (sandwich cookies).
  • Medio y medio and Tannat: the local drinks.

A menú del día lunch in a neighbourhood bar costs about €10-15 (UYU 450-700). A chivito with fries runs €12-18. Dinner at a decent parrilla with wine is €30-45 per person, more at the Mercado del Puerto. Restaurants often add a cubierto (bread and cover charge) of €2-4.

Airport to city centre and getting around

Carrasco International Airport is about 20 km east of the centre. Your options:

  • Intercity bus (COPSA or CUTCSA lines): stops outside arrivals and goes to the Tres Cruces terminal and Centro. Around €2-4, 45-60 minutes. Cheapest option.
  • Taxi or remise: book at the official desks inside arrivals. Expect about €35-50 to Pocitos or Ciudad Vieja, 25-40 minutes.
  • Uber: works in Montevideo and is usually cheaper than an airport taxi, around €20-30.

Within the city, buses are the main transport; there is no metro. Fares are around €1-1.50 (UYU 50-60) if you pay cash, and slightly less with an STM card, which locals use. Google Maps and the "Cómo Ir" app show routes. Taxis are metered and reasonable for short hops, and Uber is widely used. The Rambla and the old town are best on foot, and the city's public bike scheme or rental scooters work well along the seafront.

Day trips from Montevideo

  • Colonia del Sacramento: a UNESCO-listed colonial town with cobbled streets, a lighthouse and river sunsets. COT and Turil buses from Tres Cruces take about 2.5-3 hours each way, so leave early or stay one night.
  • Punta del Este: Uruguay's glitzy beach resort, home of the giant hand sculpture "La Mano" on Playa Brava. Around 2 hours by bus. Lively in January, ghostly in winter.
  • Canelones wineries: Bodega Bouza, Juanicó and other Tannat producers lie 30-45 minutes from the city. Book tastings ahead; a car or tour is easiest.
  • Piriápolis: an old-fashioned seaside town with a grand hotel and a chairlift up Cerro San Antonio. About 1.5 hours by bus.
  • Atlántida: an hour east along the coast, with calm beaches and Eladio Dieste's brick church at nearby Estación Atlántida, a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Common mistakes and scams

Montevideo is safe compared with most Latin American capitals, but petty theft does happen. Keep phones out of sight in Ciudad Vieja and Centro, especially after dark, at the Tristán Narvaja market and on crowded buses. Phone snatching from people on the pavement is the most common crime.

  • Underestimating costs: Uruguay is expensive. Do not budget as if it were Argentina or Peru.
  • Not checking the bill: cover charges are normal, but check for extra dishes or drinks, especially in tourist restaurants near the port.
  • Paying in cash when a card is better: tourists paying with a foreign card have often been exempt from VAT in restaurants and hotels. Check whether the rule still applies when you travel.
  • Taking unofficial taxis at the airport: use the official desks or an app.
  • Arriving on Sunday expecting a busy old town: many shops close, so plan the market and the Rambla for Sunday instead.
  • Eating dinner too early: restaurants fill from 9pm. At 7pm you will be alone.

Montevideo FAQ

How many days do you need in Montevideo?

Three days is ideal: two for the old town, the Rambla and the food, and one for Colonia or a slow Sunday. Two days is enough if you are only passing through.

Is Montevideo safe for tourists?

Yes, it is one of the safest capitals in South America. Use normal city precautions, keep your phone out of sight and take a taxi at night in quiet areas of Ciudad Vieja and Centro.

Is Montevideo worth visiting?

It is worth it if you enjoy relaxed cities, seafront walks, beef and wine. If you want big-ticket sights and nightlife, Buenos Aires delivers more, so combine the two.

What is Montevideo famous for?

The Rambla, the chivito, the longest carnival in the world, candombe drumming, mate and its football history, including the first World Cup in 1930.

Can you use US dollars or euros in Montevideo?

US dollars are accepted in some hotels and shops, but you will get better value paying in Uruguayan pesos or by card. Euros are rarely accepted outside exchange offices.

How do you get from Buenos Aires to Montevideo?

The ferry is the classic route: Buquebus runs direct ferries in about 2-3 hours, or cheaper ferry-plus-bus combinations via Colonia. Short flights also connect the two cities.

Is the water safe to drink in Montevideo?

Tap water is generally considered safe, though many locals prefer bottled for the taste. Follow local advice if there are notices about supply quality.

What to see in Montevideo in one day?

If you only have one day, follow day 1 of this itinerary: Ciudad Vieja and Centro. Morning: start at Plaza Independencia, look up at the Palacio Salvo, walk through the Puerta de la Ciudadela and down Peatonal Sarandí.
