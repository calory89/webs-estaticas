Kyoto travel guide: temples, food and how many days to stay

🏨 Hotels in Kyoto

Why go to Kyoto

Kyoto was Japan's capital for over a thousand years, and it shows. Wooden machiya townhouses still line some lanes, there are roughly 1,600 temples, and in the evening you can see geiko hurrying to appointments in Gion. It's also a real city of around 1.4 million people, with chain stores, office workers and rush-hour trains. Accept that and you'll enjoy it more.

The honest downside is crowds. The famous spots fill up from mid-morning, especially in spring and autumn. The fix is simple: get up early. Most travellers don't, so you get the best light and the quiet that Kyoto is known for.

What to see in Kyoto

  • Fushimi Inari Taisha: thousands of vermilion torii gates climbing a wooded hill, free and open around the clock. Most people turn back after the first stretch. If you keep walking up toward the summit (about two to three hours round trip), the crowd thins out fast. It's five minutes by JR train from Kyoto Station.
  • Kiyomizu-dera: a huge wooden terrace propped on stilts over a hillside, with views across the city. Go when it opens. Then walk down the stepped lanes of Ninenzaka and Sannenzaka, lined with old shopfronts.
  • Gion and Hanamikoji: the old geisha quarter, best at dusk. Don't chase geiko for photos. Residents have had enough of it, and some private alleys are now off-limits to visitors.
  • Arashiyama: the bamboo grove is lovely but tiny and packed by 9am. Come at opening time, then visit Tenryu-ji's garden, cross Togetsukyo bridge and consider the hike up to the Iwatayama monkey park for the view.
  • Kinkaku-ji: the Golden Pavilion, covered in gold leaf and doubled in its pond. It's a short visit, 30 to 40 minutes, but it's worth it. Pair it with the dry rock garden at nearby Ryoan-ji.
  • Nijo Castle: the shogun's Kyoto residence. The "nightingale floors" chirp underfoot by design, as an alarm against intruders. A good choice for a rainy morning.
  • Ginkaku-ji and the Philosopher's Path: a moss-heavy temple garden, then a gentle canal-side walk south toward Nanzen-ji. Lovely in any season.
  • Nanzen-ji: a big Zen temple with a brick aqueduct hidden among the trees. It's calmer than most places on this list.
  • Sanjusangen-do: a very long hall holding 1,001 statues of the thousand-armed Kannon. It's strangely moving and often overlooked.
  • Kyoto Imperial Palace Park: wide gravel paths and big trees in the middle of town. A good place to slow down.

Things to do

Browse Nishiki Market, a narrow covered street of pickles, tofu doughnuts, skewers and sweets. It gets crowded, so go before lunch and eat as you walk, the way locals do. Rent a bicycle if your legs are up to it. The centre is flat and grid-like, and cycling along the Kamo River is a pleasure.

Book a tea ceremony or a calligraphy session if you want something hands-on. Spend a night in a ryokan with a multi-course dinner at least once. And walk the Kamo River in the evening, when couples and students sit along the banks.

For a day trip, Nara (deer, the giant Buddha at Todai-ji) is about 45 minutes by train. Uji, known for matcha, is even closer. Osaka makes a great food-focused evening.

How many days and a suggested route

Three full days is the sweet spot. Two feels rushed and four lets you add Nara. Here's a route that keeps each day geographically sensible.

  • Day 1, east: Fushimi Inari at opening, then Kiyomizu-dera, Ninenzaka, Sannenzaka and Gion at dusk.
  • Day 2, west and north: Arashiyama first thing, then Ryoan-ji and Kinkaku-ji, and finish with the Nishiki area and Pontocho for dinner.
  • Day 3, centre and northeast: Nijo Castle, the Imperial Palace park, then Ginkaku-ji, the Philosopher's Path and Nanzen-ji.
  • Day 4, optional: Nara, or Uji for matcha.

When to go

Late March to early April brings cherry blossom, and mid-November to early December brings red maples. Both are stunning and both are crowded and expensive, so book hotels months ahead. April's end and May are pleasant and calmer. June is rainy season. July and August are hot and sticky, with temperatures often above 35°C, though the Gion Matsuri parades in mid-July are a highlight. September can still be hot and typhoons are possible.

Winter is my favourite time to avoid the crowds. It's cold, sometimes with a dusting of snow on the temples, and you'll often have gardens nearly to yourself.

Where to stay

Around Kyoto Station is the practical choice. You have trains, buses, the shinkansen and plenty of business hotels. It lacks charm but saves time. Kawaramachi and Shijo is the best all-rounder: central, packed with restaurants and walkable to Gion and Nishiki. Gion and Higashiyama is atmospheric and expensive, and it's the spot for a splurge ryokan. Arashiyama suits a quiet night once the day-trippers leave.

On a tight budget, hostels and capsule-style places are plentiful, usually from about 3,000 to 5,000 yen for a dorm bed. Business hotels typically cost 8,000 to 15,000 yen a night for a double, and more in peak season. Ryokan with dinner can run well above 25,000 yen per person.

What to eat and where

Kyoto cuisine is delicate and seasonal. Kaiseki is the multi-course high end, and lunch versions are far cheaper than dinner. Obanzai is Kyoto home cooking, built on small vegetable dishes, and is easy to find at casual set-meal restaurants. Yudofu, simmered tofu, is a classic near Nanzen-ji. Also look for yuba (tofu skin), tsukemono (pickles) and nishin soba, buckwheat noodles topped with sweet dried herring.

For a quick meal, the Ramen Koji floor in the Kyoto Station building has several ramen shops under one roof. At night, narrow Pontocho alley and Kiyamachi, along a small canal, are lined with izakaya and bars. Many have English menus, but some small places only take regulars, so don't be shy about moving on. Finish with matcha ice cream or a warabi-mochi in Higashiyama.

Getting from the airport and around

Most international flights land at Kansai International (KIX) near Osaka. The Haruka limited express runs direct to Kyoto Station in roughly 75 to 80 minutes. A one-way ticket costs around 3,000 to 3,600 yen. Airport limousine buses also run, and they're cheaper but slower. From Tokyo, the Nozomi shinkansen takes about two hours and fifteen minutes.

In the city, buses reach most temples but crowd heavily at peak times. The subway has just two lines, so it's limited. JR, Keihan and Hankyu trains fill the gaps. A rechargeable IC card (ICOCA or Suica) works on all of them and on most buses. Taxis are fair value for groups of three or four. Many places are close enough to walk between, and the walk is often the best part.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: about 7,000 to 10,000 yen, with a hostel, simple meals and a few temple entries.
  • Mid-range: about 15,000 to 25,000 yen, with a business hotel, restaurant meals and some paid sights.
  • Comfortable: 35,000 yen and up, with a good ryokan or hotel and kaiseki dinners.

Most temples charge a few hundred yen. Fushimi Inari is free. These figures are approximate and rise in the busy seasons.

FAQs

How many days do I need in Kyoto?

Three full days covers the highlights without rushing. Add a fourth for Nara or Uji.

Is Kyoto expensive?

Not by Western standards, though hotels have become costly in peak seasons. Food and transport are good value.

Do I need to speak Japanese?

No. Signs at stations and major sights are in English, and a translation app helps in smaller restaurants.

Can I see geisha in Gion?

You might spot geiko or maiko in the evening around Hanamikoji. Keep your distance, don't block them and don't enter private alleys marked as off-limits.

What's the best month to visit?

Early April and mid-to-late November have the best scenery but also the biggest crowds. Late April, May and October are good compromises.
