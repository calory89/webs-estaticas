Palermo travel guide: what to see, eat and how long to stay

🔥 1 deal to Palermo🏨 Hotels in Palermo

Why go to Palermo

Palermo is loud, a bit chaotic and layered with history. Arab domes sit next to Norman mosaics, Baroque churches and Liberty-style theatres, and the fruit sellers shout over all of it. Some streets are still scarred by wartime bombing, others have been polished up in the last decade. That contrast is what makes the city, and it is cheap compared with most European capitals.

It also has some of the best street food in Italy, a real beach twenty minutes away, and flights that regularly drop to bargain prices from across Europe. Go with an appetite and low expectations of tidiness.

What to see in Palermo

  • Cappella Palatina and Palazzo dei Normanni: the royal chapel is covered in gold mosaics from the 12th century. Book ahead in high season and go first thing, when the light hits the walls best.
  • Palermo Cathedral: a mash-up of styles, from Norman to Neoclassical. Pay extra for the roof; the view over the rooftops toward the mountains is worth it.
  • La Martorana and San Cataldo: two small churches on the same square, one with Byzantine mosaics, the other with three plain red domes. You can see both in under an hour.
  • Quattro Canti and Piazza Pretoria: the crossroads that divides the old centre into four quarters, and next to it a huge fountain locals call the Fountain of Shame because of its nude statues.
  • Teatro Massimo: one of the largest opera houses in Europe, and the steps where the final scene of The Godfather Part III was filmed. The guided visit is short and decent.
  • Catacombe dei Cappuccini: thousands of mummified bodies in corridors, sorted by profession and gender. Macabre, strangely moving, and not for everyone. Photography is not allowed.
  • Monreale Cathedral: a short bus ride out of town, with the biggest mosaic cycle in Sicily. Skip it and you will regret it.
  • Oratorio del Rosario di San Domenico: Giacomo Serpotta's white stucco figures, tucked behind the Vucciria area. Quiet and rarely crowded.
  • Palazzo della Zisa: a 12th-century Arab-Norman summer palace with cool, calm rooms. A good break in July.

Things to do

Start with the markets. Ballarò is the oldest and the most lived-in, with fish, spices, cheese and snack stalls. Il Capo is a bit less touristy and best on weekday mornings. The Vucciria was once the great market and is now mostly a nightlife spot, with drinks outside on warm evenings.

Take the bus to Mondello for a swim. The beach is a wide sandy bay beneath Monte Pellegrino, and in summer it fills up on weekends. Order a granita with a brioche, which is the local breakfast and not a dessert.

Walk along the Foro Italico seafront at sunset when the whole city seems to be out strolling. If you have the energy, hike up Monte Pellegrino to the Santuario di Santa Rosalia, the patron saint of the city.

For one evening, find a seat for an opera or a concert at Teatro Massimo or the Politeama. Tickets can be cheaper than you would think.

How many days and a suggested route

Three full days is the sweet spot. Two works if you stick to the centre. Four lets you add Cefalù or a quiet day at the beach.

  • Day 1: Palazzo dei Normanni and Cappella Palatina in the morning, then the cathedral, Quattro Canti, La Martorana and San Cataldo. Lunch at Ballarò, then the Kalsa neighbourhood in the late afternoon.
  • Day 2: Monreale in the morning, back to town for the Capo market, Teatro Massimo and the Oratorio del Rosario. Evening drinks in the Vucciria area.
  • Day 3: the Catacombe, then Mondello for the afternoon, or Cefalù by train for a day trip (about 45 minutes to an hour from Palermo Centrale).

When to go

April to early June and September to October are the best months, with warm days, sea that is swimmable by late May and fewer queues. Summer is very hot, often over 30°C, and August is brutal in the stone streets; plan indoor visits for the afternoon. A hot African wind called the sirocco can make it worse.

Winter is mild, around 10 to 15°C, but wet. It is a great time for cheap flights and empty churches. Easter has intense processions, and the Festino di Santa Rosalia on 14 and 15 July is the big city party, with fireworks and a very full centre.

Where to stay

Kalsa and the Quattro Canti area is the most convenient base, close to the main sights and the seafront. Expect some noise at night. Albergheria and Ballarò are rougher around the edges but very atmospheric, and the best for food. Politeama and Libertà are calmer, with wide avenues, shops and modern hotels, and a ten-minute walk from the old town. Mondello is for beach stays in summer, though you will be commuting for sights.

  • Budget: hostels and simple rooms from roughly 25 to 50 euros a night.
  • Mid-range: boutique B&Bs and three-star hotels from roughly 70 to 130 euros.
  • Splurge: historic palazzo hotels and sea-view rooms from roughly 150 euros up.

What to eat and where

Palermo eats on the street, and you should too. Order panelle (chickpea fritters) and crocchè (potato croquettes) in a sandwich. Try arancine, the fried rice balls, with ragù or butter and ham. Sfincione, a thick spongy pizza with onions and breadcrumbs, is sold from stalls and bakeries. If you are brave, go for pani ca meusa, a spleen sandwich that sells all over the old town.

For sit-down meals look for pasta con le sarde (sardines, wild fennel, raisins and pine nuts), caponata and fresh fish. Antica Focacceria San Francesco, near the church of the same name, is a classic and a safe first meal. Friggitoria Chiluzzo, in the Kalsa by the old city gate, is good for a cheap fried snack with a beer.

For sweets, Pasticceria Cappello is well known for its pastries and cakes. Try a cannolo filled to order, a slice of cassata and the marzipan fruit known as frutta martorana.

From the airport and getting around

Falcone-Borsellino airport (PMO) is about 35 km west of the centre. The Prestia e Comandè bus runs to the Politeama and the central station in about 50 minutes for a few euros. The Trinacria Express train reaches Palermo Centrale in about the same time at a similar price. A taxi costs a fixed fare of roughly 50 euros.

The historic centre is walkable, and the best way to see it. Traffic is chaotic, so take care crossing the road. City buses cover the rest, including routes to Monreale (from near Palazzo dei Normanni) and to Mondello. Taxis are cheap for short hops. A car is unnecessary unless you plan to tour the island, and parking in the centre is a headache.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: around 50 to 70 euros, with a hostel, street food and a few paid sights.
  • Mid-range: around 100 to 150 euros, with a comfortable room, restaurant dinners and entry tickets.
  • Comfortable: 200 euros and up, with a nice hotel, long meals and the odd taxi or private tour.

FAQs

Is Palermo safe for tourists?

Generally yes. Petty theft and bag snatching happen in crowded areas, so keep valuables close. Roads are the bigger risk than people.

Is three days enough?

Yes, for the main sights, Monreale and a swim at Mondello. Add a fourth day for Cefalù or slower meals.

Can I swim in Palermo?

Yes, but not in the city harbour. Mondello is the main beach and is easy to reach by bus. Water is warmest from June to September.

Do I need to speak Italian?

No. Basic English works in hotels and tourist spots, but a few words in Italian go down well in markets and small trattorias.

Is Palermo good in winter?

It is, if you accept some rain. The weather is mild, the sights are quiet, hotels are cheaper and the food does not change.
