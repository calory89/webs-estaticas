Things to Do in Jerusalem: An Honest 3-Day City Guide

Jerusalem is intense, and you should know that before you book. Inside one square kilometre of Old City you have the Western Wall, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and the Dome of the Rock, plus souvenir sellers, armed police, pilgrims singing in six languages and the smell of cardamom coffee. Outside the walls it is a much more ordinary modern city, with a great food market, decent museums and a nightlife that shuts down almost completely on Friday evening for Shabbat.

It suits travellers interested in history, religion, food and politics, and anyone happy to walk a lot on slippery limestone. It does not suit people looking for beaches or a relaxed party break (that is Tel Aviv, 45 minutes away). Three days is the right amount: two for the Old City and its surroundings, one for the museums and the modern city. Add a fourth if you want the Dead Sea or Bethlehem. Check your government's travel advice before booking, because the security situation can change quickly and it affects which sites are open.

What to see in Jerusalem: the places worth your time

The Old City and its four quarters

The walled Old City is the reason you came, and it is free to wander. It is split into the Christian, Muslim, Jewish and Armenian quarters, and each one feels different within a few steps. Enter through Jaffa Gate and leave through Damascus Gate to see the contrast. Plan at least a full day. Our tip: go first thing in the morning, before the tour groups, when shopkeepers are still pulling up their shutters.

Western Wall (Kotel)

The holiest accessible place in Judaism is a section of retaining wall from the Second Temple, and the plaza in front of it is open around the clock and free. Men and women pray in separate sections, and you will need to pass a security check. Twenty to thirty minutes is enough unless you are praying. Come on Friday around sunset to see Shabbat begin, with singing and dancing groups. Do not take photos on Shabbat itself.

Western Wall Tunnels

This guided tour runs underground along the hidden length of the wall, past huge Herodian stones and old cisterns. It takes about 75 minutes and costs around €10-12 (₪40-50). It sells out days ahead in high season, so book online. It is one of the few paid attractions in the Old City we would call essential.

Church of the Holy Sepulchre

Christians believe this is where Jesus was crucified, buried and resurrected. The building is a dark, chaotic, candle-smoked maze shared by six denominations who famously do not always get along. Entry is free. The queue for the tomb (the Aedicule) can take over an hour by mid-morning, so arrive early or skip the tomb and just explore Golgotha upstairs and the Ethiopian monastery on the roof. Allow an hour, and cover your shoulders and knees.

Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif

The plateau holding the Dome of the Rock and Al-Aqsa Mosque is one of the most beautiful spaces in the city. Non-Muslims can only enter through the wooden Mughrabi Bridge beside the Western Wall plaza, during short and frequently changing visiting windows, and cannot go inside the buildings. Entry is free, but queues start early and the site closes without warning during tension or holidays. Dress modestly and do not bring religious items.

Tower of David Museum

The citadel by Jaffa Gate tells the city's 3,000-year history in a clear, well-designed way, which helps everything else make sense. It costs about €12-14 (₪50-55) and takes two hours. The view from the towers is one of the best over the Old City rooftops. The night sound-and-light show on the walls is fun if you have the energy.

Ramparts Walk

You can walk on top of large parts of the Old City walls, looking down into courtyards and schoolyards you would never see from the street. Tickets are cheap, around €5 (₪20-25), and the southern section from Jaffa Gate to Dung Gate is the best. Allow 1-2 hours. It is very exposed, so avoid midday in summer.

Mount of Olives

This is the postcard view: the golden Dome of the Rock across the valley, with the vast Jewish cemetery sloping down in front of you. Take a taxi to the top (about €10) and walk down past the Church of the Pater Noster, Dominus Flevit and the Garden of Gethsemane. Go in the morning for the best light behind you. Allow two hours, and avoid walking it alone after dark.

City of David and Hezekiah's Tunnel

Just south of the Old City walls, this archaeological site sits on the oldest part of Jerusalem. The highlight is wading through Hezekiah's Tunnel, a 2,700-year-old water channel with knee-deep water, for about 30 minutes. Bring a torch, water shoes and clothes you do not mind soaking. Tickets are around €8-10 (₪30-35), and you should know the site is politically contested because it sits in the Palestinian neighbourhood of Silwan.

Yad Vashem

Israel's Holocaust memorial and museum is free, extremely well curated and emotionally heavy. Most people need 3 hours. Take the light rail to Mount Herzl and walk 10 minutes. Children under 10 are not allowed into the main museum, and you should not plan anything light for the rest of that afternoon.

Israel Museum

This is the country's largest museum, and the Shrine of the Book housing the Dead Sea Scrolls is the reason most people go. The outdoor model of Second Temple Jerusalem is surprisingly useful after a day in the Old City. Tickets are around €15-18 (₪60-70). Give it 3 hours, and pick two or three wings rather than trying to see everything.

Mahane Yehuda Market

The city's main market (the "shuk") is between Jaffa Road and Agrippas Street. By day it sells halva, spices, olives and challah. In the evening, especially Thursday, the shutters come down and it turns into a crowded strip of bars and street food. It is free to wander. Go Friday morning for the pre-Shabbat buying frenzy, and Thursday night for drinks.

Austrian Hospice rooftop

This 19th-century pilgrim guesthouse sits on the Via Dolorosa. For a small fee (a few euros), you can climb to its roof for one of the best views over the Muslim Quarter and the Dome of the Rock. Afterwards, have apple strudel and Viennese coffee in the garden café. It is a calm 45 minutes in the middle of the most hectic part of town.

Things to do in Jerusalem

Once you have seen the big sights, these are the things that make the city feel less like a checklist.

  • Thursday night at the shuk: grab a stool at one of the bars inside Mahane Yehuda and stay late. This is the liveliest night of the week.
  • Experience Shabbat: from Friday afternoon to Saturday night, West Jerusalem goes almost silent. Walk the empty streets of Rehavia, and if you can, book a Shabbat dinner with a local family through one of the hosting programmes.
  • Take a dual-narrative tour: some companies run tours led by one Israeli and one Palestinian guide. It is the fastest way to understand what you are looking at.
  • Wander Nachlaot: a maze of tiny alleys and synagogues next to the market, good for an aimless hour.
  • Visit Ein Karem: this former Arab village on the city's edge has churches, stone houses and cafés. It feels like a different country.
  • Go to the First Station: the old Ottoman train station is now a food and events space at the start of the Train Track Park, a long walking and cycling path.
  • Attend a service: listen to Armenian chant in St James Cathedral (check the times) or a Friday evening service at a synagogue.

Jerusalem for free (or almost)

A surprising amount of the best of Jerusalem costs nothing. The Old City streets, the Western Wall, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, the Garden of Gethsemane, the Mount of Olives viewpoints and Yad Vashem are all free.

  • The Garden Tomb near Damascus Gate, a quieter alternative burial site of Jesus, runs on donations.
  • The Haas Promenade (Armon Hanatziv) gives a sweeping sunset view over the whole city, with no ticket needed.
  • Mamilla Avenue, the outdoor mall linking Jaffa Gate to the new city, is pleasant for an evening stroll.
  • Many museums, including the Israel Museum, have discounted or free slots on certain days, so check before you go.
  • Free walking tours leave from Jaffa Gate daily (tip-based, about €10 is fair).

Jerusalem itinerary: 3 days

Day 1: the Old City

Morning: enter at Jaffa Gate and walk straight to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre before the crowds arrive. Continue through the Muslim Quarter to the Austrian Hospice rooftop, then have hummus at Abu Shukri.

Afternoon: head through the Jewish Quarter and the Cardo to the Western Wall, then do your pre-booked Western Wall Tunnels tour. Finish on the southern Ramparts walk if your legs allow.

Evening: walk up Mamilla to the new city and have dinner around Jaffa Road or Mahane Yehuda.

Day 2: Temple Mount, City of David, Mount of Olives

Morning: queue early at the Mughrabi Bridge for Temple Mount. Afterwards, walk out through Dung Gate to the City of David and get wet in Hezekiah's Tunnel.

Afternoon: take a taxi to the top of the Mount of Olives (the afternoon light is softer here than you would think), then walk down to Gethsemane and back in via Lions' Gate.

Evening: catch the Tower of David night show, or get knafeh near Damascus Gate.

Day 3: museums and modern Jerusalem

Morning: take the light rail to Mount Herzl for Yad Vashem.

Afternoon: take a short taxi to the Israel Museum, then walk or taxi to the German Colony and Emek Refaim for coffee, ending at the First Station.

Evening: go to Mahane Yehuda, ideally on a Thursday.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Walking the Via Dolorosa as a "route": the Stations of the Cross are small plaques squeezed between souvenir shops, and the walk is hard to follow. Join the Franciscan procession on Friday afternoons if it matters to you, otherwise simply pass through.
  • Queuing for the tomb in the Holy Sepulchre at noon: an hour in line for a few seconds inside is not worth it. Come at opening time or skip it.
  • Machneyuda restaurant for a budget traveller: it is famous and fun, but expensive. You get much of the same energy for a fraction of the price at the market stalls and bars outside.
  • Buying souvenirs in the first shops inside Jaffa Gate: they charge the highest prices in the city. Armenian ceramics are better bought from the workshops in the Armenian Quarter.

When to visit Jerusalem

The best months are April-May and October-November: warm days of around 20-27°C and cool evenings. Summer is hot (often above 30°C) but dry, and the city's altitude makes nights pleasant. Winter is chilly and rainy, and it occasionally snows. Hotels are cheapest from December to February, excluding Christmas.

Watch the calendar. Passover, Easter, Sukkot and Christmas bring big crowds and high hotel prices. Ramadan changes access to Temple Mount and the rhythm of the Muslim Quarter. Shabbat every week shuts most of West Jerusalem's shops and public transport, so plan Friday afternoon and Saturday around the Old City and East Jerusalem, which stay open.

Where to stay in Jerusalem: best areas

  • Old City (Christian or Armenian Quarter): best for atmosphere and early starts. Many options are pilgrim guesthouses and hostels. Expect €30-50 for a dorm or simple room, and €100-180 for a guesthouse double. Hotels are quiet at night, and there are lots of stairs.
  • City Centre (Jaffa Road, Ben Yehuda): best for first-timers who want restaurants, the light rail and a 15-minute walk to Jaffa Gate. Doubles are €130-250.
  • Mahane Yehuda and Nachlaot: best for food lovers and nightlife, with good boutique hotels and apartments for €120-220. It can be noisy on Thursday nights.
  • German Colony and Emek Refaim: leafy and calm, with good cafés. It suits couples and families. Expect €140-250, and it is a 25-minute walk to the Old City.
  • East Jerusalem (near Damascus Gate): the cheapest hotels, often €70-130, and open on Shabbat. It is closest to the Muslim Quarter.

What to eat in Jerusalem

Start with hummus, and treat it as a full meal. The old-school places in the Muslim Quarter (Abu Shukri on Al-Wad Street, Lina near the Holy Sepulchre) serve it warm with ful, chickpeas and a plate of raw onion, usually only until early afternoon. A plate costs about €5-8 (₪20-30).

  • Ka'ak: sesame bread rings sold from carts near Damascus Gate, eaten with za'atar. They cost a euro or two.
  • Me'orav Yerushalmi (Jerusalem mixed grill): chicken hearts, livers and spleen fried with onion and spices, stuffed in pita. The classic spots are on Agrippas Street by the market.
  • Sabich: pita with fried aubergine, hard-boiled egg, tahini and amba (mango pickle).
  • Knafeh: warm cheese pastry soaked in syrup. Order it at Jafar Sweets inside Damascus Gate.
  • Rugelach: try the chocolate ones from the market bakeries, especially warm on a Friday morning.
  • Shakshuka: for breakfast, eggs poached in tomato and pepper sauce.

Israel is not cheap. A street-food lunch is about €8-12 (₪30-50). A sit-down dinner with a drink in a mid-range restaurant costs €30-45 per person, and a beer in a bar is around €8 (₪30-35). Many kosher restaurants close on Friday afternoon and do not serve meat and dairy together, so there is no cheeseburger and no cappuccino after a steak.

Airport to city centre and getting around

Ben Gurion Airport (TLV) is about 50 km away. The fastest option is the train from the airport to Jerusalem Yitzhak Navon station: about 25 minutes and roughly €5-6 (₪20). From there the light rail is right outside. The train does not run on Shabbat.

  • Shared taxi (sherut): yellow minibuses go from outside arrivals to your hotel door for around €18-20 per person. They are slower (an hour or more) but run on Shabbat.
  • Taxi: about €75-100 (₪300-400), more at night and on Shabbat. Insist on the meter or agree the price first.
  • Light rail: the Red Line runs along Jaffa Road from the central bus station past Mahane Yehuda, City Hall and Damascus Gate, out to Mount Herzl.
  • Payment: buy a Rav-Kav card at the station, or pay with contactless or a transit app. A single ride costs about €1.50 (₪6), with free transfers for a period afterwards.
  • Arab buses: blue-and-white buses from near Damascus Gate serve East Jerusalem and Bethlehem, and they run on Shabbat.

The Old City is walk-only. Most of central Jerusalem is walkable too, but it is hilly.

How much a trip to Jerusalem costs

  • Shoestring: €60-80 per day (₪240-320). Hostel dorm, hummus and falafel lunches, market snacks for dinner, mostly free sights and one paid ticket a day, walking and light rail.
  • Mid-range: €170-250 per day (₪680-1,000). Double room in a centre hotel, two sit-down meals, the Tunnels and museums, occasional taxis and a few drinks.
  • Extras: a Dead Sea or Masada tour is €80-150, and a private guide for the Old City is €200 or more for half a day.

Day trips from Jerusalem

  • Dead Sea and Masada: Egged buses leave from the central bus station to Ein Bokek and Masada in about 1.5-2 hours. Climb or take the cable car up Masada early, then float in the Dead Sea. It is a full day, and an organised tour saves hassle.
  • Bethlehem: Arab bus 231 runs from near Damascus Gate to the checkpoint in about 30 minutes, or take a taxi. See the Church of the Nativity and the separation wall murals. Bring your passport, and check the current situation before going. Half a day is enough.
  • Tel Aviv: the train takes about 35-45 minutes from Navon station. Go for the beach, Jaffa's old port and Carmel Market, a complete contrast in one day.
  • Ein Gedi: on the same road as the Dead Sea, this oasis has waterfalls and ibex. Combine it with Masada.
  • Abu Ghosh: an Arab village 20 minutes west by bus or taxi, famous for hummus and a beautiful Crusader church. It is a short, easy half day.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Forgetting Shabbat: landing on Friday afternoon and expecting trains, supermarkets and restaurants in West Jerusalem is the classic mistake. Stock up, and book a sherut or taxi.
  • Dressing for the beach: shorts and bare shoulders will get you turned away from churches, the Western Wall and Temple Mount. Carry a scarf.
  • "Free" guides at Jaffa Gate: friendly men offer to show you around and then demand serious money at the end. Agree a price up front or decline politely.
  • Red string and blessings: at the Western Wall, someone ties a red thread on your wrist or offers a blessing, then asks for a donation. A firm "no thanks" works.
  • Taxis without meters: especially from the airport and around the Old City gates. Ask for the meter ("moneh") or use an app like Gett.
  • Underestimating security: expect bag checks at malls, stations and holy sites, and long questioning at the airport on departure. Get there three hours early.
  • Taking photos of soldiers or praying people: ask first, and do not photograph at the Western Wall on Shabbat.

Jerusalem FAQ

How many days do you need in Jerusalem?

Three days is ideal for the Old City, Yad Vashem, the Israel Museum and Mahane Yehuda. Add one or two more for the Dead Sea and Bethlehem.

Is Jerusalem safe for tourists?

Day to day, the tourist areas are generally safe and heavily policed, but the political situation can change fast. Check your government's travel advice before and during your trip, and avoid demonstrations.

What should I wear in Jerusalem?

Cover your knees and shoulders for any religious site, and wear shoes with grip, because the Old City stones are slippery. A light scarf solves most dress-code problems.

Can non-Muslims visit the Dome of the Rock?

Non-Muslims can visit the Temple Mount esplanade during limited hours through the Mughrabi Bridge, but cannot enter the Dome of the Rock or Al-Aqsa. Hours change often and the site can close without notice.

Is Jerusalem expensive?

Yes, prices are close to Western Europe's. Hotels and restaurants are the big costs, while street food and the free sights keep a budget trip manageable.

What is open in Jerusalem on Saturday?

The Old City, East Jerusalem shops and restaurants, and Arab buses all run normally. Most of West Jerusalem, including the light rail, closes from Friday afternoon until Saturday evening.

Can you visit Bethlehem from Jerusalem on your own?

Yes. Foreign tourists can take bus 231 from near Damascus Gate and cross the checkpoint with a passport. Check the current conditions first, and note that Israeli rental cars are usually not insured there.

Do I need to book the Western Wall Tunnels in advance?

Yes. It is guided-only, and slots often sell out days ahead, especially in spring and autumn. Book online as soon as your dates are fixed.
