Sarajevo is a small, hilly capital squeezed into a valley, with Ottoman bazaars, Austro-Hungarian facades and grey socialist tower blocks all within one tram ride. It's cheap, the coffee is serious and the food is heavy on meat. The 1990s siege is still visible in shrapnel-scarred walls and in how people talk about it. It isn't a pretty-postcard city in the Prague sense. It's more interesting than that, and it suits travellers who like history with weight to it, good-value food and walking uphill.

We'd give it 3 days. Two is enough for the old town, the war museums and the Trebević cable car. The third day lets you see the Tunnel of Hope and slow down, or take a day trip to Mostar. Cash still rules here: the currency is the convertible mark (KM or BAM), fixed at roughly 2 KM to €1. Euros are accepted in many tourist spots, but usually at a poor rate.

What to see in Sarajevo: the places worth your time

Baščaršija and the Sebilj fountain

Baščaršija is the old Ottoman market quarter and the place every visit starts. It's a grid of low wooden shops, coppersmiths on Kazandžiluk street, ćevapi grills and pigeons around the Sebilj, the wooden fountain everyone photographs. Give it a couple of hours, spread across several visits, because it changes a lot between early morning and evening. It's free to wander. Our tip: come before 9am for the Sebilj without the crowds, then come back at dusk when the smell of grilled meat takes over.

Gazi Husrev-beg Mosque

This 16th-century mosque is the most important Ottoman building in Bosnia, with a courtyard, ablution fountain and mausoleum that are calm even when the street outside is packed. Entry to the interior costs about €3 (around 6 KM), and you need 30 minutes. Women are given a scarf at the door if needed, and everyone should cover their legs. Avoid prayer times, especially Friday midday.

Ferhadija and the "Sarajevo Meeting of Cultures" line

Walk west along Ferhadija and you'll hit a marker in the pavement where the Ottoman city ends and Austro-Hungarian Sarajevo begins. Stand on it and look both ways: minarets behind you, Viennese-style facades ahead. Within a few hundred metres you pass the Catholic Cathedral of Jesus' Heart, the Old Orthodox Church and the old synagogue. It's free, takes 20 minutes, and explains the city better than any brochure.

Latin Bridge and the Museum Sarajevo 1878-1918

On the corner by the Latin Bridge, Gavrilo Princip shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand in 1914 and set off the First World War. The bridge itself is a modest stone arch over the shallow Miljacka river. The small museum on the corner costs about €2-3 and takes 20-30 minutes. It's worth it for the context, though it isn't a big museum. Photograph the bridge from the next bridge downstream for the best angle.

Vijećnica (City Hall)

This striped pseudo-Moorish building at the eastern end of the old town was the National Library until it was shelled and burned in 1992, along with most of its collection. It reopened in 2014 after a meticulous restoration. Entry is about €5 (10 KM) and you need 45 minutes. The ceiling of the central hall is the reason to go in. The basement exhibition on the city's history is better than people expect.

War Childhood Museum

If you only see one war museum, make it this one. It tells the siege through objects donated by people who were children then: diaries, a ballet costume, a toy gun, each with a short story attached. Tickets cost about €7 (around 14 KM). Plan an hour, and maybe a coffee afterwards, because it hits hard. It's in Logavina, a short uphill walk from Baščaršija.

Galerija 11/07/95

This gallery, on the upper floor of a building by the cathedral, documents the Srebrenica genocide through photographs, video and survivor testimony. It costs about €6 (12 KM) and needs 60-90 minutes. It's heavy, and we think it's essential if you want to understand the country you're in. Get the audio guide.

Sarajevo Tunnel (Tunel spasa)

During the siege, an 800-metre tunnel dug under the airport runway was the city's only link to the outside world. About 25 metres of it survive at a family house in Butmir, now a museum with videos and the original wheelbarrows. Entry is about €10 (20 KM), and you need an hour there plus the trip. It's near the airport, so the cheap option is tram 3 to Ilidža, then bus 32 or a short taxi. Many people visit it on the way to or from a flight, and it's on most war tours.

Yellow Fortress (Žuta tabija)

This is the classic sunset viewpoint: an old Ottoman bastion above the old town with the whole valley spread out below. It's a steep 15-20 minute walk up from Baščaršija through the Vratnik neighbourhood, and it's free. During Ramadan, the cannon fired here marks the breaking of the fast. Go 45 minutes before sunset, because the stone walls fill up quickly.

Trebević cable car and the bobsleigh track

The cable car (žičara) rebuilt in 2018 lifts you from Bistrik, near the old town, up Mount Trebević in about 10 minutes. A return ticket for visitors is roughly €10-15 (20-30 KM), and the views over the city are excellent. From the upper station, walk down to the graffiti-covered 1984 Olympic bobsleigh track and follow it downhill. Allow half a day. Stay on marked paths: the area was a front line, and the hills around Sarajevo still have uncleared mines.

Svrzo's House

Svrzina kuća is an 18th-century Ottoman family home, with the men's and women's quarters kept separate, wooden ceilings and a quiet courtyard. It costs about €2-3 and needs 30 minutes. It's the best way to picture how wealthy Sarajevans lived before the Austrians arrived, and it's rarely crowded.

National Museum of Bosnia and Herzegovina

The Zemaljski muzej has excellent archaeology and ethnography sections, plus a botanical garden. It also holds the Sarajevo Haggadah, a medieval Jewish manuscript that is shown only occasionally, so check before you go if that's why you're visiting. Tickets cost about €4-5. It's on the tram line near the Holiday Inn, so you can combine it with Sniper Alley.

Things to do in Sarajevo

The best moments here aren't always in a museum. A few ideas:

  • Drink Bosnian coffee properly. It comes in a copper džezva with a sugar cube and a rahat lokum. Sip slowly, because nobody rushes it. Try Kafana Divan in the Morića Han caravanserai or any small place in Baščaršija, for about €1.50-2.
  • Take a war history tour. Local guides, many of whom lived through the siege, run half-day tours covering the Tunnel, Sniper Alley and the Jewish cemetery. They cost about €30-40 and are worth it for the personal stories.
  • Look for Sarajevo Roses. These are mortar scars in the pavement filled with red resin where people were killed. You'll find several in the centre, including near the Markale market.
  • Walk the Old Jewish Cemetery. It's a sloping hillside cemetery that was used as a front-line position during the war, with views back over the city. It's free and makes a good stop on the way down from Trebević.
  • Ride tram 3 end to end. It runs from Baščaršija to Ilidža past the yellow Holiday Inn, the old Tito-era trams and the socialist blocks of Novo Sarajevo. It costs a couple of marks and is the cheapest city tour you'll get.
  • Catch the Sarajevo Film Festival. In August, the city fills with screenings, open-air cinemas and parties. Book accommodation early.
  • Have a beer at the Sarajevska brewery. The red-brick brewery has a large beer hall where a pint costs about €2-3. It's a short walk from the old town across the river.

Sarajevo for free (or almost)

Sarajevo is already cheap, and plenty of the best bits cost nothing at all. The Yellow Fortress at sunset, the Meeting of Cultures line, the exterior of Vijećnica, the riverside walk along the Miljacka and the courtyards of the mosques are all free. The Eternal Flame on Ferhadija, a memorial to the victims of the Second World War, is right on your route.

Climbing through Vratnik and Bistrik, the steep residential streets above the old town, costs nothing and gives you better views than half the paid viewpoints. The Markale market, a covered fruit and vegetable market and scene of two massacres during the siege, is free and very local. A burek and a coffee for under €3 counts as "almost free" in our book.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Buying "handmade" copper in Baščaršija. Plenty of it is mass-produced. If you want a real piece, watch the craftsmen working on Kazandžiluk and buy from them directly. Expect to pay more.
  • The Latin Bridge as a big-ticket sight. It's historically huge and visually tiny. Spend five minutes there and put the time you save into the War Childhood Museum.
  • Eating every meal in Baščaršija. Prices around the Sebilj are inflated and the quality is mixed. Walk ten minutes towards Marijin Dvor or Bistrik and the food gets cheaper and more local.
  • The overpriced "war souvenirs". Pens made from bullet casings are sold everywhere, and many have no link to the war at all. A donation to the War Childhood Museum shop does more good.

When to visit Sarajevo

The best months are May-June and September-October: warm days, cool evenings and fewer crowds. July and August get hot (30°C or more in the valley), busier and pricier, especially during the Film Festival. Winter is cold and often snowy. On still days, the valley traps serious air pollution, and that's the city's worst season for smog.

The cheapest time is November to March, excluding the holidays, when hotels drop their rates noticeably. Ramadan is an interesting time to visit: evenings are lively with iftar meals, though some cafés are quieter during the day.

Where to stay in Sarajevo: best areas

  • Baščaršija: the old town, best for a first visit and short stays. Everything is walkable, but it's noisier at night. Guesthouses cost €40-70 per night, and boutique hotels €70-120.
  • Bistrik and Vratnik: the hillside streets above the old town. They're quieter and have great views, but every walk home is uphill. Rooms run €35-70.
  • Ferhadija and the Austro-Hungarian centre: flat, central and full of cafés, with the best mix of convenience and calm. Expect €50-100.
  • Marijin Dvor: the business district near the Holiday Inn, with chain hotels, the National Museum and a direct tram line. It suits people who want modern rooms at €60-110.
  • Ilidža: a spa suburb at the end of the tram line, close to the airport and Vrelo Bosne. It's good for families or for a final night before a flight, at €40-90.

What to eat in Sarajevo

Start with ćevapi: small grilled minced-meat sausages served in somun flatbread with raw onion and kajmak (clotted cream). A portion of 10 costs about €5-6 (10-12 KM). Željo and Ćevabdžinica Hodžić are local favourites, and the queues move quickly.

For burek, go to a buregdžinica such as Bosna in Baščaršija. Strictly speaking, burek means meat only. Cheese is sirnica, spinach is zeljanica and potato is krompiruša. Order by weight with a glass of yoghurt, for about €2-4.

Other dishes to try:

  • Begova čorba: a creamy chicken and okra soup that is perfect on a cold day.
  • Bosanski lonac: a slow-cooked pot of meat and vegetables.
  • Sogan dolma: onions stuffed with minced meat.
  • Klepe: Bosnian dumplings with garlic yoghurt.
  • Tufahija: poached apple stuffed with walnuts. Order it with your coffee, alongside baklava.

Inat Kuća, the house that was famously moved across the river brick by brick, serves traditional food at fair prices. A street lunch costs €4-7, and a sit-down dinner with a drink runs €12-20. Vegetarians manage fine on pies, soups and salads, but meat dominates.

Airport to city centre and getting around

Sarajevo International Airport is about 12 km from the centre. The airport shuttle bus (Centrotrans) runs to Baščaršija in about 30 minutes for roughly €3 (5-6 KM), timed loosely to flights. A taxi takes 20-25 minutes and costs about €12-18. Use an official taxi with a roof sign and meter, or a ride-hailing app. Local buses to Ilidža plus tram 3 are cheaper but slow with luggage.

In town, you'll walk most of the time. Tram 3 is the backbone of the network, running along the river from Baščaršija to Ilidža. A single ticket costs about €1 (around 2 KM) from a kiosk, slightly more from the driver. Validate it on board, because inspectors do check. Day tickets exist but rarely pay off unless you're going back and forth to Ilidža.

Day trips from Sarajevo

  • Mostar: the Old Bridge and its divers are the obvious pull. The train takes about 2 hours through the Neretva canyon and is one of the most scenic rides in the Balkans. Book ahead in summer. Buses take 2.5 hours.
  • Konjic: an Ottoman bridge, plus Tito's underground nuclear bunker (ARK D-0), which you can visit only on a pre-booked tour. It's about 1 hour by train or bus, and it fits easily with a Mostar trip.
  • Vrelo Bosne: the spring of the Bosna river, with ponds, swans and shady paths. Take tram 3 to Ilidža, then walk the avenue of plane trees (about 3 km) or take a horse carriage. Plan half a day.
  • Srebrenica-Potočari Memorial: the memorial cemetery and centre for the victims of 1995. It's about 3 hours each way, so a guided tour is the easiest option.
  • Lukomir: Bosnia's highest traditional village, on Bjelašnica mountain. It's reachable by tour or with a car, in about 1.5-2 hours. The roads can be closed in winter.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Exchanging money badly: paying in euros gives you poor rates, and airport exchange desks are worse. Use a bank ATM in town and decline the "conversion" offer on screen.
  • Unmarked taxis: stick to metered taxis or apps, and agree a rough price before getting in at night.
  • Menus without prices: a few places near the Sebilj quote high prices to tourists. If there's no price on the menu, ask before you order.
  • Walking off-trail in the hills: mine fields still exist around the city. Use marked paths only on Trebević and beyond.
  • Treating the war casually: people are generally happy to talk about it if you're respectful. Avoid jokes and arguments about who was to blame.
  • Underestimating the hills: bring proper shoes. The cobbles and slopes of Vratnik are steep and slippery when wet.

Sarajevo FAQ

How many days do you need in Sarajevo?

Three days is ideal: two for the old town, the war sites and Trebević, and one for the Tunnel or a day trip to Mostar. You can cover the highlights in two days if you move quickly.

Is Sarajevo safe for tourists?

Yes. Violent crime against visitors is rare, and the main risks are pickpockets in crowded areas and dodgy taxis. The only real danger is landmines off marked trails in the surrounding hills.

Is Sarajevo expensive?

No, it's one of the cheapest capitals in Europe. A good meal costs €10-15, a coffee costs under €2 and most museums charge €3-10.

Can you use euros in Sarajevo?

Often, yes, but the exchange rate is usually rounded against you. Pay in convertible marks (KM), and carry cash because many small places don't take cards.

Is the Sarajevo Tunnel worth visiting?

Yes. It's short, but it's one of the most powerful sites in the city. It's easiest to visit on a tour or on the way to the airport.

What is the best viewpoint in Sarajevo?

The Yellow Fortress is the classic sunset spot and it's free. The Trebević cable car gives a wider view from higher up for a small fee.

Is Sarajevo or Mostar better?

They're very different. Mostar is a single stunning bridge and old town that's best seen in a day. Sarajevo has more depth, better food and enough to fill several days.
