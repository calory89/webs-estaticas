# Shanghai in 4 Days: What to See, Eat and Skip in 2026

> Shanghai travel guide by Happy Low Cost, 4-day itinerary. Original page: https://www.happylowcost.com/en/destinations/shanghai/
> Updated: 2026-11-21

Shanghai is less a museum of old China and more a preview of where cities are heading. Glass towers face 1920s banks across the river, plane-tree streets hide some of the best cafés in Asia, and lunch can be a €3 bowl of noodles or a €150 tasting menu within the same block. It's cleaner, safer and easier than most first-timers expect, though the scale will still wear you out.

It suits food lovers, architecture fans and anyone who likes walking around a city rather than ticking off temples. If you want imperial palaces, go to Beijing. Give Shanghai **4 days**: three for the city and one for a day trip to Suzhou or a water town. Two days feels rushed, and after five you're mostly eating (not the worst problem).

## What to see in Shanghai: the places worth your time

### The Bund

The Bund is the riverside promenade of colonial-era banks and hotels on Zhongshan East Road, looking straight at the Pudong skyline. It's free and takes an hour, but you should come twice: once by day to see the architecture, once after dark when both banks light up. Our tip: go at sunrise to see locals doing tai chi with almost no crowds, then come back around 8pm (the lights usually switch off around 10pm, earlier on some nights).

### Shanghai Tower

China's tallest building has an observation deck on the 118th floor, roughly 550 metres up. Tickets cost about ¥180 (€23) and the visit takes about an hour. Pick a clear day, which in Shanghai means checking the air-quality app the night before, because on a hazy day you're paying to look at fog. It beats the nearby Oriental Pearl Tower, which is more fun to look at than to go up.

### Yu Garden

Yu Garden is a classical Ming-dynasty garden of rockeries, ponds and pavilions, wrapped in a busy, rebuilt "old town" bazaar. The garden costs about ¥40 (€5) and needs an hour. Go first thing, because by 11am the zigzag bridges are packed. The bazaar around it is a souvenir mall in traditional costume, so spend your money elsewhere.

### Former French Concession

This is the part of Shanghai people fall for: leafy streets, art deco apartments, little boutiques and very good coffee. Walk Wukang Road (stop at Wukang Mansion, the ship-shaped building), then Anfu Road, Fuxing Road and Yongkang Road. It's free and you can easily spend half a day. Go on a weekday morning, because Wukang Road on a weekend is a queue of people photographing the same corner.

### Shanghai Museum

The original Shanghai Museum on People's Square has one of the best collections of ancient bronzes, ceramics and calligraphy in China. Entry has been free with advance booking, though check before you go because the system changes. Allow 2-3 hours. The newer Shanghai Museum East in Pudong is larger and more ambitious, so if you only do one and love Chinese art, our tip is the East branch.

### Jing'an Temple

Jing'an Temple is a gilded Buddhist temple squeezed between shopping malls on West Nanjing Road, and that contrast is the reason to go. Entry is about ¥50 (€6.50) and 45 minutes is enough. Come late afternoon when the golden roofs catch the light against the glass towers behind.

### Jade Buddha Temple

This working temple in Putuo houses two Buddha statues carved from Burmese jade. Entry is about ¥20 (€2.50), plus a small extra fee for the main jade Buddha hall, and it takes an hour. It's more peaceful than Jing'an, and the vegetarian noodles in the temple restaurant are cheap and good.

### Tianzifang

Tianzifang is a maze of old shikumen lane houses in the French Concession, turned into shops, bars and snack stalls. It's free and takes an hour. It's touristy, but the lanes themselves are real, so go in the morning before the crowds and the stalls open, when you can actually see the buildings.

### West Bund and the Power Station of Art

The West Bund is a long riverside park in Xuhui lined with galleries, including the Long Museum and Yuz Museum, plus skate parks and cafés. The Power Station of Art, a converted power plant, is China's first state-run contemporary art museum and is often free or cheap depending on the exhibition. Give it half a day, rent a shared bike and ride the riverside path at sunset.

### M50 Art District

M50 is a cluster of old textile mills on Moganshan Road filled with galleries and studios. It's free and takes 1-2 hours. Walk along the long graffiti wall outside, which changes regularly and is often better than what's inside.

### Lujiazui Skywalk and Riverside

The elevated pedestrian ring around Lujiazui station lets you stand among the Pudong skyscrapers instead of looking at them from across the water. It's free and takes 30 minutes. Walk down to the Pudong riverside promenade afterwards for the best view of the Bund's old buildings lit up at night.

### Xintiandi

Xintiandi is a polished pedestrian district of restored shikumen houses, now upmarket restaurants and shops. It's free to wander and takes about 45 minutes. The Site of the First National Congress of the Communist Party sits right inside it, and the free museum there is worth half an hour for the political story alone.

## Things to do in Shanghai

Some of the best things to do in Shanghai have nothing to do with ticket booths:

- **Take the Huangpu ferry.** The public ferry between the Bund side (Jinling Road East) and Pudong costs ¥2 (€0.25) and gives you most of the river-cruise view for the price of a sweet.

- **Eat breakfast like a local.** Try cifantuan (sticky rice rolls) and fried dough sticks from a street stall before 9am, or soy milk at a hole-in-the-wall shop.

- **Get a foot massage.** A good 60-90 minute session costs €20-35 in a reputable chain, and after 25,000 steps it's a basic human right.

- **Have a cocktail on a rooftop facing Pudong.** Bars along the Bund charge €12-18 a drink. You're paying for the view, so go once and do it properly.

- **Visit the Marriage Market** in People's Square Park on a weekend, where parents advertise their unmarried children's height, salary and flat ownership on umbrellas. Just look, and don't take close-up photos of people.

- **Do a food walk in the French Concession**, either on your own or with a guided tour (about €60-80), which helps if you can't read menus.

- **See the acrobats.** The Shanghai acrobatics shows (ERA at the Shanghai Circus World is the best known) are touristy but impressive, from about €25.

## Shanghai for free (or almost)

Shanghai is easy to enjoy on a small budget, because many of its best sights cost nothing:

- The Bund by day and night, plus the Pudong riverside promenade.

- Walking the French Concession: Wukang Road, Anfu Road, Fuxing Park in the morning for dancing and mahjong.

- Shanghai Museum (free with booking, check current rules).

- The Lujiazui Skywalk and the walk around Century Park's edges.

- M50's galleries and graffiti.

- The ¥2 Huangpu ferry.

- Duolun Road in Hongkou and the old lanes around Lu Xun Park, where Shanghai still feels lived in.

- The West Bund riverside path, with a shared bike for about ¥1.5-3 (€0.20-0.40) per ride.

## What is overrated (and what to do instead)

- **East Nanjing Road.** It's a neon walkway of chain stores and people trying to lure you somewhere. Walk it once on your way to the Bund, then shop and eat on West Nanjing Road or in the French Concession.

- **The Oriental Pearl Tower.** Iconic from outside, but dated inside and lower than its neighbours. Go up the Shanghai Tower, or pay for a drink in a hotel bar with the same view.

- **Nanxiang dumplings at Yu Garden.** The queues are long and the prices high for what you get. Jia Jia Tang Bao, or any busy local dumpling shop, gives you better soup dumplings for less.

- **Huangpu night cruises.** At €15-30 they're fine but crowded. The public ferry and the Pudong promenade give you 80% of the view for almost nothing.

## When to visit Shanghai

The best months are **April-May and October-November**, when it's mild (18-25°C), skies are clearer and the plane trees look great. Summer (June to August) is hot and sticky at 30-35°C, with a rainy "plum rain" season in June and the odd typhoon. Winter is short but damp and grey, around 3-10°C, and many older buildings are poorly heated.

Avoid the Golden Week holiday in early October and the Labour Day break in early May: everything is packed and hotel prices jump. Chinese New Year (late January or February) is quieter in the city as people go home, though some small restaurants close. The cheapest flights and hotels are usually in November (after Golden Week), early December and March.

## Where to stay in Shanghai: best areas

- **Former French Concession (Xuhui/Huangpu border):** the best area for first-timers who like walking, cafés and good food. Boutique hotels from €80-180 a night.

- **Jing'an:** central, safe, great metro links and a mix of business hotels and design spots. Expect €70-200.

- **The Bund and People's Square:** best for views and sightseeing on foot. Hostels from €20-35 for a bed, historic and luxury hotels on the Bund from €200 upwards.

- **Lujiazui (Pudong):** for skyline views from your room and big international chains, €120-300. It's quiet and corporate at night, though, so it suits business trips more than couples.

- **Hongkou (North Bund):** cheaper, more local, a short walk from the Bund across Garden Bridge. Good mid-range options from €50-100.

## What to eat in Shanghai

Shanghainese food is sweeter and more soy-heavy than other Chinese cuisines, and it's far better than the "it's just a business city" reputation suggests. Here's what to order:

- **Xiaolongbao:** soup dumplings. Bite a small hole, sip the broth, then eat. A basket of 8 costs ¥20-40 (€2.50-5); crab versions cost more.

- **Shengjian bao:** pan-fried pork buns with crispy bottoms. Yang's Dumplings is the famous chain, and the filling is scalding.

- **Hong shao rou:** pork belly braised in soy and sugar, the city's comfort dish.

- **Cong you ban mian:** noodles tossed in scallion oil. Simple, cheap and addictive.

- **Hairy crab:** a seasonal delicacy from October to December. Pricey (€15-40 per crab) and fiddly, but locals take it very seriously.

- **Drunken chicken and smoked fish:** classic cold starters.

A noodle or dumpling lunch costs €3-8. A dinner in a good local restaurant runs €15-30 per person, and a smart Shanghainese or international place €40-80. Avoid restaurants with touts outside on East Nanjing Road and inside the Yu Garden bazaar: same food, double the price. The best sign of a good place is a queue of office workers at lunchtime.

## Airport to city centre and getting around

From **Pudong International Airport** you have three main options:

- **Maglev:** the magnetic train reaches 300 km/h and gets you to Longyang Road in about 8 minutes for ¥50 (€6.50), about ¥40 with a same-day plane ticket. You still need to change to metro Line 2 to get central, so it's more fun than fast overall.

- **Metro Line 2:** direct to the centre in about 60-75 minutes for ¥7-8 (€1).

- **Taxi:** 45-60 minutes, about ¥160-220 (€20-28). Only use the official taxi rank, or Didi (China's ride-hailing app).

From **Hongqiao Airport**, metro Lines 2 and 10 reach People's Square and the Bund area in 30-40 minutes for about ¥5-6 (€0.70). Hongqiao is also where most high-speed trains leave.

The metro is excellent, cheap (¥3-10 per ride) and signposted in English. A one-day pass costs ¥18 (€2.30) and a three-day pass ¥45 (€5.70), but you can also scan a QR code in Alipay or the Metro Daduhui app. Taxis via Didi are cheap compared with Europe, around €3-10 for most trips in town. Set up **Alipay or WeChat Pay with your foreign card before you fly**: cash is still accepted by law, but many small places barely handle it.

## Day trips from Shanghai

- **Suzhou:** famous for classical gardens and canals. The high-speed train takes about 25-35 minutes and costs ¥30-40 (€4-5) each way. Our top pick.

- **Hangzhou:** West Lake, tea plantations at Longjing and pagodas. About 1 hour by high-speed train, roughly ¥75 (€10) each way. Worth an overnight if you have time.

- **Zhujiajiao:** a canal water town on the edge of Shanghai, reachable on metro Line 17 in about an hour from Hongqiao for under €1. Busy on weekends.

- **Wuzhen:** a larger, more polished water town, about 2 hours by bus or a combination of train and car. Lovely at night when the lanterns are on, so it's best as an overnight.

Book train tickets on the official 12306 app or Trip.com, and bring your passport: you need it to board.

## Common mistakes and scams

- **The tea house scam:** friendly "students" around East Nanjing Road and People's Square invite you to practise English over tea, and the bill runs to hundreds of euros. Politely say no.

- **Fake art students** leading you to a "graduation exhibition" with high-pressure sales. Same trick, different prop.

- **Unofficial taxis** at the airport and train stations. Use the official rank or Didi.

- **Not setting up payments and a VPN in advance.** Google, WhatsApp and Instagram are blocked. Install a VPN or buy an eSIM with roaming data before arriving, and download Alipay plus offline maps (Amap or Apple Maps work well).

- **Trying to see everything.** The city is huge and travel between sights takes 30-45 minutes. Group by area, as in the itinerary above.

- **Turning up at museums without booking.** Many popular sights need advance reservations with your passport number.

## Shanghai FAQ

### How many days do you need in Shanghai?

Four days is ideal: three for the city's main sights and neighbourhoods, plus one for a day trip to Suzhou or a water town. With only two days, focus on the Bund, Pudong and the French Concession.

### Do I need a visa for Shanghai?

Many European nationalities can currently enter China visa-free for short stays, and many others can use the 240-hour visa-free transit if flying on to a third country. Rules change often, so check with the Chinese embassy before booking.

### Is Shanghai expensive for tourists?

Not really. Transport and local food are very cheap, and many sights are free. Hotels in prime areas and cocktails with a view are the main costs.

### Is Shanghai safe?

Shanghai is one of the safest big cities in the world, including at night. The main risks are petty scams aimed at tourists, not violent crime.

### Can I use credit cards in Shanghai?

Big hotels and malls accept them, but daily life runs on Alipay and WeChat Pay. Both now link to foreign Visa and Mastercard cards, so set one up before you arrive.

### What is the best area to stay in Shanghai for first-timers?

The Former French Concession or Jing'an. Both are central, walkable, well connected by metro and full of good places to eat.

### Is the Maglev worth it?

As an experience, yes: 300 km/h for €6.50 is fun. As transport it only saves time if you're staying near Longyang Road or in Pudong.

### Do people speak English in Shanghai?

More than elsewhere in China, especially younger people and hotel staff, but not in most small restaurants. A translation app with camera mode solves almost every menu problem.
