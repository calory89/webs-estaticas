Things to Do in Madrid: 3 Days of Art, Tapas and Rooftops

Madrid is not a postcard city like Paris or Rome. Few landmarks will stop you in your tracks. What it has instead is world-class art packed into a single mile, a street life that starts at 9pm and doesn't stop, and food that is still cheap by Western European standards. People who love museums, long lunches and late nights tend to fall for it. People who want a checklist of monuments sometimes leave wondering what the fuss was about.

Three days is the right amount. That gives you one day for the big museums and the Retiro, one for the old centre and the Royal Palace, and one for neighbourhoods and markets. Add a fourth or fifth if you want a day trip to Toledo or Segovia, which we strongly recommend.

What to see in Madrid: the places worth your time

Museo del Prado

The Prado is the reason many people come to Madrid, and it deserves it: Velázquez's Las Meninas, Goya's Black Paintings, Bosch's Garden of Earthly Delights. Give it 3 hours at least. Tickets cost about €15, and entry is free for the last two hours of most days. Our tip: the free slot brings a long queue, so either join it 30 minutes early or pay and go first thing, when Velázquez's rooms are almost empty.

Museo Reina Sofía

This is Madrid's modern art museum, and most visitors come for Picasso's Guernica, which is far bigger and more affecting in person than you expect. You also get Dalí and Miró. Budget 2 hours. Tickets are about €12, with free entry on some evenings and Sunday afternoons (check the hours). It sits right by Atocha station, so it combines easily with the Prado.

Museo Thyssen-Bornemisza

The third corner of the "Golden Triangle" fills the gaps the other two leave: Impressionists, Hopper, early Italians and German Expressionists. It's the easiest of the three to enjoy in 2 hours. Expect to pay about €13, though Mondays are free for the permanent collection. If you only have energy for two big museums, pick Prado plus this one if you prefer classic painting, or Prado plus Reina Sofía if Guernica matters to you.

Parque del Retiro

The Retiro is Madrid's living room, and it costs nothing. Walk to the Palacio de Cristal (a glass pavilion with free exhibitions), rent a rowing boat on the main lake for about €6-8, and watch the Sunday crowds. Allow 1-2 hours. Go on a weekend morning if you want to see locals jogging, busking and arguing over chess.

Royal Palace (Palacio Real)

The official royal residence is huge, with over 3,000 rooms, and the state rooms are properly over the top. You need about 1.5 hours. Tickets are around €14-16. Book online, because the walk-up queue in summer can take an hour. Afterwards, walk round to the Jardines de Sabatini or the Campo del Moro for the best views of the façade.

Plaza Mayor and Puerta del Sol

Plaza Mayor is the grand 17th-century square with red façades and arcades. It's worth 15 minutes, but not a meal: the terraces are overpriced. Grab a bocadillo de calamares (squid sandwich, about €4-6) in one of the bars on the side streets instead. Sol, five minutes away, is the noisy crossroads with the Kilometre Zero plaque and the bear-and-strawberry tree statue. See it once and move on.

Templo de Debod

A real 2,000-year-old Egyptian temple, gifted to Spain in the 1960s and rebuilt on a hill near Plaza de España. Entry to the park is free, and it's the classic sunset spot looking west over Casa de Campo. Arrive 30 minutes before sunset to get space on the wall. Expect crowds and someone playing guitar.

Gran Vía and Círculo de Bellas Artes rooftop

Gran Vía is the early 20th-century avenue of theatres, chain shops and grand domed buildings. Walk it from Plaza de España to the Metrópolis building at the Alcalá end. Then go up to the Círculo de Bellas Artes rooftop (about €5) for the best panorama in the centre. Drinks up there are pricey, so take your photos and come down.

Mercado de San Antón and other markets

Mercado de San Antón in Chueca has a real produce floor downstairs, tapas stalls upstairs and a rooftop terrace. It's more local than San Miguel and cheaper. Mercado de la Cebada in La Latina is even more down-to-earth. Plan on 45 minutes and €10-15 for a few bites and a drink.

El Rastro

Madrid's Sunday flea market runs down Ribera de Curtidores in La Latina. Expect antiques, vintage clothes, junk and a lot of people. Go early (before 11am) and keep your phone in a front pocket, because this is pickpocket heaven. Afterwards the whole neighbourhood moves to the bars of Cava Baja and Plaza de la Paja for vermouth and tapas.

Palacio de Cibeles

The wedding-cake building on Plaza de Cibeles is Madrid's city hall. It has free exhibitions inside and a viewpoint at the top (about €3-4) that looks straight down Alcalá and Gran Vía. Allow 45 minutes. It's a good alternative when the Círculo rooftop has a queue.

Santiago Bernabéu

Real Madrid's newly renovated stadium is a must-see for football fans and skippable for everyone else. The self-guided tour runs about €25-35 and takes 1.5 hours. Match tickets start at around €60-80 for big games and go up fast. Take metro line 10 to Santiago Bernabéu.

Things to do in Madrid

The city's best moments usually happen away from the sights. Here's what we'd make room for:

  • A tapas crawl on Cava Baja. This La Latina street is lined with tapas bars. Order one thing per bar and move on. Thursday to Sunday nights are the liveliest.
  • Vermouth hour. Around 1pm on weekends, madrileños drink vermut de grifo (vermouth on tap) with an olive and a slice of orange, about €3-4. Try Lavapiés or around the Rastro.
  • Churros at San Ginés. Chocolatería San Ginés, by the church off Calle Arenal, is open almost round the clock. Go at 2am after a night out, not at 5pm when the queue wraps around the alley. Churros with chocolate cost about €5-6.
  • Flamenco. Madrid has some of Spain's best tablaos. Corral de la Morería is the famous (expensive) one. Shows with a drink typically cost €35-50. Skip anything with a promoter handing out flyers on Sol.
  • Matadero Madrid. This former slaughterhouse in Arganzuela is now a cultural centre with exhibitions, cinema and a weekend vibe. It's mostly free, and it leads onto the Madrid Río park along the Manzanares.
  • A night in Malasaña. Bars around Plaza del Dos de Mayo and Calle del Pez, cheap beer, indie crowd. Nothing gets going before midnight.
  • A boat in the Retiro, then a picnic. Buy jamón, cheese and bread at a market and eat on the grass. It's cheaper than any terrace.

Madrid for free (or almost)

Madrid is one of the cheapest big capitals in Western Europe to see for nothing, mainly because its museums have generous free slots.

  • Prado: free the last two hours most days.
  • Reina Sofía: free some evenings and Sunday afternoons.
  • Thyssen: permanent collection free on Mondays.
  • Royal Palace: free for EU residents on certain late afternoons (bring ID and check the schedule).
  • Templo de Debod, the Retiro, Madrid Río and Casa de Campo: always free.
  • Almudena Cathedral: free to enter the main church (a small donation is appreciated).
  • Free tapas: some old-school bars still give a small tapa with every drink, especially in Lavapiés and around La Latina.
  • Free walking tours: they leave from Plaza Mayor and Sol daily. Tip €10-15 if it was good.

Madrid itinerary: 3 days

Day 1: Art and the Retiro

Morning: Be at the Prado at opening and spend 3 hours there. Head for Velázquez and Goya first, before the tour groups arrive. Afternoon: Have lunch with a menú del día in the Huertas/Barrio de las Letras area, then walk into the Retiro for the Palacio de Cristal and the lake. Evening: Go up to the Círculo de Bellas Artes rooftop at sunset, walk Gran Vía, then have dinner at 9:30pm in Chueca.

Day 2: Old Madrid and the palace

Morning: Walk from Sol to Plaza Mayor (a squid sandwich counts as breakfast), then on to the Royal Palace with a pre-booked ticket, and Almudena Cathedral next door. Afternoon: Have lunch around Mercado de San Miguel's side streets rather than inside it. Then visit the Reina Sofía for Guernica, or rest if it's August. Evening: Watch sunset at Templo de Debod, then take the metro or walk to La Latina for a tapas crawl on Cava Baja.

Day 3: Neighbourhoods and markets

Morning: If it's Sunday, go to El Rastro early. Otherwise, visit the Thyssen, then wander through Lavapiés. Afternoon: Have vermouth and a long lunch, then walk down to Matadero and Madrid Río. Evening: Go out in Malasaña, then get churros at San Ginés on the way home.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Mercado de San Miguel. It's beautiful iron-and-glass, but it's basically a tourist food court with €4 croquetas and nowhere to sit. Go to San Antón or La Cebada instead.
  • Eating on Plaza Mayor. You pay double for frozen paella. Paella isn't even a Madrid dish. Eat cocido or callos in a proper taberna instead.
  • Puerta del Sol. It's a transport hub with a statue, nothing more. Pass through it, don't plan around it.
  • Botín as a dinner. The world's oldest restaurant (according to Guinness) is historic, and the roast suckling pig is good. But the experience is very touristy and not cheap. If you want cochinillo, have it in Segovia, where it's the main event.

When to visit Madrid

The best times are late April to June and mid-September to October: 20-28°C, terraces open and long evenings. July and August are brutal, often 35-40°C. Many locals leave in August, and some family-run restaurants close, although hotels drop their prices.

Winter (December to February) is cold and dry, around 5-12°C with sunny days. It's the cheapest and quietest time, perfect for museums. Avoid the San Isidro festival in mid-May and big Real Madrid weekends if you want lower hotel rates, though both are fun if you're here for them.

Where to stay in Madrid: best areas

  • Sol / Centro: This is the most central area, where you can walk to everything. It's noisy and full of chain hotels. Expect €100-180 a night for a mid-range double, and €30-45 for a hostel bed. It suits first-timers.
  • Barrio de las Letras (Huertas): It sits between Sol and the Prado, with literary streets, bars and good value. Expect €90-160. It's our pick for a first visit.
  • La Latina: This is the old town, with tapas on your doorstep and the Rastro on Sunday. It's charming but loud on weekend nights. Expect €90-150.
  • Malasaña and Chueca: These are young, nightlife-heavy and LGBTQ-friendly (Chueca), full of cafés and vintage shops. Expect €90-150. Ask for an interior room if you want sleep.
  • Salamanca: This area is elegant, quiet and upmarket, close to the Retiro. Expect €150-300. It suits couples and families.

What to eat in Madrid

Madrid's own cooking is hearty and unfussy. The essentials:

  • Cocido madrileño: chickpea and meat stew served in two or three courses. It's a winter lunch dish. La Bola and Lhardy are the classics (around €25-30).
  • Bocadillo de calamares: fried squid in a bread roll, about €4-6.
  • Huevos rotos: fried eggs broken over chips with jamón. Casa Lucio on Cava Baja is famous for them.
  • Callos a la madrileña: spicy tripe stew, for the brave.
  • Croquetas, patatas bravas, tortilla de patatas: standard tapas, but quality varies wildly. Good bravas have a properly spicy sauce.
  • Jamón ibérico: a plate of decent ración costs €15-25.
  • Churros or porras with chocolate: porras are the thicker version.

Lunch is the main meal, from 2pm to 4pm. A menú del día (three courses plus a drink) costs €13-18 on weekdays and is the best deal in Spain. Dinner starts at 9pm or later. Tapas and drinks will run €20-30 a head, and a sit-down dinner with wine €35-50. A caña (small beer) is about €2-3.50.

Airport to city centre and getting around

Madrid-Barajas is close to town, and every option is cheap:

  • Airport Express bus (203): runs 24 hours from all terminals to Cibeles, and to Atocha during the day. The fare is €5, paid by card on board, and the trip takes 35-45 minutes.
  • Metro line 8: runs to Nuevos Ministerios in about 15-20 minutes, then you change for the centre. The fare is about €4.50-5 including the €3 airport supplement.
  • Cercanías train (C1) from T4: runs to Chamartín, Atocha and Sol in about 25-30 minutes for €2.60 or so. It's the best option if you land at T4.
  • Taxi: a flat fare of €33 to anywhere inside the M-30 ring road. The trip takes 20-40 minutes depending on traffic.

In town, the metro is fast, clean and frequent. Get a rechargeable Tarjeta Multi and load a 10-trip ticket (about €12), which works on metro and city buses. The Abono Turístico tourist pass (from about €8.40 for one day, around €17 for three in zone A) includes the airport supplement and makes sense if you ride a lot. Most of the centre is walkable, though.

How much a trip to Madrid costs

  • Shoestring: €60-80 a day. Hostel bed €30-40, menú del día lunch, a tapas-and-beer dinner, free museum slots, walking and a 10-trip metro card.
  • Mid-range: €150-220 a day per person (sharing a double). 3-star hotel €110-160 per room, one paid museum or the palace, a proper dinner with wine, a rooftop drink and the occasional taxi.
  • Extras to budget for: a flamenco show €35-50, a Bernabéu tour €25-35, a day trip train €15-40 return.

Day trips from Madrid

  • Toledo: a hilltop medieval city with a magnificent cathedral and El Greco paintings. The AVE from Atocha takes about 35 minutes (€15-25 each way), or the bus from Plaza Elíptica takes around 1 hour for less. Give it a full day.
  • Segovia: home to the Roman aqueduct, the fairy-tale Alcázar and roast suckling pig. Take the fast train from Chamartín (about 30 minutes, plus a bus from Segovia-Guiomar station) or the bus from Moncloa (about 1 hour 15 minutes).
  • El Escorial: Philip II's enormous monastery-palace. Cercanías C3 takes about 1 hour, or take the bus from Moncloa. Half a day is enough.
  • Ávila: a town wrapped in complete medieval walls you can walk on. The train takes about 1.5 hours.
  • Alcalá de Henares: Cervantes' birthplace with a historic university, and free tapas with drinks in many bars. Cercanías takes about 40 minutes. It's an easy half-day trip.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Pickpockets: they work Sol, Gran Vía, the Rastro and metro lines 1 and 3. Keep your phone off the table on terraces and your bag zipped in front of you.
  • The rosemary or petition trick: someone hands you a sprig "for luck" or a clipboard to sign, then demands money or lifts your wallet. Keep walking.
  • Eating at 7pm: you'll find empty, tourist-only places. Shift your clock: lunch at 2pm, dinner at 9:30pm.
  • Not booking the palace and the Prado in summer: you'll lose an hour in queues.
  • Ordering paella or sangria everywhere: neither is from Madrid, and both are often poor in the centre. Drink a caña or vermouth instead.
  • Unlicensed taxis at the airport: use the official white taxis with a red stripe at the rank, which charge the €33 flat fare.

Madrid FAQ

How many days do you need in Madrid?

Three days covers the main museums, the Royal Palace, the Retiro and a few good neighbourhoods. Add one or two days for Toledo or Segovia.

What is the best free thing to do in Madrid?

Visit the Prado in its free last two hours, then watch sunset at Templo de Debod. Both cost nothing and are among the best things in the city.

Is Madrid expensive to visit?

Not by Western European standards. Thanks to the menú del día, cheap beer and free museum hours, you can travel comfortably on €60-80 a day as a backpacker.

Is Madrid safe for tourists?

Yes, it's one of Europe's safer capitals, even late at night. The main risk is pickpocketing in crowded areas like Sol and the metro.

What area is best to stay in Madrid for first-timers?

Barrio de las Letras or Sol, because you can walk to the museums, the old town and the tapas bars.

What time do people eat dinner in Madrid?

Usually between 9pm and 11pm. Many kitchens don't really fill up until 10pm, especially on weekends.

Is Madrid or Barcelona better for a city break?

Barcelona has the beach and Gaudí, while Madrid wins on art museums, food prices and nightlife. Madrid also tends to be cheaper and less crowded with tourists.

How do I get from Madrid airport to the city centre?

The cheapest options are the 24-hour Airport Express bus (€5, about 40 minutes) or the Cercanías train from T4. A taxi costs a flat €33.
