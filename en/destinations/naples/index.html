Why go to Naples

Naples is loud, crowded, a bit scruffy and completely unlike anywhere else in Italy. Washing hangs across alleys, scooters ignore every rule you thought existed, and a stranger will probably tell you where to eat before you've asked. It's also one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in Europe, with a historic centre that is a UNESCO site and a food scene that most Italian cities would like to have.

It is also a very good base. Pompeii, Herculaneum, Vesuvius, Capri and the Amalfi Coast are all within reach, and flights here are often cheaper than to Rome or Florence. Go with some patience and you'll like it a lot.

What to see in Naples

Centro Storico and Spaccanapoli

The old town is laid out on a Greek grid, and Spaccanapoli is the straight street that slices through it. Walk it early, before the shutters go up and the crowds arrive. Nearby, Via dei Tribunali is the pizza and church street, and Via San Gregorio Armeno is the workshop street for nativity figurines, busiest at Christmas but fun all year.

Cappella Sansevero

A small chapel holding the Veiled Christ, a marble sculpture so fine that the shroud looks like real cloth. Book ahead, as it's small and sells out. Even sceptics go quiet in front of it.

Museo Archeologico Nazionale (MANN)

Much of what was found at Pompeii and Herculaneum ended up here: mosaics, frescoes, the Farnese collection. Visit it before you see the ruins and they make far more sense. Check the weekly closing day before you plan around it.

Napoli Sotterranea

A guided walk through the Greek and Roman cisterns and tunnels below the centre, later used as air-raid shelters. It's cool in summer and a good wet-weather plan. Tours are in English and the passages can be tight.

Duomo and Santa Chiara

The cathedral holds the relics of San Gennaro, the city's patron saint. The cloister at Santa Chiara, covered in painted majolica tiles, is the calmest spot in the old town.

Piazza del Plebiscito, Royal Palace and Teatro San Carlo

The grand, open side of Naples. The huge semicircular square is where the city breathes out, and the palace and one of Europe's oldest opera houses sit right beside it. The Galleria Umberto I, a glass-roofed arcade, is a few steps away.

Castel dell'Ovo and the seafront

Walk out to the castle on its little islet, then along Lungomare Caracciolo with Vesuvius across the bay. Sunset here is the free show of the city. The Santa Lucia and Chiaia areas behind it are the polished face of Naples.

Certosa di San Martino and Castel Sant'Elmo

Up on the Vomero hill, this former monastery has the best panorama over the bay, rooftops and volcano. Take the funicular up rather than the taxi, since it's part of the experience.

Quartieri Spagnoli

Dense, steep, loud and very photogenic, with murals of Maradona. Go in daylight and keep your phone in your pocket in the narrowest alleys. It's lively rather than dangerous, but pay attention.

Things to do

  • Eat pizza standing up. A pizza a portafoglio, folded in four, is a lunch for a few euros.
  • Ride the metro for the art. Toledo station is often named among Europe's most striking, with a tunnel of blue mosaic.
  • Browse Porta Nolana market. Fish, lemons, cheese and shouting, an honest slice of daily life.
  • Climb Vesuvius. The crater walk takes a couple of hours from the car park, and the views over the bay are worth it on a clear day.
  • Take a boat. Ferries and hydrofoils run to Capri, Ischia and Procida. Procida is the calmest and prettiest of the three.
  • Visit Pozzuoli and the Campi Flegrei. Steaming volcanic ground, Roman ruins and an amphitheatre, with almost no tour groups.

Naples itinerary: 4 days, day by day

Three days covers the city well, and four lets you add a day trip without rushing. Two is possible if you skip the museums.

4-day itinerary for Naples
DayPlan
Day 1Spaccanapoli, Cappella Sansevero, Duomo, Santa Chiara, then pizza on Via dei Tribunali. Finish at Napoli Sotterranea or with an evening walk through the Quartieri Spagnoli.
Day 2MANN in the morning, then Via Toledo, Galleria Umberto I, Plebiscito and the Royal Palace. Walk to Castel dell'Ovo for sunset.
Day 3Pompeii or Herculaneum by train. Herculaneum is smaller and better preserved, so choose it if you only have half a day.
Day 4(optional). Vomero views and the Certosa, or a boat to Procida or Capri.

When to go

April to early June and September to October are the sweet spot, with warm days, sea you can still swim in, and fewer queues than high summer. July and August are hot, humid and often above 30°C, and the ruins offer almost no shade. Winter is mild, typically around 8 to 14°C, but wet, and some island and coastal services are reduced. If you only care about food and culture, November to March is cheap and quiet.

Where to stay

  • Centro Storico: the most atmospheric option, with everything on foot. Expect noise at night. Good for short stays and mid-range budgets.
  • Chiaia and Santa Lucia: tidier, with the seafront, nicer shops and the best hotels. Costs more but feels calmer.
  • Vomero: residential and quiet, with a funicular down to the centre. Good for families and light sleepers.
  • Near Napoli Centrale and Piazza Garibaldi: the cheapest beds and the best transport links, but the area is rough at night. Fine for a one-night stopover, less so for a holiday mood.

Budget travellers will find hostels and simple rooms in the old town. Mid-range hotels are plentiful and good value compared with Rome. Book somewhere with decent soundproofing.

What to eat and where

Start with pizza, because it started here. The classic Neapolitan pizza has a soft, puffy edge and a soupy middle, and it's eaten with a knife and fork or folded by hand. Famous names include Da Michele, Sorbillo and Di Matteo, and the queues can be long. Plenty of smaller places a few streets away are just as good.

Beyond pizza, look for:

  • Frittatina and street fried food: a cuoppo is a paper cone of fried bites, ideal for a snack.
  • Pasta e patate con provola: creamy, comforting, better than it sounds.
  • Spaghetti alle vongole: clams, olive oil and garlic, best near the sea.
  • Ragù napoletano: a slow-cooked meat sauce, usually a Sunday dish.
  • Sfogliatella and babà: pastry for breakfast. The riccia is crisp and layered, the frolla softer. Pintauro on Via Toledo and Attanasio near the station are well known for them.
  • Coffee: short, strong, drunk at the bar for little money. Gran Caffè Gambrinus is the grand old café if you want the setting.

For a proper trattoria lunch, the Quartieri Spagnoli and the streets around Piazza Bellini have loud, cheerful places. Look for a short menu and a room full of locals.

Getting from the airport and around

Naples Capodichino airport is close to the centre. The Alibus runs to Piazza Municipio near the port and via the main station, and takes about 20 to 30 minutes in normal traffic. A taxi to the centre has set fares for common destinations, so ask the driver to confirm the price before you get in.

In town, the city centre is walkable, though hilly in places. Metro Lines 1 and 2, funiculars and buses cover the rest. Tickets are cheap, roughly a couple of euros for a single ride. For Pompeii and Herculaneum, take the Circumvesuviana train from Napoli Garibaldi. Get off at Ercolano Scavi for Herculaneum or Pompei Scavi for Pompeii, and keep a close grip on your bag, since pickpockets target that line. Cross roads confidently and steadily, and let the scooters go around you.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: about €60 to 80, with a hostel bed, street food and pizza, and public transport.
  • Mid-range: about €120 to 180, with a comfortable hotel, a couple of sit-down meals and one or two entrances.
  • Comfortable: €250 and up, with a seafront hotel, good restaurants and private transfers.

Pizza is among the best-value meals in Italy, often under ten euros. Archaeological sites and museums are the main extra cost.

FAQs

Is Naples safe for tourists?

Mostly yes, with the usual big-city caution. Pickpocketing and bag snatching are the main risks, especially around the station and on crowded trains. Keep valuables out of sight.

Is three days enough?

Yes for the city and one half-day trip. Add a fourth day if you want Capri, the Amalfi Coast or a Vesuvius hike.

Should I rent a car?

No. Traffic is chaotic and parking is difficult. Trains, ferries and buses reach nearly everywhere you'll want to go.

Pompeii or Herculaneum?

Pompeii is larger and more famous, and needs most of a day. Herculaneum is compact, better preserved and easier in a morning. Do both if time allows.

Can I drink the tap water?

Yes, Naples tap water is safe and widely praised. Fill a bottle and save money.

What to see in Naples in one day?

If you only have one day, follow day 1 of this itinerary: Spaccanapoli, Cappella Sansevero, Duomo, Santa Chiara, then pizza on Via dei Tribunali.

What to see in Naples in two days?

With two days, follow days 1 and 2 of this itinerary. Day 1: Spaccanapoli, Cappella Sansevero, Duomo, Santa Chiara, then pizza on Via dei Tribunali. Day 2: MANN in the morning, then Via Toledo, Galleria Umberto I, Plebiscito and the Royal Palace.
