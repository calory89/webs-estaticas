Why go to San Francisco

San Francisco is small, steep and strange, and that is exactly why it works. You can cross the city on foot in a day, yet it packs in a world-famous bridge, a Chinatown that predates most of America's, taquerías that deserve a pilgrimage and a coastline that disappears into fog at 4 p.m. in July. It is also an expensive city with visible social problems, and I'd rather tell you that now than have you find out on Market Street. Go with open eyes and you will leave a fan.

What to see in San Francisco

Golden Gate Bridge and the Presidio

Walk onto the bridge if the weather allows, but the best views are from the sides. Battery Spencer, on the Marin side, gives you the postcard shot. On the city side, take the Presidio's Crissy Field promenade, a flat, easy stretch with the bridge looming over you. Go early, before the fog decides to stay.

Alcatraz

Yes, it is touristy, and yes, it is worth it. The audio tour, narrated by former guards and inmates, is one of the best of its kind. Ferries leave from Pier 33 and sell out days or weeks ahead in busy months, so book before you fly.

Cable cars

Treat them as transport with a view, not a ride you must queue an hour for. The Powell-Hyde line is the prettiest, dropping toward the bay near Lombard Street. The California Street line is far less crowded and just as fun. Each ride costs a few dollars more than a normal bus, so don't use them for daily commuting.

Golden Gate Park

It is bigger than New York's Central Park and far more relaxed. Pick two stops: the California Academy of Sciences (aquarium, rainforest and planetarium under one living roof), the de Young Museum with its free-to-enter observation tower, or the Japanese Tea Garden. Rent a bike on a Sunday, when part of the main road is closed to cars.

The Mission District

Murals on Clarion Alley and Balmy Alley, sunshine at Dolores Park, and some of the best food in the city within ten blocks. Come in the afternoon, linger into the evening.

Chinatown and North Beach

Chinatown is a living neighbourhood, not a theme park, so wander off Grant Avenue into Stockton Street, where locals shop for produce. Ross Alley has a small fortune cookie factory. North Beach, the old Italian quarter, sits next door: City Lights Bookstore, a coffee at Caffè Trieste, then a climb up to Coit Tower.

The Painted Ladies and Alamo Square

The row of Victorian houses facing downtown is a quick photo stop, about twenty minutes. Combine it with Hayes Valley's cafés and shops.

Lands End and Sutro Baths

The coastal trail on the city's western edge, with the ruins of old swimming baths, cypress trees and views back to the bridge. It is the best free half-day in town and nobody talks about it enough.

Twin Peaks and the Ferry Building

Twin Peaks gives you the whole city laid out below, if it isn't swallowed by cloud. The Ferry Building, on the Embarcadero, is a food hall, a ferry terminal and, on Saturday mornings, home to the biggest farmers' market in town.

Things to do

  • Take a ferry to Sausalito or Tiburon for a lazy lunch and a view of the skyline from across the water.
  • Rent a bike and ride across the Golden Gate Bridge. It's mostly flat to the bridge, then a gentle climb, and you can return by ferry.
  • Drink an Irish coffee at the Buena Vista, near Fisherman's Wharf, where they have been making them for decades.
  • Browse Haight-Ashbury's vintage shops and record stores, then walk up into the quieter streets nearby.
  • Visit Muir Woods among the coastal redwoods. Parking and entry need advance reservations, so plan ahead, or take a tour from the city.
  • Watch the sea lions at Pier 39. They are noisy, smelly and oddly addictive.

San Francisco itinerary: 3 days, day by day

Three full days is the sweet spot. Four lets you slow down or add Muir Woods and Sausalito. Two is possible but rushed.

3-day itinerary for San Francisco
DayPlan
Day 1Morning at the Ferry Building, then Chinatown and North Beach on foot, Coit Tower, and a cable car down to the waterfront. Finish with sunset at Fisherman's Wharf or a walk along the Embarcadero.
Day 2Alcatraz first thing (book the earliest slot), then the Presidio and Golden Gate Bridge. Afternoon at Crissy Field, with dinner in the Marina or Cow Hollow.
Day 3Golden Gate Park in the morning, Lands End in the afternoon, then the Mission for burritos and murals at dusk.

When to go

September and October are the best months. The fog retreats, the days are warm and the crowds thin slightly. Spring is also lovely and green. Summer surprises people: July and August are often cold, grey and windy, especially on the western side, and a jacket is not optional. Winter brings rain, mostly between December and February, but it's rarely freezing, and the hotels are cheaper. Whatever the month, dress in layers, because you can go from sunshine to chill within a few blocks.

Where to stay

Union Square and Downtown: Central and well connected, with plenty of choice, but the streets around it can feel rough at night, especially toward the Tenderloin. Check the exact block before booking.

Fisherman's Wharf and the Marina: Good for families and first-timers who want to be near the water. Touristy, but safe and easy. Hostelling International has a hostel at Fort Mason with sea views.

North Beach: Great for atmosphere, cafés and walking. Quieter than downtown after dinner, though weekends can be loud.

Hayes Valley and the Mission: Best for food lovers and nightlife. Good Airbnbs and boutique hotels, though street noise is common.

Richmond and Sunset: Residential, cheaper and near Golden Gate Park and the ocean, but you will spend more time on buses.

Rough prices per night: dorm beds around $50 to $90, mid-range hotels $200 to $300, and upscale places $400 or more. Add hotel taxes, and note that parking at a hotel can cost $50 or more a night.

What to eat and where

The Mission burrito is the city's signature dish, big, foil-wrapped and stuffed with rice and beans. La Taqueria and El Farolito are the usual arguments, and both are worth it. Sourdough bread is a local religion, so try a clam chowder in a bread bowl at Fisherman's Wharf, even if locals roll their eyes.

Dungeness crab is the seafood to chase, with the season running roughly from late autumn through spring. Swan Oyster Depot on Polk Street, a tiny counter with a long line, is a favourite for crab, oysters and cold seafood. For cioppino, the tomato-based fish stew born here, head to the old Italian places near North Beach or the wharf.

For dim sum, Yank Sing near the Financial District is a classic, or find a no-frills spot in Chinatown. Pastries and morning buns at Tartine in the Mission are worth the wait, and Ghirardelli Square is fine for a sundae if you have a sweet tooth.

Getting from the airport and around

San Francisco International (SFO) is connected to downtown by BART, the regional train. It takes about 30 minutes to Powell Street or Embarcadero and costs around $10 to $12. Taxis and rideshares cost roughly $40 to $60, depending on traffic. Oakland Airport is also an option, linked by BART and often cheaper to fly into.

Inside the city, Muni buses, metro lines and the historic F streetcar cover most routes. Load a Clipper card on your phone or use the MuniMobile app. Walking is the best way to see many neighbourhoods, but expect steep hills and wear proper shoes. Driverless Waymo cars operate in parts of the city, and rideshares are widely used. I wouldn't rent a car for the city itself. Parking is expensive, and car break-ins are common, so never leave anything visible inside a parked vehicle. If you want to visit Muir Woods or Napa, rent for a day only.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: about $120 to $170, with a hostel bed, burritos, public transport and free sights like Lands End.
  • Mid-range: about $300 to $450, with a decent hotel, sit-down meals, an attraction or two and occasional rideshares.
  • Comfortable: $500 and up, with a nicer hotel, good restaurants and tours.

Tipping is expected: around 18 to 20 percent in restaurants. Prices on menus often exclude tax and sometimes a surcharge.

Frequently asked questions

Is San Francisco safe for tourists?

Mostly yes, if you use common sense. Petty theft and car break-ins are the main issues, and some areas, such as parts of the Tenderloin and around Sixth Street, are best avoided. The main tourist areas are fine.

How many days do I need?

Three days covers the highlights without rushing. Add a fourth for Muir Woods, Sausalito or simply more slow time.

Do I need a car?

No. BART, Muni and your own feet cover almost everything. A rental only makes sense for day trips outside the city.

Is Alcatraz worth the money?

Yes, for most visitors. The audio tour is excellent, but tickets sell out, so reserve in advance.

What should I wear?

Layers, always. A light jacket or windbreaker will serve you well even in summer, plus comfortable shoes for the hills.
