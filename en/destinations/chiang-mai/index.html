Chiang Mai is a mid-sized city with a moated square of old brick walls at its centre, more than 100 temples inside and around them, and green mountains on every horizon. It is slower and cheaper than Bangkok and the food is better than its reputation. There is no beach. The traffic outside the moat is worse than you expect, and from February to April the air can be some of the dirtiest in Asia.

It suits food lovers, temple wanderers, people who want a cooking class and a massage every day, and digital nomads who came for a week and stayed a year. Four days is the right amount: two for the city, one for Doi Suthep and the west side, and one for a day trip to the mountains or an elephant sanctuary. Add a few days if you plan to continue to Pai or Chiang Rai.

What to see in Chiang Mai: the places worth your time

Wat Phra That Doi Suthep

This is the temple on the mountain above the city, and if you only see one temple in Chiang Mai it should probably be this one. You climb the 306-step naga staircase (or take the small funicular) to a gold chedi with views over the whole valley. Allow 2-3 hours including the drive. Entry is about 30 baht (under €1). Our tip: go first thing, before the tour vans arrive, and bring a layer because it is noticeably cooler up there.

Wat Chedi Luang

A huge, half-ruined brick chedi in the middle of the Old City, partly brought down by an earthquake centuries ago. Up close it is more impressive than any polished temple. Give it 45 minutes. Entry is about 50 baht (€1.30). The "monk chat" tables in the grounds let you sit and talk with novice monks, which is far more interesting than reading the signboards.

Wat Phra Singh

The most important temple inside the walls, at the western end of Ratchadamnoen Road. The small Wihan Lai Kham at the back, with its Lanna murals, is the real reason to come. Allow 40 minutes. The main grounds are free and the wihan costs about 40 baht (€1). Come early or late in the day, because by midday the white walls reflect the heat straight back at you.

Wat Umong

A forest temple near the foot of Doi Suthep with meditation tunnels dug into a hillside and trees hung with little proverb signs. It feels a world away from the Old City even though it is a 15-minute Grab ride. Allow an hour. Entry is free or nearly so. Combine it with Wat Suan Dok and its white royal tombs, which are on the way back.

Wat Pha Lat and the Monk's Trail

A small jungle temple beside a waterfall, halfway up Doi Suthep, reached by a forest path that starts behind Chiang Mai University. The hike takes 45-60 minutes up and is steep and muddy after rain. Entry is free. Wear proper shoes, start early, and if you have the legs, continue on up to Doi Suthep and get a red truck back down.

The Old City walls and moat

The square moat is about 1.5 km on each side, and walking or cycling part of it is the easiest way to understand the layout. Tha Phae Gate on the east side is the busy, photogenic one. It is free and takes as long as you like. Late afternoon is the best light, and the quieter northern and western stretches are better for walking.

Sunday Walking Street

Every Sunday from late afternoon, Ratchadamnoen Road from Tha Phae Gate to Wat Phra Singh closes to traffic and turns into a long market of crafts, street food and buskers. It is the best market in the city and also the most crowded. Allow 2-3 hours. Arrive around 5pm, eat in the temple courtyards along the route, and leave before 9pm when the crowds stop moving.

Saturday Walking Street on Wualai Road

The smaller sibling, just south of the Old City on the silversmiths' street. It has fewer people, the same food, and Wat Sri Suphan, the silver temple, lit up next door. Women are not allowed inside the main ubosot at Wat Sri Suphan, which is clearly signposted. Allow 2 hours. If your dates only cover one walking street, Saturday is the more relaxed choice.

Warorot Market (Kad Luang)

The working market by the Ping River, where locals buy sai ua sausage, nam prik dips, dried fruit and cheap clothes. It is far more real than the Night Bazaar. Allow an hour or two. Entry is free. Go in the morning, then walk along to the flower market on the riverside.

Nimmanhaemin Road (Nimman)

The young, café-heavy district west of the Old City, packed with specialty coffee shops, small bars, the One Nimman complex and Maya mall at the top end. It is not traditional Thailand and does not pretend to be. Spend an afternoon or evening there. Prices are higher than inside the moat, with coffee at about 80-120 baht (€2-3).

Lanna Folklife Museum

A compact museum in an old colonial-style building behind the Three Kings Monument. It explains Lanna temple art, rituals and daily life better than any tour guide will. Allow an hour. Entry is about 90 baht (€2.50). Visit early, because it gives you the context to understand every temple you see afterwards.

Baan Kang Wat

A small artists' village near Wat Umong, with studios, ceramics, workshops and a weekend morning market. It is pretty and low-key, and you can walk round it in an hour. Entry is free. Go in the morning on a weekend if you want it lively, or on a weekday if you want it quiet.

Things to do in Chiang Mai

  • Take a cooking class. Most include a market visit and a farm setting outside the city, and cost about 1,000-1,500 baht (€26-40) for a half or full day. The Chiang Mai version usually includes khao soi, which is the reason to book here rather than in Bangkok.
  • Visit an ethical elephant sanctuary. Elephant Nature Park is the best known. Expect about 2,500 baht (€65) or more, and pick places with no riding and no bathing shows.
  • Get a Thai massage from ex-inmates. Lila Thai Massage trains women released from prison. An hour costs about 300-400 baht (€8-10) and the massage is genuinely good.
  • Watch Muay Thai. The Thapae Boxing Stadium runs regular fight nights. Tickets cost about 600 baht (€16), with ringside seats more.
  • Rent a scooter for the Samoeng Loop. This is a hilly 100 km circuit west of the city. Only do it if you are confident on a bike and have a licence your insurance accepts.
  • Go to the weekend Jing Jai Market. It is a morning farmers' and crafts market with good coffee, popular with locals.
  • Join Yi Peng and Loy Krathong if you are here in November, when thousands of lanterns go up and floating offerings drift down the river. Book accommodation months ahead.

Chiang Mai for free (or almost)

A good chunk of what is worth seeing costs nothing. Most temples inside the moat are free or under €1.50, and the walking streets, markets and river walks cost only what you choose to eat.

  • Wander the small temples of the Old City: Wat Chiang Man (the oldest), Wat Lok Molee near the north moat, and Wat Phan Tao next to Chedi Luang.
  • Do the Monk's Trail hike to Wat Pha Lat.
  • Sit at the monk chat tables at Wat Chedi Luang or Wat Suan Dok.
  • Watch sunset at the Chiang Mai University reservoir (Ang Kaew), a local jogging spot with the mountain behind it.
  • Browse the Sunday or Saturday walking street without buying anything.

Chiang Mai itinerary: 4 days

4-day itinerary for Chiang Mai
DayPlan
Day 1Old City. Morning: start at the Lanna Folklife Museum, then walk to Wat Chedi Luang and Wat Phan Tao. Afternoon: have khao soi for lunch, then Wat Phra Singh and the smaller temples to the north, with a massage to finish. Evening: eat khao kha moo from the stall at Chang Phueak Gate, or the Sunday Walking Street if it is Sunday.
Day 2Doi Suthep and the west side. Morning: hike the Monk's Trail to Wat Pha Lat early, then carry on up to Doi Suthep by red truck or on foot. Afternoon: Wat Umong, Baan Kang Wat and Wat Suan Dok. Evening: sunset at Ang Kaew, then dinner and a drink in Nimman.
Day 3Cooking class and the river. Morning: a half-day cooking class (most pick you up from your hotel). Afternoon: Warorot Market, the flower market, and a walk along the Ping River. Evening: Muay Thai at Thapae, or the Saturday Walking Street on Wualai Road.
Day 4Day trip. Choose one: Doi Inthanon, an elephant sanctuary, or the Sticky Waterfall plus Mae Kampong village. Most take the whole day. Back in the city, eat a slow last dinner at Huen Phen.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • The Night Bazaar. Chang Klan Road sells the same elephant trousers and fake goods you can find anywhere, at inflated prices. Go to the walking streets instead.
  • Tiger Kingdom and elephant riding. These are bad for the animals and depressing to watch. Choose a no-riding sanctuary, or skip animals altogether.
  • The "long neck" village tours. They function more like a human zoo than a cultural visit. Spend the money on a trek with a local Karen-run homestay instead.
  • Visiting Wat Rong Khun (the White Temple) on a rushed day tour. Six hours in a van for 45 minutes of temple is a poor trade. Sleep a night in Chiang Rai instead.

When to visit Chiang Mai

The best time is November to early February: cool mornings, dry days and clear mountain views. It is also the most expensive and crowded period, especially around Yi Peng in November and over Christmas and New Year.

Late February to April is burning season. Farmers burn crops and smoke fills the valley, so check air quality before booking, particularly if you have asthma. April is also very hot and hosts Songkran, the national water fight, which is a blast or a nightmare depending on you.

June to October is the rainy season. You get green hills, cheaper hotels and fewer people, and the rain usually comes as afternoon downpours rather than all-day storms. This is the best season for value.

Where to stay in Chiang Mai: best areas

  • Old City (inside the moat): walkable to most temples and best for first-timers. Guesthouses cost from about €15, and good boutique hotels €40-80. The quieter northwest corner beats the area around Tha Phae Gate.
  • Nimman: cafés, bars, malls and modern condos, so it suits longer stays, nomads and couples. Expect €30-90 a night.
  • Riverside (east of the moat): calmer, with upmarket hotels and good restaurants along the Ping River. Expect €50-200.
  • Santitham: local, cheap and close to Nimman and the Old City. It works for budget travellers and longer stays at €15-35.

What to eat in Chiang Mai

Chiang Mai is the home of northern Thai food, which is less sweet than central Thai cooking, heavy on herbs, and often eaten with sticky rice. The dishes below are the ones to order.

  • Khao soi: curry noodle soup with crispy noodles on top, usually with chicken drumstick. Khao Soi Khun Yai near the north moat and Khao Soi Lamduan Fah Ham are reliable, at about 60-80 baht (€1.60-2).
  • Sai ua: herby grilled pork sausage. Buy it at Warorot Market.
  • Nam prik noom: roasted green chilli dip, eaten with pork crackling and vegetables.
  • Gaeng hang lay: a rich Burmese-style pork curry.
  • Khanom jeen nam ngiao: rice noodles in a tomato and pork broth.
  • Khao kha moo: stewed pork leg on rice, from the famous "cowboy hat lady" stall at Chang Phueak Gate, about 50-70 baht.

A street lunch costs €2-4. Dinner at a decent local restaurant such as Huen Phen runs €6-12, and a nicer Nimman dinner with drinks €15-30. Avoid restaurants around Tha Phae Gate with laminated photo menus in six languages, because they are usually mediocre and overpriced.

Airport to city centre and getting around

Chiang Mai International Airport is only about 4 km from the Old City. The official taxi counter charges about 150-200 baht (€4-5) and the ride takes 15-20 minutes. Grab and Bolt work too and are usually similar or cheaper, with pickup at the marked zone.

For getting around:

  • Red trucks (songthaew): shared rides around town for about 30-50 baht per person. Agree the price before getting in.
  • Grab and Bolt: easy, fixed prices, and usually €1.50-4 within the city.
  • Scooter rental: about 200-300 baht (€5-8) a day. You need an international driving permit with a motorcycle category for police checkpoints and insurance.
  • Walking: fine inside the moat, but miserable along the big roads outside it.

There is no useful tourist travel pass. Pay per ride.

Day trips from Chiang Mai

  • Doi Inthanon National Park: Thailand's highest peak, with the twin royal chedis, waterfalls and a cloud forest boardwalk. It is about 2 hours by car, so a private driver or tour is easiest. Expect park fees of about 300 baht (€8).
  • Bua Thong (Sticky) Waterfall: limestone cascades you can climb barefoot without slipping. It is about 1 hour north by Grab or a hired driver. Entry is free.
  • Mae Kampong: a small mountain village of wooden houses and coffee farms, about 1 hour 15 minutes east. Combine it with the Sticky Waterfall on a driver day.
  • Chiang Rai: home to the White Temple, Blue Temple and Black House. It is about 3 hours by Greenbus from Arcade bus station, so it is better done as an overnight than a day trip.
  • Pai: 3 hours by minivan on a road with hundreds of curves. Take motion sickness pills, and give it at least two nights.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Booking in burning season without checking air quality. The views vanish and your lungs suffer.
  • Riding a scooter without the right licence. Fines are the small problem; your travel insurance refusing to pay after a crash is the big one.
  • Handing over your passport as a scooter deposit. Offer cash instead, and photograph the bike's existing scratches before riding off.
  • Tuk-tuk drivers offering cheap city tours. These end at gem and tailor shops where they earn commission.
  • Dressing wrongly for temples. Cover shoulders and knees, especially at Doi Suthep, or you will be renting a sarong at the gate.
  • Booking "sanctuaries" that still offer riding or bathing. Read the activity list carefully, not just the name.
  • Underestimating the distances outside the moat. Nimman looks close on the map but is a hot, ugly walk along busy roads.

Chiang Mai FAQ

How many days do you need in Chiang Mai?

Four days covers the Old City, Doi Suthep, a cooking class and one day trip. Add two or three more for Pai or Chiang Rai.

Is Chiang Mai worth visiting?

Yes, especially for food, temples and mountains at a lower cost than Bangkok or the islands. Skip it from late February to April if you are sensitive to air pollution.

Is Chiang Mai cheap?

Yes. You can travel comfortably on €60-100 a day, or about €30 on a tight budget, with khao soi at around €2.

What is the best month to visit Chiang Mai?

November to January for weather and festivals, and the rainy months for lower prices. Avoid March and April unless you specifically want Songkran.

Should I stay in the Old City or Nimman?

Stay in the Old City for a short first trip, because you can walk to the temples. Nimman suits longer stays and people who care more about cafés and nightlife.

Is Chiang Mai safe for tourists?

Very safe by most standards. The real risks are traffic, scooter accidents and the occasional overcharge.

Can you do Doi Suthep without a tour?

Yes. Red trucks run up from near Chiang Mai University, or you can take a Grab, or hike the Monk's Trail. You do not need a guide.

What to see in Chiang Mai in one day?

If you only have one day, follow day 1 of this itinerary: Old City. Morning: start at the Lanna Folklife Museum, then walk to Wat Chedi Luang and Wat Phan Tao.

What to see in Chiang Mai in two days?

With two days, follow days 1 and 2 of this itinerary. Day 1: Old City. Morning: start at the Lanna Folklife Museum, then walk to Wat Chedi Luang and Wat Phan Tao. Day 2: Doi Suthep and the west side. Morning: hike the Monk's Trail to Wat Pha Lat early, then carry on up to Doi Suthep by red truck or on foot.
