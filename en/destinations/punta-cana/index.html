Travel guide

Punta Cana in 5 days: beaches, food and honest travel tips

🏨 Hotels in Punta Cana

Why go to Punta Cana

Punta Cana is not a town. It is a 30-odd-mile run of coconut-palm coastline on the eastern tip of the Dominican Republic, stitched together by resorts, golf courses and a few real settlements. If you want warm Caribbean water, long sand and flights that are often cheap from Europe and North America, it delivers better than almost anywhere.

The catch is that most people never leave their resort. That is a shame, because twenty minutes inland you find sugar-cane roads, roadside fruit stands and a very different Dominican Republic. My advice: enjoy the beach, but give yourself at least two days outside the fence.

What to see: the places worth your time

  • Bávaro Beach. The classic: wide, pale sand, shallow water and palms leaning over the shore. Walk it early, before 8am, when only joggers and rakers are out.
  • Macao Beach. Wilder and more local, with waves, a public feel and a few beach shacks serving fried fish. It gets busy at weekends with Dominican families, which is part of the charm.
  • Juanillo Beach (Cap Cana). A sheltered, calm cove with very clear water. Access is controlled, so check how to get in before you go.
  • Playa Blanca and Cabeza de Toro. Quieter stretches south of Bávaro, good for a long walk and a less crowded swim.
  • Hoyo Azul at Scape Park. A freshwater cenote-style pool at the base of a cliff in Cap Cana. It is touristy and priced like it, but the turquoise water is genuinely beautiful.
  • Indigenous Eyes Ecological Reserve. Freshwater lagoons inside the Puntacana Resort grounds, set in tropical forest. It is calm and good for a swim between beach days.
  • El Cortecito. The most walkable beachfront in Bávaro, with bars, souvenir stalls and fishing boats. It is touristy, but it feels like a place.
  • Isla Saona. The day trip everyone does, by speedboat and catamaran, with a stop at a natural pool full of starfish. It is crowded but pretty. Book a small-group option if you can.
  • Altos de Chavón (near La Romana). A hilltop replica of a Mediterranean village with an amphitheatre above the Chavón River. Go late afternoon for the light.
  • Basílica de Higüey. The towering concrete basilica is the country's most important pilgrimage site. It is not pretty in a conventional way, but it is striking and tells you something real about Dominican faith.

Things to do

Snorkelling on the reef off Bávaro and Cap Cana is easy and rewarding. Catamaran trips with an open bar are sold on every corner; they are fun but loud, so pick one with a modest group size.

Buggy tours are the other big thing: you drive through mud and countryside, stop at a cenote-style cave and sometimes at a local farm for coffee, cacao and fresh fruit. You will get filthy, which is the point.

Golf is a real draw. Punta Espada in Cap Cana and Corales at Puntacana Resort are well known and booked well ahead in winter. If you prefer horses, the countryside rides inland are far nicer than anything on the beach.

For nightlife, Coco Bongo at Downtown Punta Cana is the show-club option. For something calmer, a rum on the sand at El Cortecito does the job.

How many days, and a suggested route

Four to five days is the sweet spot. Less than three and you are just landing and leaving; more than a week and you will want a second base elsewhere in the country.

  • Day 1: Arrive, swim, sunset walk on Bávaro, dinner at El Cortecito.
  • Day 2: Macao Beach in the morning, buggy tour in the afternoon.
  • Day 3: Isla Saona full day, or a catamaran trip if you want something shorter.
  • Day 4: Scape Park or Indigenous Eyes, then Cap Cana marina for dinner.
  • Day 5: La Romana and Altos de Chavón, or Higüey basilica, or simply sleep in and read.

Santo Domingo's Zona Colonial is a proper day trip, but it is roughly two and a half to three hours each way. If colonial history matters to you, stay a night there instead of rushing it.

When to go: weather and seasons

Peak season runs December to April: dry, warm and breezy, with highs around 28-30°C. It is also the most expensive. May to June is a good compromise: hotter, a few more showers, lower prices.

The rainy season is roughly June to November, with hurricane risk highest from August to October. Rain usually comes in short, heavy bursts rather than all-day grey. Sargassum seaweed can wash up on some beaches, mainly in spring and summer; it varies year to year and beach to beach, so check recent photos before booking.

Where to stay by area and budget

  • Bávaro and Cortecito (mid-range to budget). The biggest choice, with the best beach and a lot of all-inclusives. Good for first-timers and families.
  • Punta Cana Resort area (upper mid-range to luxury). Golf, a marina and the airport close by. Tidy and quiet, but you will pay for it.
  • Cap Cana (luxury). Gated, polished, with a marina and top golf. Lovely, but not cheap and not very local.
  • Uvero Alto (mid-range to luxury). Further north, with dramatic coastline and quieter beaches, but you are far from everything outside your resort.
  • Apartments and guesthouses near Los Corales. The best option for budget travellers who want to eat locally and avoid all-inclusive prices.

All-inclusive is the default and usually good value. Check the beach, not just the room: some resorts sit on rocky or seaweed-prone sections.

What to eat and where

The national plate is la bandera: white rice, red beans and stewed chicken or beef, usually with salad and fried plantain. It is cheap, filling and eaten for lunch. Try sancocho (a thick meat-and-root-vegetable stew) on a Sunday, mangú (mashed plantain with fried onions and eggs or cheese) for breakfast, and pescado con coco if you see it.

At beach shacks, order the day's catch with tostones (twice-fried green plantain). Pair it with a cold Presidente beer. At the end of the evening, a glass of Brugal or Barceló rum, or mamajuana, the herb-and-bark rum liquor sold in every souvenir shop, is the local way to finish.

For eating outside the resort, Jellyfish on Bávaro beach is a well-known sit-down option with tables on the sand. The marina in Cap Cana has a string of good restaurants. For the cheapest honest meal, find a comedor, a simple local diner, away from the beach road.

Getting from the airport and getting around

Punta Cana International (PUJ) is unusual: open-air terminal buildings with palm-thatched roofs, a short walk from the plane. Immigration queues can be long at peak times, so be patient.

Most hotels offer transfers, and licensed taxis run fixed rates posted at the exit. Bávaro is about 20-30 minutes away and costs roughly US$30-40 by taxi, with more for Uvero Alto or Cap Cana. Agree the price first.

Getting around is the weak point. Ride-hailing is unreliable in the tourist zones, so use official taxis or hotel drivers. Local guaguas (shared minibuses) run along the main road toward Higüey and are very cheap, but they are crowded and hard to read without Spanish. For day trips, a rental car is useful if you are comfortable with confident local driving; otherwise book organised tours.

Before you fly, complete the official online entry and exit form, which is required for travellers to the country. Check the current requirements for your passport.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget (apartment or guesthouse, local food): about US$60-90 a day.
  • Mid-range (all-inclusive, one excursion every couple of days): about US$150-250 per person a day.
  • Luxury (Cap Cana, golf, private trips): US$400 and up.

Day trips such as Saona commonly run from around US$70-120 per person. US dollars are widely accepted, but pay in pesos at local places for fairer prices.

FAQ

Is Punta Cana safe?

The resort zones are generally safe and well patrolled. Use normal city sense: don't leave valuables on the beach, avoid isolated areas at night and use official taxis.

Can I drink the tap water?

No. Stick to bottled or filtered water, including for brushing teeth if you have a sensitive stomach. Resorts supply drinking water.

Do I need Spanish?

Not in resorts, where English is common. A few basics help a lot outside them: gracias, buenos días and ¿cuánto cuesta?

Is all-inclusive worth it?

Usually, if you plan to spend most of your time at the resort. If you want to eat out and explore, a room-only deal in Bávaro can work out cheaper and more interesting.

What is the best month for cheap flights?

May, June and early November tend to be cheaper than winter, though they come with more rain and hurricane-season risk. January to March is the safest weather but the priciest.
