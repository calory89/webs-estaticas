Mexico City travel guide: what to see, eat and skip in 4 days

🏨 Hotels in Mexico City

Why go to Mexico City

Mexico City is enormous, loud, high up in the mountains and one of the best places on earth to eat. It sits at around 2,240 metres, which you will notice on your first staircase. Underneath the modern city lie the remains of Tenochtitlan, the Aztec capital, and you can see them from a café terrace in the middle of downtown.

It is also good value. Great museums cost a few dollars, a plate of tacos costs less than a coffee back home, and the quality of the food holds up at every price level. Flights here are often cheap enough to justify a long weekend, though you will want more time than that.

What to see: the places that earn their queue

  • The Zócalo and Catedral Metropolitana. The vast main square is the centre of everything. Go early, before the crowds and the stalls. The cathedral leans slightly because it was built on soft lakebed, and you can see it in the floor.
  • Templo Mayor. The excavated Aztec temple precinct sits right beside the cathedral. The site museum is small and excellent, and it makes the layered history of the city click.
  • Palacio de Bellas Artes. A white marble opera house with Rivera, Orozco and Siqueiros murals inside. Even a quick visit is worth it for the Art Nouveau and Art Deco interior.
  • Museo Nacional de Antropología. In Chapultepec Park. This is the one museum I tell everyone not to skip. Give it at least three hours, and remember it is closed on Mondays.
  • Castillo de Chapultepec. A short uphill walk from the museum, with a historic castle and a wide view over Reforma and the skyline.
  • Coyoacán and the Casa Azul. Cobbled lanes, a lively plaza and the Frida Kahlo Museum, which sells out. Book tickets online days ahead and expect it to be shut on Mondays.
  • Xochimilco. Colourful trajineras glide along the old canals. Go on a weekday morning and it feels like a pleasant boat ride; on a Saturday afternoon it is a floating party.
  • Roma and Condesa on foot. Not a sight so much as a way to spend an afternoon: tree-lined streets, Art Deco apartment blocks, bookshops, and Parque México with its dog walkers.
  • Ciudad Universitaria (UNAM). A UNESCO-listed campus with Juan O'Gorman's mosaic library. It is off the main tourist loop, which is half the appeal.
  • Teotihuacán. Technically a day trip, but it belongs on the list. The Pyramids of the Sun and the Moon are astonishing, and the site is exposed, so bring sun protection.

Things to do beyond the checklist

Catch a lucha libre night at Arena México. It is theatrical, cheap and very loud. On Sundays, part of Paseo de la Reforma closes to cars for cyclists and walkers, which is a good, calm way to see the city's wide boulevard.

Wander the markets: Mercado de Coyoacán for tostadas, Mercado de San Juan for the odd ingredients chefs buy. Spend an evening in a mezcalería in Roma Norte or Centro, and ask for a small pour of something you cannot pronounce. For art, the Museo Soumaya has a free-entry reputation and a silver tile-covered building you will either love or find odd, and the Museo Tamayo in Chapultepec is quieter.

How many days and a suggested route

Four days is the sweet spot. Three works if you stay disciplined. Five lets you add Teotihuacán or Xochimilco without rushing.

  • Day 1, Centro Histórico: Zócalo, cathedral, Templo Mayor, Bellas Artes, then dinner nearby. Keep the pace slow while the altitude settles in.
  • Day 2, Chapultepec: Anthropology museum in the morning, the castle after lunch, and an evening in Polanco or Condesa.
  • Day 3, south: Coyoacán and the Casa Azul, then Xochimilco or UNAM in the afternoon.
  • Day 4, Teotihuacán: Leave early, be back by mid-afternoon, and spend the evening in Roma.

When to go: weather and seasons

The city is mild year-round thanks to the altitude. The dry season runs from about November to April, with sunny days and cool nights, so bring a jacket. March to May is the warmest, and the air can feel heavy. The rainy season, roughly June to October, usually means a downpour in the late afternoon, then clear skies again. Mornings are generally dry and fine for sightseeing.

Late October and early November bring Día de Muertos, with altars, parades and marigolds everywhere. It is wonderful, but flights and hotels get pricier, so book early. September has Independence Day celebrations in the Zócalo on the night of the 15th.

Where to stay by area and budget

  • Roma Norte and Condesa: The best base for first-timers. Walkable, great food, safe feeling at night. Boutique hotels and apartments from mid-range upward, with a few good hostels.
  • Centro Histórico: Handy for the big sights and cheaper. It is busy by day and can be empty and rough around the edges at night, so choose your street with some care.
  • Polanco: Upscale, quiet, close to Chapultepec's museums. Better for comfort than for character.
  • Coyoacán: Calm and leafy, but far from everything else. Good for a longer stay.

As a rough guide, expect hostel beds from around US$15 to 30, solid mid-range rooms at US$70 to 130, and polished boutique stays above that.

What to eat and where

Start with tacos al pastor, pork shaved from a spit with a sliver of pineapple. Taquería Orinoco and countless corner stands do them well. Try suadero and campechanos late at night; Los Cocuyos in the centre is a classic stop.

For breakfast, order chilaquiles, tamales or a tlacoyo, a stuffed blue-corn oval from a street cart. In the evening, grab esquites, corn in a cup with mayonnaise, lime and chilli. Weekend lunch means pozole. For something sit-down, Contramar in Roma is famous for its tuna tostadas and grilled fish, so book ahead. Pujol is the headline fine-dining name if you have the budget.

Finish with churros and thick hot chocolate at Churrería El Moro. It is a ritual, not a secret, and still worth it. Stick to busy stalls with a queue and a high turnover, and you will usually be fine.

Getting from the airport and getting around

The main airport is Benito Juárez (AICM). The quickest sensible option is an official airport taxi from the booths inside the terminal, where you pay a fixed fare first, or a ride-hailing app. Uber works well in the city and is usually the easiest way to avoid street taxi hassles. The Metro does run near the airport, but it is crowded and awkward with a suitcase. Some budget flights use Felipe Ángeles (AIFA), which is much farther north, so check before you book and allow extra time for the transfer.

Within the city, the Metro is cheap and fast but gets crushed at rush hour, and some carriages are reserved for women and children. The Metrobús runs along main avenues like Insurgentes. Buy a rechargeable card. Walk Roma, Condesa and Coyoacán; use Uber between neighbourhoods at night.

Rough daily budget

  • Tight: about US$40 to 60 with a hostel, street food, Metro and one or two museums.
  • Comfortable: about US$100 to 150 with a decent hotel, good restaurants and a few rides.
  • Treat yourself: US$250 or more with a boutique hotel and a tasting menu.

FAQ

Is Mexico City safe for tourists?

Most visitors have no problems in the central, popular areas. Use common sense: keep your phone out of sight on the street, use ride-hailing at night, and avoid empty streets late.

Can I drink the tap water?

No. Drink bottled or filtered water. Ice in restaurants is normally made with purified water.

Do I need Spanish?

Not in tourist areas, but a few phrases help a lot. Street stalls and markets rarely have English menus.

How do I cope with the altitude?

Drink water, go easy on alcohol the first night and keep day one light. Most people adjust within a day or two.

Is it worth visiting Teotihuacán?

Yes. Go early to beat the heat and the tour buses, wear a hat and carry water. Climbing is limited on some structures, so check access before you go.
