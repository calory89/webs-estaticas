Bordeaux is a handsome, slightly smug port city of pale limestone, wide quays and very good lunches. Twenty years ago the facades were black with soot and the riverfront was car parks. Today it's one of the most walkable cities in France, with a tram that actually works and a centre listed by UNESCO almost in its entirety. The wine is famous, but the city itself is the reason to come: long evenings on the Garonne, Saturday oysters at the market, and neighbourhoods that feel lived-in rather than staged.

It suits couples, food people, slow walkers and anyone who wants France without Paris prices or Paris crowds. Families do fine too, thanks to the flat streets and the riverside parks. Three days is the right amount: two to see the city properly and one for a day trip to Saint-Émilion or the Atlantic coast. With only a weekend you can cover the centre, but you'll be choosing between wine country and the sea.

What to see in Bordeaux: the places worth your time

Place de la Bourse and the Miroir d'Eau

This is the postcard: an 18th-century royal square facing the river, with the world's largest reflecting pool laid out in front of it. The Miroir d'Eau is a thin sheet of water on granite that floods, drains and lets off mist every few minutes. It's free and takes 20 minutes, unless you sit down, which most people do. Our tip: come at dusk when the facades light up gold, or first thing in the morning when the reflection is perfectly still and nobody is splashing through it. The pool is switched off in the colder months, so check before you plan photos around it.

Saint-Pierre and the old town

The tangle of streets behind the Bourse is the oldest part of Bordeaux and the nicest place to wander. Place du Parlement and Place Saint-Pierre are lined with café terraces, and Rue des Bahutiers and Rue du Pas-Saint-Georges hold good small restaurants. Allow an hour or two, free. Avoid the terraces on the main squares for dinner. Eat one street back and have a drink on the square afterwards.

Cathédrale Saint-André and Tour Pey-Berland

The Gothic cathedral is free to enter and worth 20 minutes. The real reason to come is the detached bell tower next to it. Climbing the Tour Pey-Berland costs about €8, involves roughly 230 narrow steps and gives the best view over the city's rooftops. Go early in the day: the tower lets small groups up at a time, and the queue builds by late morning.

La Cité du Vin

The swirling, decanter-shaped wine museum on the river in Bacalan is the most famous building in modern Bordeaux. Entry is about €22 and includes a glass at the top-floor Belvedere bar. The interactive exhibition is clever but long and noisy, and budget two to three hours. Book online for a specific time slot, go in the morning, and save the tasting for the end, when the light over the river is better.

Les Chartrons

The old wine merchants' quarter just north of the centre is the most pleasant neighbourhood for a slow afternoon. Rue Notre-Dame is lined with antique shops, delis and cafés. On Sunday mornings the Marché des Quais sets up along the river, where locals go for oysters and white wine. Free, two hours, and an easy walk or tram B ride from the centre.

Marché des Capucins

Bordeaux's main covered market, near Saint-Michel, is where the city eats on weekend mornings. Order a half dozen Arcachon oysters with a glass of Entre-Deux-Mers at one of the counters for about €10-15. Go on a Saturday or Sunday before 11am. After that it's packed, and by early afternoon it closes. Skip it on a Monday, when it's dead.

Saint-Michel

The Basilique Saint-Michel and its separate 114-metre spire (the tallest in southern France) dominate a scruffy, multicultural neighbourhood with a flea market around the square. The spire can be climbed in the warmer months for a few euros. The area is a little rough at night, but in the daytime it's a refreshing break from the polished centre. Allow an hour, including a coffee on the square.

Grosse Cloche and Rue Sainte-Catherine

The Grosse Cloche is a medieval city gate with a huge bell, and it's a quick, free photo stop. From there you hit Rue Sainte-Catherine, one of Europe's longest pedestrian shopping streets. It's chain stores almost all the way, so walk it once to get your bearings and spend your time elsewhere.

Musée d'Aquitaine

The best museum in town for understanding the city, from prehistory to the port's role in the Atlantic slave trade, which it covers frankly. Expect about €5-8 and one to two hours. Municipal museums are free on the first Sunday of the month, though that also makes them busy.

CAPC Musée d'Art Contemporain

A contemporary art museum inside a vast 19th-century colonial goods warehouse in Chartrons. The building alone is worth the ticket of around €5-8. Exhibitions vary in quality, so check what's on first. The rooftop café is a good, quiet stop.

Bassins de Lumières

Immersive digital art projections inside a giant WWII German submarine base, reflected in the flooded docks. Tickets run about €15-17 and the visit takes an hour. It's busy at weekends, so book a weekday slot. The concrete bunker itself is as memorable as the show.

Jardin Public

An elegant 18th-century park north of the centre, with a pond, palm trees and locals reading on the grass. It's free and good for an hour of shade in summer, and makes a natural break between the Musée d'Aquitaine side of town and Chartrons.

Pont de Pierre

Napoleon's stone bridge has 17 arches, supposedly one for each letter of Napoléon Bonaparte. It's now closed to private cars and is the best place to see the long curve of the quays. Walk across around sunset, free, 30 minutes there and back.

Things to do in Bordeaux

The city rewards doing, not just looking. These are the things we'd put on the list:

  • Have oysters and white wine for breakfast at the Capucins or the Chartrons Sunday market. It's the most Bordeaux thing you can do for under €15.
  • Take a wine class or tasting in town rather than an expensive château tour. The École du Vin de Bordeaux runs short, affordable sessions, and plenty of wine bars do tasting flights.
  • Hire a bike along the quays. The riverside path runs for kilometres, and the city's V3 bike share makes this cheap.
  • Cross to the Rive Droite on the BatCub river shuttle, part of the TBM transport network. Walk the right-bank gardens for the best view back at the Bourse facades.
  • Spend an afternoon at Darwin, a former military barracks on the right bank turned into an eco-hub with a skate park, street art, organic canteen and craft beer. It's free to wander.
  • Watch the Pont Chaban-Delmas lift if a large ship is passing. The central span rises vertically and it's an odd, satisfying sight.
  • Do an apéro on the quays. On summer evenings everyone sits on the grass along Quai des Chartrons with a bottle. Join them.

Bordeaux for free (or almost)

Most of the best of Bordeaux costs nothing. The Miroir d'Eau, the quays, the cathedral, the Jardin Public, the Chartrons streets, the Saint-Michel flea market and Darwin are all free. Walking from the Pont de Pierre to the Cité du Vin along the river is one of the great free walks in France, about 45 minutes one way.

Municipal museums are free on the first Sunday of each month. Oysters at the market, a canelé from a bakery and a glass of wine on a terrace keep food costs low without cheating yourself. If you're staying central, you barely need public transport.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Rue Sainte-Catherine. A kilometre of high-street chains. Walk it once, then shop on Rue Notre-Dame in Chartrons or the small streets of Saint-Pierre.
  • Expensive château tours from the city. Full-day Médoc minibus tours often run €100-150 and spend hours on the road. Saint-Émilion by train is cheaper and more fun.
  • The queue at L'Entrecôte. The steak-frites-and-secret-sauce place is a decent meal, but not worth an hour on the pavement. Order entrecôte bordelaise at a regular bistro instead.
  • Cité du Vin if you hate museums. It's long and screen-heavy. If you mainly want to drink good wine, spend the €22 on a tasting flight at a wine bar.

When to visit Bordeaux

The best time is May to June and September to October: warm, long days, terraces open, and in autumn the vineyards are harvesting. July and August are hot, often well over 30°C, and busier, though locals partly leave for the coast.

Winter is mild but grey and damp, with the Miroir d'Eau switched off and some châteaux closed. It is, however, the cheapest time for flights and hotels. Book well ahead for late June in odd-numbered years, when the Bordeaux Fête le Vin wine festival takes over the quays, and for Vinexpo trade-fair weeks, when hotel prices spike.

Where to stay in Bordeaux: best areas

  • Saint-Pierre / Hôtel de Ville: the old town, walkable to everything. Best for first-timers and short stays. About €110-200 a night for a double.
  • Chartrons: quieter, residential and full of good places to eat. Great for couples and repeat visitors. About €90-170.
  • Triangle d'Or (Grand Théâtre / Quinconces): grand and central, with the smartest hotels. About €150-300+.
  • Saint-Michel / Victoire: cheaper, younger, livelier, slightly rougher at night. Good for budget travellers. About €60-110.
  • Gare Saint-Jean: convenient for early trains and day trips, but less charming. About €70-120, and tram C takes you into town in 10 minutes.

What to eat in Bordeaux

Bordeaux food is meaty, buttery and built around the wine. Start with these:

  • Canelé: a small, caramelised rum-and-vanilla cake, crunchy outside and custardy inside. Baillardran is everywhere, but independent bakeries are often better. About €1.50-2 each.
  • Entrecôte à la bordelaise: rib steak with a red wine and shallot sauce, sometimes with bone marrow.
  • Arcachon oysters with crépinettes (little grilled sausages), an odd but excellent local pairing.
  • Lamproie à la bordelaise: lamprey stewed in red wine. An acquired taste, so order it if you're curious, not hungry.
  • Dune blanche: a cream-filled choux pastry from the Cap Ferret area, now sold in town too.

Lunch is the bargain: a formule (set menu) of two courses runs about €16-22 on weekdays. A good dinner with a glass of wine costs €35-55 a head. A glass of decent Bordeaux in a bar is €5-9. Order local wines by the glass from smaller appellations (Côtes de Bordeaux, Entre-Deux-Mers, Fronsac) rather than paying for big names.

Airport to city centre and getting around

Bordeaux-Mérignac airport is about 12 km west of the centre. Your options:

  • Tram A: runs from the airport to the centre in about 40-45 minutes for the price of a standard ticket (around €2). The cheapest option.
  • 30'Direct shuttle bus: to the Gare Saint-Jean via the centre, about 30-40 minutes, around €8-9.
  • Taxi: €40-60 depending on traffic and time, around 25-40 minutes.

Arriving by train, the Gare Saint-Jean is on tram C, about 10 minutes from the centre. Paris is roughly 2 hours by TGV.

In town, the TBM network covers trams (A, B, C, D), buses and the BatCub river shuttles. A single ticket is about €2, and day passes and multi-day tickets are good value if you'll ride more than twice. Validate every time you board, because inspectors fine on the spot. The Bordeaux CityPass (from about €35 for 24 hours) bundles transport and museums and only pays off if you pack in several paid sights.

How much a trip to Bordeaux costs

  • Shoestring: €60-80 a day. Hostel or budget room (€30-60 per person), market oysters and bakery lunches, free sights, walking.
  • Mid-range: €140-220 a day per person. Central hotel (€110-180 per room), set lunches, a bistro dinner with wine, one or two paid attractions.
  • Extras: Cité du Vin about €22, Saint-Émilion return train about €20-25, a château tasting €15-40.

Day trips from Bordeaux

Saint-Émilion

A medieval hilltop village surrounded by vineyards, with an underground monolithic church. The regional train takes around 35-40 minutes to Saint-Émilion station, then it's a 20-minute walk uphill through vines. Allow a full day.

Arcachon and the Dune du Pilat

The seaside town is about an hour by train, and Europe's tallest sand dune is a short bus ride beyond. Go early in summer, as the dune gets hot and crowded by midday. A full day.

The Médoc

The road of grand châteaux like Margaux and Pauillac. It needs a car or a tour, since public transport is poor. Book tastings in advance, as many châteaux are appointment-only.

Cap Ferret

Oyster shacks and dunes on the other side of the Arcachon basin. Reach it by ferry from Arcachon. Best as a long day in good weather.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Not validating your tram ticket. Fines are immediate and inspectors are common.
  • Turning up at châteaux unannounced. Most need booking, and many close at weekends or in winter.
  • Eating on the main squares. Place du Parlement and around the Bourse charge for the view. Walk one street back.
  • Pickpockets on crowded trams and around Rue Sainte-Catherine and the station. Keep phones out of back pockets.
  • Petition and bracelet hustlers near the Bourse and Saint-Michel. Keep walking.
  • Visiting the Capucins market on a weekday afternoon. You'll find it shut or half-empty.

Bordeaux FAQ

How many days do you need in Bordeaux?

Three days is ideal: two for the city and one for a day trip to Saint-Émilion or Arcachon. A weekend covers the centre only.

Is Bordeaux worth visiting if you don't drink wine?

Yes. The architecture, riverfront, markets and food are reason enough, and the coast is an hour away.

Is Bordeaux walkable?

Very. It's flat, much of the centre is pedestrianised, and the tram fills any gaps.

How do I get from Bordeaux airport to the city centre?

Tram A is the cheapest at around €2 and 40-45 minutes. The 30'Direct shuttle costs about €8-9, and a taxi €40-60.

Can you visit Saint-Émilion without a car?

Yes. Trains run from Bordeaux in around 35-40 minutes, then it's a 20-minute walk to the village.

What is Bordeaux famous for food-wise?

Canelés, entrecôte bordelaise and Arcachon oysters, all ideally with local wine.

Is Bordeaux expensive?

It's cheaper than Paris but not cheap. Set lunches and markets keep costs reasonable.

Is Bordeaux safe?

Generally yes. Watch for pickpockets in crowded spots and be a little careful around Saint-Michel and the station late at night.
