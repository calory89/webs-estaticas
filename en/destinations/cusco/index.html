Cusco is the old Inca capital, rebuilt by the Spanish on top of Inca walls, and sitting at about 3,400 metres. You will feel that altitude on the first staircase. The centre is compact and very walkable, though steep. It is also very touristy around the Plaza de Armas, where touts sell massages, tours and menus every ten metres. Step a few blocks away, into San Blas, the San Pedro market or the streets behind Santa Ana, and it becomes a working Andean city again.

It suits history fans, hikers and anyone heading to Machu Picchu. It is less ideal for people who struggle with altitude or want beaches and nightlife comfort. Give the city and the Sacred Valley 4 days. Day one is for acclimatising, days two and three for the city and the valley, and day four for a big day trip. Machu Picchu needs one or two extra days on top of that.

What to see in Cusco: the places worth your time

Plaza de Armas

The main square is the obvious starting point and worth an hour, ideally twice: once in the morning and once lit up at night. The Cathedral and the Iglesia de la Compañía face each other across the gardens. It is free. Our tip: watch it from a balcony café on the upper floors around the square, then eat anywhere else.

Cusco Cathedral

The Cathedral is heavy and gold-laden, and it is the best place in the city to understand the Cusco School of painting. Look for the Last Supper with a guinea pig on the plate. Allow an hour. Entry is about €10 (S/40), sold as part of the religious circuit ticket. No photos are allowed inside, so do not bother arguing with the guards.

Qorikancha (Convento de Santo Domingo)

This was the Incas' most important temple, once lined with gold, with a Dominican convent built on top. It is the clearest example in Cusco of two empires stacked on each other, and the precision of the Inca stonework is startling up close. You need about an hour. Entry is about €5 (S/20), and a guide at the door is worth it. Go first thing, before the tour groups arrive.

Sacsayhuamán

Sacsayhuamán is a huge Inca fortress above the city, built with zig-zag walls of stones weighing well over 100 tonnes. It is the single most impressive site in Cusco itself. Plan two hours. It is covered by the Boleto Turístico (general ticket about €32, S/130). Walk up via San Cristóbal church, which takes 30 steep minutes, and take a taxi back if your lungs disagree.

San Blas

San Blas is the artisan quarter: whitewashed walls, blue doors, steep lanes, workshops and small cafés. It is the nicest neighbourhood to wander. Allow half a day if you like browsing. The church of San Blas has an extraordinary carved pulpit and a small entry fee. The view from the little plaza at sunset is free and excellent.

Hatun Rumiyoc and the Twelve-Angled Stone

This is a street of original Inca wall running from the Plaza towards San Blas, and it includes the famous twelve-angled stone. It takes ten minutes and costs nothing. Expect a man dressed as an Inca king to pose next to it for tips. The stone is worth seeing, but the whole wall is more impressive than the single block everyone queues for.

Mercado San Pedro

San Pedro is the central market: juice stalls, soup counters, cheese, bread rounds, frog shakes and dried llama foetuses. Go for breakfast and spend an hour. A fresh juice costs about €1-1.50 (S/5-6) and refills come with it. Watch your bag and keep your phone in your front pocket, because it is a known pickpocket zone.

Museo de Arte Precolombino (MAP)

MAP is a small, beautifully curated museum of pre-Inca and Inca objects in a colonial mansion on Plaza de las Nazarenas. It takes about an hour, costs around €5 (S/20), and gives the context most visitors skip. Its courtyard restaurant is a calm spot for a coffee afterwards.

Museo Inka

Museo Inka is a more traditional museum run by the university, just up from the Plaza. It has mummies, ceramics and textiles, with weavers often working in the courtyard. Plan an hour; entry is about €5 (S/20). It is less polished than MAP but better on the Inca period itself.

Q'enqo, Puka Pukara and Tambomachay

These are three smaller Inca sites on the road north of Sacsayhuamán. Q'enqo has a carved rock shrine, and Tambomachay has Inca water channels. All three are on the Boleto Turístico. Allow half a day. Our tip: take a taxi to Tambomachay (about €5) and walk back downhill past all of them to Sacsayhuamán and the city.

Cristo Blanco viewpoint

Cristo Blanco is a white statue of Christ on the hill beside Sacsayhuamán with the best panoramic view of Cusco. It is free and takes 20 minutes once you are up there. Go late afternoon and combine it with Sacsayhuamán. Do not walk down alone after dark.

Convento de Santa Catalina

Santa Catalina is a working convent with a museum of religious art and a glimpse into cloistered life. It is quieter than the big churches and part of the religious circuit. An hour is enough. It suits you if you liked the Cathedral; skip it if one gilded altar was plenty.

Things to do in Cusco

The sightseeing is only half of Cusco. These are the things we would make time for:

  • Take a cooking class: most include a San Pedro market tour and a pisco sour lesson. They cost about €35-50 and are better value than a fancy dinner.
  • Drink coca tea: every hotel offers it free and it helps with mild altitude symptoms. Coca leaves are legal here, but do not carry them home.
  • Visit a weaving cooperative: the Centro de Textiles Tradicionales del Cusco on Avenida El Sol has fair prices, and the money reaches the weavers.
  • Catch a folk dance show: the Centro Qosqo de Arte Nativo is included in the Boleto Turístico and runs nightly.
  • Eat chicharrón on Calle Pampa del Castillo: a street of chicharronerías serving fried pork with mote and mint. It is a proper local lunch.
  • Do a chocolate workshop: ChocoMuseo near Plaza San Francisco runs bean-to-bar sessions. They are touristy but fun on a rainy afternoon.
  • Climb to the San Cristóbal viewpoint at sunset: it is closer than Cristo Blanco and has a great angle over the Plaza.

Cusco for free (or almost)

A lot of Cusco costs nothing, because the Inca walls are simply part of the streets.

  • Walk Calle Loreto and Hatun Rumiyoc for original Inca stonework.
  • Watch Plaza de Armas at night, and Sunday morning flag ceremonies when they happen.
  • Take in the views from San Cristóbal, San Blas plaza and Cristo Blanco.
  • Join a free walking tour from the Plaza, then tip what it was worth (S/20-30 is fair).
  • Browse Mercado San Pedro without buying, though the juice is hard to resist.
  • Walk up the steps of Calle Siete Borreguitos and around San Cristóbal.
  • Visit the Plaza de San Francisco and the Plaza Regocijo gardens.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Rainbow Mountain at peak hours: the colours are real, but so are the 3am pickup, hundreds of people and a 5,000-metre ridge. Palcoyo is a less crowded, easier alternative with similar striped hills.
  • Restaurants on the Plaza de Armas: you pay for the view and touts. Walk two blocks to Calle Plateros, San Blas or the market.
  • A night in Aguas Calientes for its own sake: it is a concrete town built for Machu Picchu traffic. Sleep in Ollantaytambo and take an early train instead.
  • Buying "baby alpaca" on the street: most of it is synthetic or llama. Buy real alpaca from cooperatives or established shops, and expect to pay properly for it.

When to visit Cusco

The best weather is in the dry season, May to September: sunny days around 18-20°C and nights near or below freezing. It is also peak season, with June to August the busiest and most expensive, and Inti Raymi on 24 June filling every bed.

The rainy season runs December to March, with afternoon downpours, muddy trails and the Inca Trail closed in February. It is cheaper and greener, and hotels drop prices. Our pick is April or October to early November: decent weather, fewer people and lower rates. Pack layers whatever month you go, because sun at noon and frost at night happen on the same day.

Where to stay in Cusco: best areas

  • Historic centre (around the Plaza de Armas): the most convenient for first-timers and flat enough on arrival day. Expect noise. Hostels cost €12-20, mid-range hotels €50-100.
  • San Blas: charming, quieter and full of cafés, but steep. It suits couples and anyone staying a while. Guesthouses run €35-80, boutique hotels €100+.
  • Calle Plateros and Procuradores: backpacker central with bars and cheap tours. It is good for social travellers and bad for light sleepers. Dorms cost €10-15.
  • San Cristóbal: above the Plaza, with views and calm streets, close to Sacsayhuamán. Mid-range stays run €40-90.
  • Avenida El Sol area: flatter, more local and close to the bus routes towards the airport. Business-style hotels cost €45-90.

Whatever the area, check whether the room has a heater. Many places do not, and Cusco nights are cold.

What to eat in Cusco

Cusco food is hearty highland cooking, with plenty of potatoes, corn, pork and soup. Order these:

  • Cuy al horno: roast guinea pig. It is festive food and expensive in tourist restaurants (€15-25). Try it once in a picantería.
  • Chicharrón con mote: fried pork with big-grain corn, best on Calle Pampa del Castillo.
  • Adobo cusqueño: a pork stew eaten as a Sunday breakfast. Look for it in local spots, not hotels.
  • Rocoto relleno: a stuffed spicy pepper, really from Arequipa but everywhere here.
  • Trucha: fresh Andean trout, cheap and good.
  • Lomo saltado: stir-fried beef with chips. It is the safe option, and still excellent.
  • Chiri uchu: a cold Corpus Christi platter, served mostly in June.
  • To drink: chicha morada (purple corn), pisco sour and Cusqueña beer.

A menú del día (soup, main and drink) in a local place costs €2.50-4 (S/10-15). The market is cheaper still. A mid-range dinner with a drink runs €15-25 per person. Upmarket restaurants like Chicha by Gastón Acurio or Cicciolina cost €30-50. Go easy on alcohol the first two days, because altitude doubles its effect.

Airport to city centre and getting around

Alejandro Velasco Astete airport is only about 15-20 minutes from the Plaza de Armas. These are the options:

  • Official airport taxi: book at the desks inside arrivals. It costs about €8-12 (S/30-45) and is the easiest choice.
  • Taxi from outside the airport gate: cheaper at about €4-6 (S/15-25), but agree the price first.
  • Hotel transfer: about €10-15. It is worth it if you arrive at night.
  • Ride apps: Uber and similar apps work in Cusco, with similar prices to street taxis.

In the centre you walk everywhere. Taxis inside the city cost €2-4 (S/8-15). There is no metro or useful travel pass for transport. The Boleto Turístico is the pass that matters: about €32 (S/130) for 10 days covers Sacsayhuamán, the Sacred Valley sites, museums and the dance show. Partial tickets cost about €18 (S/70). For the Sacred Valley, colectivos and shared vans run cheaply (€1-3), towards Pisac from around Calle Puputi and towards Urubamba and Ollantaytambo from Calle Pavitos.

Day trips from Cusco

Machu Picchu

Take a colectivo or taxi to Ollantaytambo (about 1.5 hours), the train to Aguas Calientes (about 1.5 hours), then the bus up to the site. It is doable in one very long day. Two days is better. Book tickets weeks ahead in high season, as they sell out by circuit and time slot.

Sacred Valley: Pisac and Ollantaytambo

Pisac is about 1 hour by colectivo and Ollantaytambo about 1.5-2 hours. Both have major Inca ruins covered by the Boleto Turístico. Doing them independently is easy and cheaper than a tour.

Maras and Moray

These are the salt pans and the circular agricultural terraces, about 1-1.5 hours away. A taxi for the day or a half-day tour (€15-25) is easiest. The salt pans charge a separate entry of a few euros.

Humantay Lake

Humantay is a turquoise glacial lake below a snow peak. It means about 3 hours' drive plus a steep 1.5-hour hike to around 4,200 metres. Tours cost €30-45 with very early starts. Only go once you are acclimatised.

Rainbow Mountain (Vinicunca) or Palcoyo

Both are about 3 hours each way, and Vinicunca adds a hard 1.5-2 hour hike. Tours cost €30-45. Palcoyo is the gentler, quieter option.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Doing too much on day one: altitude sickness ruins more trips than anything else. Rest, hydrate and ask a pharmacy about acetazolamide before you fly if you are worried.
  • Photos with women and baby llamas: it is fine, but they expect a tip (S/5-10). Agree first, because some will ask for more afterwards.
  • Cheap tour touts on the Plaza: some sell trips that never materialise or cut corners on safety. Book through your hotel or a well-reviewed agency with an office.
  • Counterfeit notes and coins: check S/100 and S/50 notes, and refuse ones that look worn or odd. Use ATMs inside banks.
  • Unmarked taxis at night: use an app or ask a restaurant to call one.
  • Leaving Machu Picchu tickets too late: July and August sell out, and train seats too.
  • Massage touts on Calle Procuradores: harmless but persistent. A firm "no, gracias" works.

Cusco FAQ

How many days do you need in Cusco?

Four days covers the city, the Sacred Valley and one big day trip, including acclimatising. Add one or two more for Machu Picchu.

Is Cusco safe for tourists?

Generally yes, with normal city care. Pickpocketing in markets and crowded spots is the main risk, plus unmarked taxis late at night.

How do you avoid altitude sickness in Cusco?

Take it slow on day one, drink plenty of water and coca tea, and skip alcohol early on. Some people start in the Sacred Valley, which is lower, and come up to Cusco afterwards.

Is the Boleto Turístico worth it?

Yes, if you plan to see Sacsayhuamán and the Sacred Valley ruins, because buying entries separately is not really possible for most sites. It does not include the Cathedral, Qorikancha or Machu Picchu.

Can you visit Machu Picchu as a day trip from Cusco?

Yes, but it is a 14-16 hour day. Sleeping in Ollantaytambo or Aguas Calientes makes it far more relaxed.

What is the best month to visit Cusco?

May and September offer dry weather with slightly smaller crowds than June to August. April and October are good value shoulder months.

Do I need cash in Cusco?

Yes. Markets, colectivos, small restaurants and tips are cash only in soles. Cards work in hotels and mid-range restaurants.

What to see in Cusco in one day?

If you only have one day, follow day 1 of this itinerary: Arrive slowly. Morning: land, check in, drink coca tea and do nothing for two hours.

What to see in Cusco in two days?

With two days, follow days 1 and 2 of this itinerary. Day 1: Arrive slowly. Morning: land, check in, drink coca tea and do nothing for two hours. Day 2: Centre, San Blas and Sacsayhuamán. Morning: breakfast at San Pedro market, then the Cathedral and MAP.
