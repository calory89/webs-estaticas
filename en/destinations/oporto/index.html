Porto city guide: what to see, eat and where to stay in 3 days

🔥 1 deal to Porto🏨 Hotels in Porto

Why go to Porto

Porto is a city of granite, tiles and hills, and it rewards people who walk. The old centre drops steeply to the Douro river, the façades are covered in blue-and-white azulejos, and the light at six in the evening turns the whole riverbank gold. It is smaller and less polished than Lisbon, and most visitors count that in its favour.

It is also good value by Western European standards. A solid lunch with wine still costs less than a sandwich in some capitals, and the food is hearty, unfussy and very good.

What to see in Porto

Ribeira and the Dom Luís I Bridge

The riverfront quarter is the postcard, and it deserves the crowds. Go early, before ten, when the arcades are quiet. Then walk across the upper deck of the Dom Luís I Bridge, an iron double-decker with the best view of the city. Mind the trams and the drop.

São Bento station

Walk into the entrance hall and look up. Some 20,000 azulejo tiles tell scenes of Portuguese history. It is free, it is a working station, and ten minutes is enough.

Livraria Lello

The neo-Gothic bookshop with the red staircase has a ticket system and queues. It is lovely, but small. Book a slot online or skip it if the line is long; the Clérigos church next door is quieter.

Torre dos Clérigos

Climb the 225 steps of this baroque tower for a rooftop panorama of terracotta roofs and the river. Late afternoon is best for the light.

Palácio da Bolsa

The former stock exchange is visited by guided tour only. The Arab Room, covered in gilded stucco, justifies the ticket on its own.

Sé do Porto (cathedral)

A fortress-like Romanesque cathedral on a hill, with a tiled cloister. The square outside gives you another angle over the old town.

Port cellars in Vila Nova de Gaia

Cross the bridge to the south bank, where the big names (Graham's, Taylor's, Sandeman, Cálem) age their wine. Tours usually end with a tasting of two or three ports. Pick one big house and one smaller producer rather than cramming in five.

Serralves

A contemporary art museum with an Art Deco villa and a large park. It is a good rainy-day plan and a break from the cobbles.

Mercado do Bolhão

The historic market, renovated and reopened in 2022, sells fish, cheese, flowers and fruit. Go in the morning for the real market and not the lunch crowd.

Foz do Douro

Where the river meets the Atlantic. Take the historic tram along the water (line 1), walk the promenade and watch the waves hit the breakwater. It feels like a seaside town inside the city.

Things to do

  • Take a short river cruise under the "six bridges". It is touristy but pleasant at sunset.
  • Ride the Teleférico de Gaia cable car, or the Funicular dos Guindais if you want to skip the climb back up from Ribeira.
  • Sit on a terrace in Rua das Flores or Miragaia with a glass of white port and tonic, the local summer drink.
  • Watch the sunset from the Jardim do Morro in Gaia, where locals gather with a drink.
  • Go to a Fado or live music evening in a small bar around Ribeira.
  • Browse the shops and cafés around Cedofeita and Rua Miguel Bombarda, the gallery and design street.

How many days and a suggested route

Two days covers the essentials. Three is better, and gives you time to slow down.

Day 1: São Bento station, the cathedral, down to Ribeira, across the bridge to Gaia for a cellar visit, and sunset at the Jardim do Morro.

Day 2: Bolhão market in the morning, Clérigos, Livraria Lello and the Baixa, then the Palácio da Bolsa. Dinner in Cedofeita.

Day 3: Tram to Foz, a long walk on the seafront, then Serralves in the afternoon. Or take a day trip: the Douro Valley by train or boat, or Guimarães and Braga.

When to go

Spring (April to June) and early autumn (September, early October) are the best balance of mild weather and manageable crowds. Summer is warm, around 25 to 28°C, usually with an Atlantic breeze. It is also the busiest and most expensive time to visit. Winters are mild but wet and grey, with plenty of rain from November to February, so pack a proper jacket.

The São João festival on the night of 23 to 24 June is the city's big party, with street grills, plastic hammers and fireworks over the river. Book accommodation far ahead if you want to be there.

Where to stay

Baixa and Ribeira: best for first-timers who want to walk everywhere. Expect noise at weekends and steep hills.

Cedofeita and Miguel Bombarda: a more local feel, with cafés, galleries and good restaurants. A good mid-range choice.

Vila Nova de Gaia: quieter, near the cellars, with river views back towards the old town.

Foz do Douro: for beach and sea air, but further from the sights.

Budget travellers will find hostels in the centre from about 20 to 35 euros for a dorm bed. Mid-range hotels and apartments often start around 80 to 130 euros a night. Upscale riverside hotels cost considerably more, especially in summer.

What to eat and where

The signature dish is the francesinha, a sandwich of cured meats and steak, covered in melted cheese and a beer-and-tomato sauce, and often topped with a fried egg. It is enormous. Share one if you want to see anything afterwards. Cervejaria Brasão and Café Santiago are two well-known places to try it.

Also look for tripas à moda do Porto, a tripe and bean stew that locals take pride in, and bacalhau in its many versions. For a quick lunch, a bifana (pork sandwich) with a small beer is hard to beat. Grilled sardines in summer are excellent, and Matosinhos, a short metro ride from the centre, is the place for fish restaurants where you pick your fish by weight.

For coffee and cake, the grand Café Majestic on Rua de Santa Catarina is beautiful but pricey. Locals are more likely to be at a small pastelaria, ordering a pastel de nata and a bica (espresso) standing at the counter.

Getting from the airport and getting around

Francisco Sá Carneiro Airport is about 11 km from the centre. The simplest option is the metro Line E (violet), which runs to the centre in around 30 minutes for a few euros. Taxis and ride-hailing apps take 20 to 30 minutes and cost roughly 20 to 30 euros depending on the time of day.

The centre is compact but hilly, and comfortable shoes matter more than anything else you pack. Metro do Porto covers the wider area, and the Andante card is the rechargeable ticket for metro, buses and some trains. Heritage trams such as lines 1 and 22 are fun but not fast. Trains from São Bento and Campanhã go to the Douro Valley, Braga and Guimarães.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: about 50 to 70 euros, with a hostel, market lunches and free sights.
  • Mid-range: about 100 to 160 euros, with a private room, sit-down meals and one or two paid visits.
  • Comfortable: 200 euros and up, with a nice hotel, tasting menus and private tours.

These are rough figures and vary with the season.

FAQs

Is Porto walkable?

Yes, the historic centre is compact, but the hills are steep. Use the metro, funicular or tram to save your legs.

How many days do you need in Porto?

Two days for the highlights, three for a relaxed visit, or four if you want a day trip to the Douro Valley.

Is Porto safe for tourists?

Generally very safe. Watch for pickpockets in crowded areas like Ribeira, and keep your bag closed on trams.

Do I need cash?

Cards are widely accepted, but a little cash helps in small cafés and markets.

What is the best time to visit Porto?

May, June and September give you pleasant weather and fewer crowds than July and August.
