Why go to Helsinki

Helsinki is compact, calm and a little odd, and that is the appeal. It is a capital where you can swim in the sea after a sauna, then be back in a design shop within twenty minutes. Neoclassical squares sit next to Art Nouveau facades and some very confident modern architecture. Nobody rushes you.

It is also an easy add-on if you are flying across Europe or the Atlantic, and the Baltic ferries to Tallinn make a day trip simple. Just know that Finland is not cheap. Plan for it and the city is a pleasure.

What to see in Helsinki

Suomenlinna

An 18th-century sea fortress spread over several islands, ten to fifteen minutes by ferry from Market Square. Bring a picnic, walk the ramparts and leave time to get lost. Go late in the afternoon when the day-trippers thin out.

Senate Square and Helsinki Cathedral

The white cathedral on its steps is the postcard image. Climb up, take a look at the square and then head inside, which is surprisingly plain. It is the best orientation point in town.

Uspenski Cathedral

The red-brick Orthodox cathedral on Katajanokka feels like a different country from the Lutheran one just across the water. Gold domes outside, dark and glittering inside.

Temppeliaukio Church

The "Rock Church" is carved into solid granite in Töölö, with a copper dome and daylight coming through a ring of windows. It gets busy at midday, so aim for early morning.

Oodi Central Library

Opened in 2018 opposite Parliament. Locals really use it: sewing machines, 3D printers, a cinema and a rooftop terrace. It is free and tells you a lot about how Finns think about public space.

Kiasma and Ateneum

Kiasma is the contemporary art museum, a curved building near the station. Ateneum, a short walk away, holds classic Finnish painting, including the Golden Age landscapes that explain the national mood. Pick one if you are short on time.

Helsinki Central Station

Eliel Saarinen's granite station, with its stone giants holding lamps, is worth a few minutes even if you are not catching a train.

Design District and Punavuori

A scatter of studios, vintage shops and showrooms around Fredrikinkatu and Uudenmaankatu. The Design Museum is a good starting point. Window-shopping is free, buying is another matter.

Market Square and the Old Market Hall

The harbour-side market is touristy but still pleasant for coffee and a look at the boats. The Old Market Hall (Vanha Kauppahalli) next door, from 1889, is better for lunch.

Things to do

  • Sauna, properly. Löyly, on the waterfront in Hernesaari, combines wood-fired saunas with a plunge in the Baltic. Kulttuurisauna in Hakaniemi is smaller and more local. Allas Sea Pool, by the harbour, has pools and saunas with a view of the cathedral.
  • Cycle or walk Töölönlahti and the park paths towards the Sibelius Monument, a forest of steel pipes that looks better than it sounds.
  • Visit Seurasaari, an island open-air museum with old wooden farmsteads, especially nice around Midsummer.
  • Browse Kallio, the former working-class district, now full of cheap bars, record shops and second-hand stores.
  • Take a ferry to Tallinn for the day if you have a spare one. It is about two hours each way.

Helsinki itinerary: 3 days, day by day

Two full days cover the highlights, three let you slow down. With a long weekend:

3-day itinerary for Helsinki
DayPlan
Day 1Central Station, Oodi, Kiasma or Ateneum, Senate Square and the cathedral, lunch in the Old Market Hall, Uspenski in the afternoon, then an evening sauna at Allas or Löyly.
Day 2Morning ferry to Suomenlinna, a long lunch on the island, then back to Punavuori for design shops and dinner.
Day 3Temppeliaukio Church, the Sibelius Monument, a coffee stop at Regatta in Töölö, and an afternoon in Kallio.

When to go

June to August is the easy choice: highs around 18 to 22°C and daylight until nearly midnight. Around Midsummer (late June) many Finns leave for the lakes and the city goes very quiet, so some places close. May and September are cheaper, calmer and still pleasant, with autumn colour in the parks.

December brings the Christmas market on Senate Square and lights everywhere. January to March is cold, often around -5°C, with snow and a frozen harbour. If you like winter and saunas, it is great. Do not count on northern lights here. They are rare this far south.

Where to stay

  • Kamppi and the centre: the most practical base, close to trains, trams and the airport link. Good for a first visit, and the most expensive.
  • Punavuori and the Design District: restaurants, cafés and shops on your doorstep. Slightly more atmospheric than the centre.
  • Kallio: best value for bars and food, a little rougher around the edges, ten minutes by metro or tram from the centre. My pick for budget travellers.
  • Töölö: quiet, leafy and residential. Good if you want calm.
  • Katajanokka: near the harbour, beautiful architecture, good for ferry connections.

Hostels are the cheapest option, with beds typically from 30 to 50 euros. Mid-range hotels usually run from 100 to 170 euros a night, and top-end places around the cathedral and Esplanadi go well above that.

What to eat and where

Start with lohikeitto, creamy salmon soup, in the Old Market Hall or at one of the stalls on Market Square. Try karjalanpiirakka, a rye pastry filled with rice porridge and topped with egg butter, and korvapuusti, a cinnamon bun that deserves its reputation. Grilled Baltic herring (silakka) is a market classic, and poronkäristys, sautéed reindeer with mashed potato and lingonberries, is the traditional main course.

Coffee matters here. Finns drink more of it than almost anyone, so a good café is never far. Try Fazer Café for chocolate and pastries, or Regatta in Töölö for a cinnamon bun by the water. The Hakaniemi Market Hall is a more local alternative to the Old Market Hall. For evening, Kallio and Punavuori have the best choice, from cheap lunch deals to tasting menus. Lunch menus on weekdays are usually the best value in the city.

Getting from the airport and getting around

Helsinki-Vantaa is about 18 km north of the centre. The simplest option is the I and P trains to Central Station, around 30 minutes. A ticket valid for zones ABC costs a few euros and also works on trams, buses, the metro and the Suomenlinna ferry. Buy it in the HSL app. Bus 615 and the Finnair City Bus are alternatives. A taxi costs roughly 40 to 60 euros.

The centre is easy on foot. For the rest, the tram network is the best way to see the city. Lines 2 and 3 are good sightseeing routes. The metro covers longer distances toward Kallio and Hakaniemi. In summer, renting one of the city bikes is a good option.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: about 80 to 110 euros, with a hostel, market lunch and supermarket dinner.
  • Mid-range: about 150 to 220 euros, with a hotel, a couple of restaurant meals and a sauna.
  • Comfortable: 300 euros and up, with a central hotel and dinner out with drinks.

Alcohol is expensive, so drinks add up quickly. Tap water is excellent, and you can tip lightly or not at all.

FAQ

Is three days enough for Helsinki?

Yes. Three days covers the main sights, a sauna and an island trip without hurrying. Two works if you are selective.

Is Helsinki expensive?

Yes, compared with southern Europe. Hotels, restaurants and alcohol cost more. Lunch deals, hostels and free sights like Oodi and the cathedral help a lot.

Do I need cash?

Almost never. Cards and phone payments are accepted nearly everywhere, even at small market stalls.

Do people speak English?

Yes, very widely. Finnish and Swedish are the official languages, and you will see both on signs, but English works everywhere in tourist areas.

Is it safe?

Very. Normal city precautions are enough, though you should keep an eye on pickpockets around the station and on trams in busy periods.
