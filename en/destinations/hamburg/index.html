Why go to Hamburg

Hamburg is Germany's second city, and it doesn't try to be Berlin or Munich. It is a port first and everything else second. Cranes, brick warehouses, ferries that count as normal public transport, and a rough-edged nightlife district a few minutes from a very polished lake. Locals are reserved at first and then quietly funny.

It also works well for a short, cheap trip. Flights are frequent, the centre is compact, and one transport ticket covers trains, buses and harbour ferries.

What to see in Hamburg

Elbphilharmonie and its Plaza

The concert hall sits on top of an old warehouse on the Elbe. You don't need a concert ticket: the public Plaza, a viewing terrace halfway up, is free (check the current booking rules on the official site). Go at sunset, when the harbour turns copper.

Speicherstadt and HafenCity

The world's largest warehouse district is a forest of red brick, canals and iron bridges. It's best early in the morning or after dark, when it's lit up and nearly empty. Next door, HafenCity is the new, glassy neighbourhood. It's interesting, though a little sterile.

Miniatur Wunderland

Yes, it's a model railway. It's also one of the most absurdly detailed things you'll see in Europe, with airports that actually work and whole countries in miniature. Book ahead and avoid weekends if you can.

Landungsbrücken and the Old Elbe Tunnel

The floating piers are where the ferries leave. Beside them, a 1911 tunnel under the river (the Alter Elbtunnel) takes you to the south bank on old tiled lifts. The view of the skyline from the other side is the reason to go.

St. Michaelis (the Michel)

Hamburg's baroque church is the city's emblem. Climb or take the lift up the tower for a wide look over the port and the centre.

Kontorhaus District and Chilehaus

This UNESCO-listed area of brick office buildings from the 1920s is easy to miss. The Chilehaus, shaped like a ship's prow, is the showpiece. Photograph it from the corner at Fischertwiete.

Rathaus and the Alster

The neo-Renaissance City Hall faces the Binnenalster, the smaller of the two city lakes. Walk the Jungfernstieg promenade, then continue north along the Außenalster. Locals jog, sail and drink beer here, depending on the hour.

Reeperbahn and St. Pauli

The famous red-light strip is louder and tackier than its reputation, but the surrounding streets have good bars and live music. It's worth a late-evening walk. Keep your wits about you and your phone in your front pocket.

Schanzenviertel and Karoviertel

Independent shops, street art, cafés with mismatched chairs. It's the best part of town for an aimless afternoon.

Blankenese

A former fishing village on the western Elbe, with white houses and tiny stairways climbing the hillside. Take the S-Bahn out, then walk down to the beach and watch container ships pass close enough to read the names.

Things to do

  • Ride ferry line 62 from Landungsbrücken. It's included in a normal public transport ticket and gives you a cheap harbour cruise.
  • Visit the Fischmarkt in Altona on Sunday morning. It opens very early (around 5 am, a little later in winter) and wraps up by mid-morning. The fish is secondary to the atmosphere and the live band in the old fish auction hall.
  • Take a harbour tour from the piers if you want the big-ship view. The small-boat canal tours through the Speicherstadt are better.
  • Walk through Planten un Blomen, a long park near the centre. The water-light shows in summer evenings are a pleasant surprise.
  • Catch the Hamburg Dom, the big funfair on the Heiligengeistfeld. It runs three times a year, in spring, summer and winter.
  • Visit the Kunsthalle if it rains. It has a solid collection from the Middle Ages to the present, and it's next to the main station.

Hamburg itinerary: 3 days, day by day

Two days covers the headline sights. Three is the sweet spot, and four lets you add Blankenese and a slower pace.

3-day itinerary for Hamburg
DayPlan
Day 1Rathaus, Jungfernstieg, Kontorhaus District and Chilehaus, then Speicherstadt and Elbphilharmonie at sunset.
Day 2Michel, Landungsbrücken, the Old Elbe Tunnel, ferry 62, then dinner and drinks in St. Pauli.
Day 3Schanzenviertel in the morning, Miniatur Wunderland or Planten un Blomen after lunch, a last stroll by the Alster. On Sunday, swap in the Fischmarkt at dawn.

When to go

Hamburg's climate is maritime and changeable, so a rain jacket belongs in your bag year-round. May to September is the best window: long days, outdoor terraces, and highs around 20 to 24°C. July and August can be warmer, but a heatwave is not the norm.

Spring is fresh and often bright. Autumn is moody and cheaper. Winter is cold and grey, usually hovering between 0 and 5°C, but the Christmas markets (the Rathausmarkt one is the big one) make late November and December a decent time to visit. Wrap up warmly, since the wind off the Elbe cuts through.

Where to stay

St. Pauli and Landungsbrücken

Lively and close to the harbour. Good for nightlife and for hostels, but noisy on weekends.

Schanzenviertel and Karoviertel

The best choice if you want cafés, bars and a local feel. Mid-range hotels and apartments dominate.

City centre and around the Hauptbahnhof

Convenient for transport and first-time visits. The streets right around the station are a bit gritty, so look at the exact block. Streets towards the Alster feel nicer.

Altona and Ottensen

Calmer, residential and walkable, with good bakeries and fewer tourists. A smart pick for longer stays.

Budget: dorm beds roughly €25 to €40. Mid-range: €90 to €150 per night for a double. Top end: harbour or lake-view hotels from about €200 upwards. Prices jump during trade fairs and big events, so check dates.

What to eat and where

The signature quick bite is the Fischbrötchen, a bread roll with herring, smoked salmon or crab. Brücke 10, near the Landungsbrücken, is the well-known stop, and any harbour stall will do a decent one. Labskaus (a salty mash of corned beef, potato and beetroot, usually with a fried egg) is a sailor's dish that looks odd and tastes better than it looks.

Look for Pannfisch (pan-fried fish with mustard sauce) and Aalsuppe (a sweet-and-sour eel soup, with or without eel). For something sweet, try a Franzbrötchen, a flat cinnamon pastry, from any bakery, and Rote Grütze, red berry pudding, with cream or vanilla sauce.

For drinks, locals have a loyalty to Astra beer, the lager of St. Pauli. If someone offers you an Alsterwasser, it's a beer-and-lemonade shandy. Cheap eats are plentiful in Schanzenviertel and around Sternschanze station, including solid Turkish and Vietnamese spots.

Getting from the airport and getting around

Hamburg Airport (HAM) is connected by the S1 line, which goes straight to the Hauptbahnhof in roughly 25 minutes. It runs frequently and costs a few euros. A taxi into town takes about the same time but is considerably pricier.

Inside the city, the HVV network ties together U-Bahn, S-Bahn, buses and ferries. Day tickets are inexpensive and the Hamburg Card bundles transport with museum discounts. It pays off only if you plan several paid attractions. The centre is walkable, and bikes are easy to rent. Ticket machines take cards, though keep a little cash for small stalls.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget traveller: about €70 to €90 per day (dorm bed, bakery breakfast, street food, a day transport ticket).
  • Mid-range: about €130 to €190 (a decent hotel, sit-down dinner, one or two paid sights).
  • Comfortable: €220 and up, with a nicer hotel and restaurant dinners.

Many of the best things here cost nothing: the Plaza, the ferry rides on a transport ticket, the Speicherstadt walks and the Alster paths.

FAQs

Is Hamburg worth visiting for a weekend?

Yes. Two days handles the harbour, Speicherstadt, Elbphilharmonie and a night in St. Pauli without rushing.

Is Hamburg expensive?

It's mid-range for Germany, cheaper than London or Paris. Accommodation is the main cost, while food and transport are reasonable.

Is Hamburg safe for tourists?

Generally yes. Around the Hauptbahnhof and the Reeperbahn late at night, watch your belongings and avoid arguments with drunk groups.

Do I need German?

No. English is widely spoken in tourist areas, though a simple "Moin" (the northern hello, used all day) will get you a smile.

What is the best way to see the harbour?

Ferry line 62 from Landungsbrücken. It uses your normal transport ticket and gives you the skyline and the port from the water.

How many days do you need in Hamburg?

3 days are enough to see the essentials of Hamburg without rushing. The route in this guide is organised day by day.
