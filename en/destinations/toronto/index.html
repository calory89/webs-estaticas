Toronto is a city of neighbourhoods. The postcard skyline and the CN Tower are just the front door. The real city is a patchwork of districts like Kensington Market, Koreatown, Little Portugal, the Danforth and Leslieville, and a short streetcar ride takes you from one to the next. Half the people who live here were born somewhere else, and you can taste that: Caribbean roti, Hakka chilli chicken and Portuguese custard tarts are local food here, not novelties.

The city suits food lovers, people who enjoy walking around neighbourhoods, and anyone who likes big-city energy without big-city chaos. It is not cheap and it is not especially pretty in the grey months, but it is easy, safe and well connected. Three days is the right amount of time. That covers the main sights, a day of neighbourhoods and food, and the islands. Add a fourth day if you want Niagara Falls.

What to see in Toronto: the places worth your time

CN Tower

The tower is the city's symbol, and the view from 346 metres up is worth paying for once. Allow 1.5-2 hours including queues. A standard ticket is about CAD 45-50 (€30-34), and it costs less if you book online. Our tip: book a slot just before sunset so you see the city in daylight and lit up at night. If you have the nerve and about CAD 200 (€135), the EdgeWalk takes you around the outside ledge in a harness.

St Lawrence Market

This is one of the best food markets in North America, in a 19th-century hall in Old Town. Give it an hour, or longer if you're hungry. Entry is free, and lunch costs €8-15. Order the peameal bacon sandwich at Carousel Bakery (about CAD 10/€7), which is the city's signature sandwich. Saturday morning brings the farmers' market to the north building, but also the biggest crowds. Note that the market is closed on Sundays and Mondays.

Toronto Islands

The islands are a car-free chain of parkland 10-15 minutes by ferry from downtown. They have beaches, bike paths and the best skyline view in the city. Plan half a day. The return ferry from Jack Layton Ferry Terminal costs about CAD 9 (€6). Take the boat to Ward's Island or Hanlan's Point rather than Centre Island, because the queues are shorter and those areas are quieter. Hanlan's has a clothing-optional beach, so you have been warned.

Kensington Market

Kensington is a scruffy, independent pocket of vintage shops, cheap eats and murals just west of Spadina Avenue. Spend 1-2 hours wandering. It's free unless you shop. On Pedestrian Sundays in summer the cars are banned, which makes it far more pleasant. Grab a patty or tacos and eat on the move.

Art Gallery of Ontario (AGO)

The AGO has a superb collection of Canadian art, including the Group of Seven, inside a building redesigned by Frank Gehry. You need 2-3 hours. Adult entry is about CAD 30 (€20). Entry is free on Wednesday evenings, and visitors under 25 can get a free annual pass, so check the details before you go. Don't miss the spiral wooden staircase.

Royal Ontario Museum (ROM)

The ROM is Canada's biggest museum, covering everything from dinosaurs to world cultures. Its crystal-shaped extension on Bloor Street gets mixed reviews. Allow 2-3 hours. Entry is about CAD 26 (€18). Go first thing on a weekday so you miss the school groups, and head straight for the dinosaur gallery.

Distillery District

This is a former whisky distillery with Victorian brick buildings and cobbled lanes, now full of galleries, cafés and shops. An hour is enough. It's free to walk around. It is busiest and most atmospheric during the Christmas market, which charges entry on weekend evenings. Most of the shops are pricey, so come for the setting and a drink at Mill Street Brewery.

Casa Loma

Casa Loma is a mock-Gothic castle built by a financier in the early 1900s, complete with secret passages and a tunnel to the stables. Plan 1.5-2 hours. Tickets cost about CAD 45 (€30). The gardens and towers are the highlights. Afterwards, walk down the Baldwin Steps towards Spadina for a good view back over the city.

Graffiti Alley (Rush Lane)

This is a kilometre of street art running parallel to Queen Street West between Spadina and Portland. You need 30 minutes, and it's free. Go in the morning for better light and fewer people posing for photos. Combine it with a walk along Queen West, one of the city's best shopping streets.

High Park

High Park is the city's biggest park, with trails, a pond, a small free zoo and the famous cherry blossoms. Allow 1-3 hours. It's free, and you get there on Line 2 to High Park station. The blossoms usually appear in late April or early May for about a week and bring huge crowds. Go at dawn, or skip it during the peak.

Scarborough Bluffs

These white cliffs rise 90 metres above Lake Ontario on the eastern edge of the city. It is a half-day trip by subway and bus, and it costs nothing. Bluffer's Park, down at the base, gives the best view. Don't climb the cliff edges, because people get stuck or hurt every year.

Hockey Hall of Fame

The Hockey Hall of Fame is in a former bank on Yonge and Front, and it holds the Stanley Cup. Plan 1.5 hours. Entry is about CAD 25 (€17). It's worth it if you care even slightly about hockey and skippable if you don't. The interactive shooting games are fun for kids.

Things to do in Toronto

Beyond the big sights, these are the experiences that make the trip:

  • Watch a Leafs or Raptors game at Scotiabank Arena. Leafs tickets are expensive (from about CAD 150/€100). Raptors seats are easier to get, and a Blue Jays baseball game in summer is the cheapest option (from about CAD 20/€14).
  • Kayak or paddleboard around the islands' lagoons. Rentals cost about CAD 40-50 (€27-34) per hour.
  • Bike the Martin Goodman Trail along the waterfront using Bike Share Toronto. A day pass costs about CAD 15 (€10).
  • Eat dim sum on a weekend morning in Chinatown on Spadina, or head north to Markham if you're serious about it.
  • Bar-hop on Ossington Avenue or Dundas West, where the city's best cocktail bars and pubs are.
  • Catch a show in the Entertainment District, or a film at TIFF Lightbox. In September the Toronto International Film Festival takes over the city.
  • Skate at Nathan Phillips Square in winter in front of the Toronto sign. The rink is free, and skate rental is about CAD 15 (€10).

Toronto for free (or almost)

Toronto is expensive, but some of its best moments cost nothing:

  • The AGO on Wednesday evenings is free.
  • Walk the waterfront from Harbourfront Centre to Sugar Beach, with its pink umbrellas.
  • Evergreen Brick Works has a Saturday farmers' market in an old quarry, plus trails into the ravines.
  • Riverdale Park East is one of the best free sunset spots over the skyline.
  • Explore the PATH, a 30 km underground network of shopping corridors under downtown. It's useful in January.
  • The ferry costs about CAD 9 (€6), and the views of the skyline from the islands cost nothing.
  • Free admission at the Toronto Public Library's Toronto Reference Library, a striking atrium building near Yonge and Bloor.

Toronto itinerary: 3 days

Day 1: Downtown and the waterfront

Morning: Start at St Lawrence Market for a peameal bacon sandwich, then walk five minutes to the Distillery District. Afternoon: Walk west along Front Street to Union Station and the Hockey Hall of Fame, or skip straight to the waterfront at Harbourfront. Evening: Go up the CN Tower before sunset, then have dinner in the Entertainment District or on King West.

Day 2: Neighbourhoods and food

Morning: Visit the AGO, then walk west to Kensington Market and Chinatown for lunch. Afternoon: Walk Queen West and Graffiti Alley, then continue to Trinity Bellwoods Park. Evening: Have dinner and drinks on Ossington or in Little Portugal on Dundas West.

Day 3: Islands, uptown and the east

Morning: Take the ferry to Ward's Island, walk or bike to Centre Island, and come back from Hanlan's. In winter, swap this for the ROM. Afternoon: Take Line 1 north to Dupont for Casa Loma, or go to Yorkville for window shopping. Evening: Take Line 2 east to the Danforth for Greek food, then catch the sunset from Riverdale Park East.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Yonge-Dundas Square: It's billboards, chain stores and noise, a poor imitation of Times Square. Walk five minutes west to the AGO or Kensington instead.
  • Ripley's Aquarium on a weekend: It's a good aquarium, but at about CAD 50 (€34) with weekend crowds it's miserable. Go on a weekday evening or skip it.
  • The 360 restaurant in the CN Tower: It's a revolving restaurant with average food at high prices. The tower entry is included, but you'll eat better elsewhere for the same money.
  • Centre Island in July: Long ferry queues and crowded picnic areas. Ward's Island has better beaches and is quieter.

When to visit Toronto

The best time to visit is from late May to June or September to early October, when temperatures are around 18-25°C, the patios are open and there are fewer crowds than in high summer. July and August are hot, humid and busy, and they're also when hotel prices peak. September brings the film festival, when prices spike.

Winter runs from December to March, with temperatures often between -5°C and -15°C and wind off the lake. It's grim for walking, but you'll find the cheapest flights and hotels, plus skating rinks and the Distillery Christmas market. November and late March to April are grey but quiet and cheap.

Where to stay in Toronto: best areas

  • Entertainment District / King West: This puts you close to the CN Tower, the theatres and nightlife. It's best for first-timers. Expect CAD 220-400 (€150-270) per night for mid-range hotels.
  • Old Town / St Lawrence: It's quieter and close to the market and the Distillery, with good transit links. Expect CAD 200-350 (€135-235).
  • Queen West / Ossington: Best for food and bar lovers who want local character. It has boutique hotels like the Drake and the Gladstone. Expect CAD 200-380 (€135-255).
  • Yorkville / the Annex: Yorkville is upmarket, and the Annex next door is leafier and more residential. Both are close to the ROM and on the subway. Expect CAD 180-500 (€120-340).
  • Budget: Hostels downtown and in Kensington cost CAD 50-80 (€34-55) for a dorm bed.

Our tip: avoid staying near the airport to save money. The savings disappear in transport time and cost.

What to eat in Toronto

Toronto's food is its strongest argument, and the best of it is cheap and immigrant-run. Here is what to order:

  • Peameal bacon sandwich: Brined pork loin rolled in cornmeal, served on a bun. Get it at Carousel Bakery in St Lawrence Market.
  • Jamaican patties and roti: Look on Eglinton West or in Kensington. A beef patty in coco bread is the cheapest filling lunch in the city.
  • Poutine: It's Québécois, but it's everywhere. Smoke's is the chain version, and pubs do better ones.
  • Butter tarts: A very sweet Canadian pastry, often with raisins. Try them at the market.
  • Dim sum, pho and Hakka Chinese: Find these in Chinatown on Spadina. Chilli chicken and Manchurian dishes are a local Indo-Chinese favourite.
  • Korean barbecue and fried chicken in Koreatown on Bloor West, and souvlaki on the Danforth.
  • Portuguese custard tarts in Little Portugal on Dundas West.

A cheap lunch costs CAD 12-20 (€8-14). A mid-range dinner with a drink runs CAD 50-80 (€34-55) per person. Menu prices don't include the 13% tax, and tipping 15-18% is expected, so add about 30% to what you see.

Airport to city centre and getting around

The fastest route from Pearson Airport is the UP Express train to Union Station. It takes 25 minutes, runs every 15 minutes, and costs about CAD 12-13 (€8-9) with a contactless card or Presto. A taxi or Uber takes 30-60 minutes depending on traffic and costs about CAD 60-75 (€40-50). The cheapest option is the 900 Airport Express bus to Kipling station, then the subway. It costs a single TTC fare of about CAD 3.30 (€2.25) and takes about an hour.

If you fly into Billy Bishop, the island airport used by Porter, it's a short pedestrian tunnel to the mainland and then a walk, streetcar or quick taxi downtown.

In the city, the TTC runs the subway, streetcars and buses. Tap your credit card or a Presto card for a fare of about CAD 3.30 (€2.25), which includes two hours of transfers. A day pass costs about CAD 13.50 (€9), so it only pays off if you take more than four trips. Downtown is very walkable, and the 504 King and 501 Queen streetcars are the most useful lines.

How much a trip to Toronto costs

  • Shoestring: CAD 110-150 (€75-100) per day. This covers a hostel dorm, market lunches and patties, transit, and mostly free sights.
  • Mid-range: CAD 300-450 (€200-300) per day. This covers a 3-star hotel or private room, two restaurant meals, one or two paid attractions and the occasional taxi.
  • Extras: Budget CAD 45-50 (€30-34) for the CN Tower, CAD 30 (€20) for a museum, and CAD 10-14 (€7-10) for a beer in a bar.

Day trips from Toronto

  • Niagara Falls: The GO train or bus takes about 2 hours each way, and a return costs about CAD 40 (€27). Do the Hornblower boat or Journey Behind the Falls, then leave the Clifton Hill strip quickly.
  • Niagara-on-the-Lake: A pretty town with wineries and ice wine, 2 hours away. You need a car or a tour. Combine it with the Falls.
  • Hamilton waterfalls: Hamilton has more than 100 waterfalls, about 1 hour away by GO train. Webster's Falls and Tews Falls are the classics.
  • Elora Gorge: Limestone cliffs, river tubing in summer and a charming village, 1.5 hours away by car.
  • Algonquin Park: Lakes, canoes, moose and autumn colours, about 3 hours away by car. It's better as an overnight trip, but doable in a long day in October.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Forgetting tax and tips: Your bill will be about 30% higher than the menu price. Budget for it.
  • Thinking Niagara is next door: It's 2 hours each way. Don't squeeze it into a 2-day trip.
  • Resale ticket sites: Fake or massively marked-up Leafs and concert tickets are common. Buy from Ticketmaster or official club apps.
  • Overpriced Niagara bus tours sold in hotel lobbies with "free" winery stops that are really sales pitches. The GO train plus a local tour is cheaper.
  • Short-term rental scams: Toronto restricts short-term rentals, and fake listings appear. Book through platforms with payment protection, never by bank transfer.
  • Underdressing in winter: Wind chill makes -10°C feel like -20°C. Pack a real coat, a hat and gloves.
  • Driving downtown: Traffic, construction and parking at CAD 30-50 (€20-34) a day make it a bad idea. Use transit.

Toronto FAQ

How many days do you need in Toronto?

Three days is ideal for the main sights, the neighbourhoods and the islands. Add a fourth if you want a day trip to Niagara Falls.

Is Toronto expensive to visit?

Yes, especially for hotels, which are often over CAD 250 (€170) a night in summer. Food can be cheap if you stick to markets and immigrant neighbourhoods.

Is the CN Tower worth it?

Once, yes, especially around sunset. Book online for a time slot and skip the revolving restaurant unless you want the experience.

Is Toronto safe for tourists?

Toronto is one of the safer big cities in North America. Use normal city sense late at night around Yonge-Dundas and parts of downtown east.

What is the best month to visit Toronto?

June and September have warm weather, open patios and fewer crowds than July and August. Winter is cheapest but very cold.

Can you do Niagara Falls as a day trip from Toronto?

Yes. The GO train takes about 2 hours each way, so leave early and plan 4-5 hours at the Falls.

What food is Toronto known for?

The peameal bacon sandwich is the local classic, but the city is really known for diverse food: Caribbean roti, dim sum, Korean and Portuguese.

Do you need a car in Toronto?

No. The subway, streetcars and walking cover everything in the city. Only rent a car for day trips like Algonquin or Prince Edward County.
