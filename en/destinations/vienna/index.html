What to see in Vienna in 3 days: a practical traveller's guide

🔥 5 deals to Vienna🏨 Hotels in Vienna

Why go to Vienna

Vienna is a city that still works the way it did a century ago, only with better trams. Imperial palaces, coffeehouses where nobody rushes you, an opera house that sells standing tickets for the price of a sandwich, and tap water that comes straight from the Alps. It is clean, safe and easy to navigate, and it rewards slow days better than checklists.

It also suits almost any budget if you pick your battles. The big palaces are pricey, but the parks, the Ringstrasse, the markets and the cafés cost very little.

What to see: the places worth your time

Stephansdom and the Innere Stadt

The Gothic cathedral with its patterned tile roof sits in the middle of the old centre. Climb the south tower (about 340 steps) for rooftops and a close look at the tiles. Then wander the Graben and the lanes around it without a plan.

The Hofburg

The former imperial residence is a huge complex rather than a single building. The Imperial Apartments and the Sisi Museum are the main draw, and the Spanish Riding School is nearby if you like horses. Allow two hours and skip the rest if you are short on time.

Schönbrunn Palace

Go for the gardens as much as the interior. Walk up to the Gloriette for the view back over the palace and the city. Arrive at opening time, because by eleven the tour groups have taken over.

The Belvedere

Klimt's The Kiss hangs in the Upper Belvedere, and it is smaller and more golden than you expect. The baroque gardens between the two palaces are free and lovely in the late afternoon.

Kunsthistorisches Museum

This is the one big museum I would not skip. The Bruegel room alone, with Hunters in the Snow and the Tower of Babel, justifies the ticket. The domed staircase hall is a sight in itself, and the café under the dome is a good place to rest.

Karlskirche

A baroque church with a green dome and two columns that look borrowed from Rome. Inside, a lift takes you up close to the dome frescoes, which is a rare angle.

Prater and the Riesenrad

The giant Ferris wheel dates from 1897 and turns slowly enough to enjoy. The Wurstelprater amusement park around it is free to enter and pleasantly old-fashioned. For a real walk, continue into the long green Prater park behind it.

Naschmarkt

Walk it for the atmosphere, but eat elsewhere, as many stalls on the main stretch charge tourist prices. On Saturdays a flea market runs at the far end, and that is where the fun is.

Hundertwasserhaus

A wavy, colourful apartment block in Landstraße. You can only admire the outside, which takes ten minutes. Worth it if you are passing.

Things to do beyond the sights

  • Take a seat in a coffeehouse. Café Central, Café Sperl and Demel are the classics. Order a Melange and a slice of cake, and stay as long as you like.
  • Stand at the Opera. Standing tickets at the Staatsoper go on sale shortly before the performance and cost a few euros. Queue early.
  • Ride the Ringstrasse by tram. Lines 1 and 2 circle the grand boulevard past the Parliament, Rathaus and Burgtheater for the price of a normal ticket.
  • Go to a Heuriger. These are wine taverns in the vineyards on the edge of the city. Grinzing is the best known and the most touristy. Nussdorf and Stammersdorf feel more local.
  • Cycle or stroll the Donauinsel. A long, narrow island in the Danube with swimming spots in summer.

How many days and a suggested route

Three days is the sweet spot. Two will feel rushed if you want museums, and four starts to suit day trips to the Wachau valley.

Day 1 (old centre): Stephansdom, Graben, Hofburg, a long coffee break, then a tram loop around the Ringstrasse. Finish with dinner in a Beisl (a traditional tavern) near Stephansplatz.

Day 2 (palaces): Schönbrunn early, then Naschmarkt for a quick bite, Karlskirche, and the Belvedere in the afternoon light.

Day 3 (museums and play): Kunsthistorisches Museum in the morning, a wander through the MuseumsQuartier courtyard, then the Prater at sunset. A Heuriger evening fits here if you have energy.

When to go

The best months are April to June and September to October, when days are mild and the parks are at their best. July and August can hit 30°C or more, and the heat gets heavy in the stone streets, though locals head to the Danube and the open-air pools. Winter is cold, often around freezing, but the Christmas markets from mid-November (Rathausplatz, Schönbrunn, Spittelberg) are worth the layers. January and February are ball season, when the city dresses up even if you do not. Expect grey days then, but museums and cafés are at their cosiest.

Where to stay

Innere Stadt (1st district)

Everything is on foot and the grand hotels are here, including Hotel Sacher and the Imperial. It is the priciest area and a bit sleepy at night.

Neubau and Mariahilf (7th and 6th)

My pick for most travellers. Independent shops, good bars and cafés, and the MuseumsQuartier on the doorstep. Mid-range hotels and apartments are plentiful.

Josefstadt (8th)

Quiet and residential, with a relaxed feel and a short walk to the centre. Good for a calm base.

Leopoldstadt (2nd)

Close to the Prater, with a growing café and restaurant scene around Karmelitermarkt. A good value-for-money option.

Budget

Hostels and cheaper hotels cluster around Westbahnhof and Hauptbahnhof. Both are well connected by U-Bahn and train, so the location is better than it looks on the map.

What to eat and where

Start with Wiener Schnitzel: veal, breaded, thin, served with potato salad and a lemon wedge. Figlmüller near Stephansplatz is famous for its huge ones, and while busy, it does the job. For Tafelspitz (boiled beef with apple-horseradish), Plachutta is the name locals mention.

For sweets, Sachertorte at Hotel Sacher is the icon, though Demel's cakes are just as good. Apfelstrudel and Kaiserschmarrn (torn, caramelised pancake with plum compote) are the dessert order of the day.

When you want something cheap and fast, a Würstelstand (sausage stand) is a Viennese institution. Order a Käsekrainer, a sausage with melted cheese inside, and eat it standing. Bitzinger by the Albertina is the best-known one. For a drink, try a glass of Grüner Veltliner or a Gemischter Satz, the local field-blend white.

A main course in a traditional place usually lands around 15 to 25 euros.

Getting from the airport and getting around

Vienna Airport (VIE) is in Schwechat, about 20 minutes from the centre. Three train options are worth knowing:

  • CAT (City Airport Train): non-stop to Wien Mitte in about 16 minutes. It is the fastest, at around 15 euros one way.
  • S7 suburban train: to Wien Mitte in about 25 minutes, for a few euros. It is the sensible choice for most.
  • Railjet and regional trains: some go on to Hauptbahnhof, handy if you are staying near it.

Airport buses also run to Schwedenplatz and the main stations, but the trains are quicker.

In the city, buy a Wiener Linien pass (24, 48 or 72 hours) and use the U-Bahn, trams and buses. A single ticket costs roughly 2.50 euros. The centre is compact and walkable, and trams are the most scenic way to bridge the gaps. Remember that shops are closed on Sundays, so plan groceries ahead.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: around 60 to 80 euros per person (hostel, street food, public transport, one paid sight).
  • Mid-range: roughly 120 to 180 euros (a decent hotel, sit-down meals, a couple of museums).
  • Comfortable: 250 euros and up, which gets you grand hotels and fine dining.

These are approximate figures. Palace tickets and cake add up fast.

FAQ

Is Vienna expensive?

Moderately. It is cheaper than Paris or London, and public transport and cafés are good value. Sights and hotels in the centre cost the most.

Is three days enough?

Yes, for the main sights, a couple of museums and a Heuriger evening. Add a fourth day if you want a day trip.

Is tap water safe to drink?

Yes, and it is excellent, coming from Alpine springs. Bring a refillable bottle and save money.

Do I need to speak German?

No. English is widely spoken in hotels, restaurants and museums. A simple Grüß Gott or Danke still goes down well.

Should I tip?

Yes. Round up or add around 10 percent in restaurants and cafés, and tell the waiter the total when you pay rather than leaving cash on the table.
