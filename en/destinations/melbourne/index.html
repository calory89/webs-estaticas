Melbourne doesn't do postcard landmarks the way Sydney does. There's no Opera House and no famous bridge. What it has is a grid of laneways full of espresso bars, free museums, excellent food from every corner of the world, a sports obsession bordering on religion, and suburbs that each feel like a different small town. You don't really sightsee here. You eat, drink, wander and catch a tram whenever your legs give out.

It suits food lovers, coffee snobs, art people, sports fans and anyone who likes cities more than beaches. Give it 4 days: two and a half for the city and inner suburbs, and one for a day trip (Phillip Island or the Yarra Valley). If you want the Great Ocean Road properly, add a fifth day and stay overnight on the coast.

What to see in Melbourne: the places worth your time

The CBD laneways (Degraves Street, Centre Place, Block Arcade)

The laneways are the Melbourne cliché, and they deserve it. Degraves Street and Centre Place, off Flinders Lane, are packed with tiny cafés, and the Block Arcade on Collins Street has mosaic floors and the old-school Hopetoun Tea Rooms (expect a queue for the cake window). Allow 1-2 hours, free. Our tip: go mid-morning on a weekday, when office workers are grabbing coffee and the lanes feel lived-in rather than staged.

Hosier Lane and the street art lanes

Hosier Lane, opposite Federation Square, is the most famous street art spot in Australia, and the art gets painted over constantly, so it's never the same twice. It's also crowded with selfie sticks and smells of spray paint. Spend 20 minutes, free. Then walk to AC/DC Lane and Duckboard Place nearby, which are just as good and far quieter.

National Gallery of Victoria (NGV International)

The NGV on St Kilda Road is the best art museum in the country, and the permanent collection is free. Lie on the floor under the stained-glass ceiling of the Great Hall, which is a local tradition and not a weird thing to do. Allow 2-3 hours. Paid blockbuster exhibitions run about A$30 (€17), and you need to book weekend slots. The Ian Potter Centre at Federation Square holds the Australian collection, also free.

Royal Botanic Gardens

Huge, free and beautifully kept, the Botanic Gardens sit a 15-minute walk south of the city along the Yarra. Guilfoyle's Volcano and the Fern Gully are the highlights, and the lake is good for a picnic. Allow 1.5-2 hours. Combine it with the Shrine of Remembrance, which is right next door.

Shrine of Remembrance

This is Melbourne's war memorial, a solemn and striking building, and it's free. Climb to the upper balconies for one of the best free views of the skyline straight up St Kilda Road. Allow 45 minutes. If you're there on 11 November at 11am, a ray of light hits the Stone of Remembrance inside.

State Library Victoria

The domed La Trobe Reading Room is the most photographed interior in the city, and you can walk straight in for free. Head to the upper galleries for the view down onto the desks. Allow 30-60 minutes. Keep your voice down, because people are working, and the staff will let you know.

Queen Victoria Market

This sprawling 19th-century market on the north edge of the CBD sells fruit, meat, deli goods and, less excitingly, cheap souvenirs. Go for the deli hall and the famous hot jam doughnuts from the van (about A$2.50 each, €1.50). Allow 1-1.5 hours. It's closed on Mondays and Wednesdays, so check the hours. In summer, the Wednesday night market runs with street food stalls.

Melbourne Cricket Ground (MCG)

The MCG holds about 100,000 people and is the spiritual home of Australian sport. If there's an AFL (Aussie rules football) game on during the season (roughly March to September), go: tickets start around A$30-35 (€18-20) and the crowd is the real show. Otherwise, the stadium tour costs about A$35 and takes roughly 75 minutes. The Australian Sports Museum is inside.

Fitzroy and Collingwood

Brunswick Street and Gertrude Street in Fitzroy, plus Smith Street on the Collingwood side, are where Melbourne goes for vintage shops, record stores, bars and serious brunch. Take tram 11 up Brunswick Street. Spend an afternoon and evening here. It's free to wander, but your wallet won't come out unscathed.

St Kilda and the penguins

St Kilda is Melbourne's seaside suburb: palm trees, a pier, the old-fashioned Luna Park and the cake shops of Acland Street. At dusk, little penguins come ashore at the St Kilda breakwater, and you can watch them for free from the boardwalk (no flash, and volunteers will tell you off). Take tram 96 from the city, about 30 minutes. Allow half a day, and go on a weekday if you can.

Melbourne Museum and the Royal Exhibition Building

The Royal Exhibition Building in Carlton Gardens is UNESCO-listed and gorgeous from outside. Tours inside are occasional, so check before going. The Melbourne Museum beside it costs about A$15 (€9) and is excellent on Indigenous history (Bunjilaka) and natural science, plus a whale skeleton. Allow 2-3 hours. Lygon Street, Melbourne's old Italian strip, is a 5-minute walk away for lunch.

ACMI at Federation Square

Australia's museum of screen culture is free, interactive and much more fun than it sounds, especially if it's raining. Allow 1-1.5 hours. Federation Square itself is mostly a meeting point, so use it as one.

Things to do in Melbourne

Some of the best things to do in Melbourne have nothing to do with ticking off sights. These are the ones we'd prioritise:

  • Go to the footy. An AFL match at the MCG or Marvel Stadium is the most Melbourne thing you can do. Learn the basic rules in five minutes from the person next to you, who'll be happy to explain.
  • Do a coffee crawl. Hit three cafés in a morning: Patricia Coffee Brewers (standing room only, off Little Bourke Street), Market Lane at Queen Victoria Market, and something in Fitzroy. Order a flat white or a magic (a Melbourne-specific double ristretto with milk).
  • Rooftop bars. Try Rooftop Bar at Curtin House on Swanston Street (there's a cinema up there in summer) or Naked for Satan on Brunswick Street. Expect A$12-15 (€7-9) for a beer.
  • Catch live music. Melbourne has more live music venues than almost anywhere. The Corner Hotel in Richmond and the Northcote Social Club are reliable, with gigs often A$20-50.
  • Eat dumplings in Chinatown. Little Bourke Street has some of the oldest Chinatown streets in the Western world. Shanghai Street dumplings are cheap and fast.
  • Cycle or walk the Yarra. The path from Southbank past the Botanic Gardens toward Richmond is flat and pleasant.
  • Watch sport in season. The Australian Open (January) and the Grand Prix at Albert Park (usually March) take over the city. Book accommodation early.

Melbourne for free (or almost)

Melbourne is expensive, but a surprising amount of what you'll want to do costs nothing. Trams inside the CBD Free Tram Zone are free, which covers Queen Victoria Market, Federation Square, Docklands and the State Library. The heritage City Circle tram (route 35) loops the centre with a recorded commentary, also free.

  • NGV International and the Ian Potter Centre (permanent collections)
  • State Library Victoria and its reading room
  • Royal Botanic Gardens and the Shrine of Remembrance balcony
  • ACMI
  • Hosier Lane and the street art lanes
  • The St Kilda penguins at dusk
  • Free walking tours from the State Library area (tip what you think it's worth)
  • Brighton beach and the bathing boxes, for the price of a train fare

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Eureka Skydeck. It's high and the glass box is fun, but at around A$30+ (€17+) you're paying a lot for a view of a flat city. Go to the Shrine balcony for free, or have a drink at a rooftop bar instead.
  • The Great Ocean Road as a day trip. The Twelve Apostles are roughly a 3.5-hour drive each way, so a day tour means 10-12 hours, mostly on a bus. Stay a night in Lorne or Apollo Bay, or skip it and do Phillip Island.
  • Southbank restaurants. The riverfront chains near Crown cost a lot for average food. Walk ten minutes into the CBD laneways.
  • Brighton bathing boxes at midday on a weekend. They're colourful huts on a beach, now surrounded by tour groups. If you go, go early on a weekday, and don't expect more than 30 minutes of interest.

When to visit Melbourne

The best time to visit Melbourne is March to May (autumn): mild days around 18-25°C, settled weather and the footy season starting. Spring (September to November) is also good, though windier and more changeable.

Summer (December to February) brings warm days, outdoor events and the Australian Open, but also occasional 40°C scorchers followed by a cold change. Locals joke about "four seasons in one day", and it's not much of a joke. Winter (June to August) is grey and cool, around 13-15°C. It's also the cheapest and quietest time for hotels, and the city's indoor culture of bars, galleries and cafés holds up fine. Prices spike during the Australian Open, the Grand Prix, the AFL Grand Final (late September) and the Melbourne Cup (early November).

Where to stay in Melbourne: best areas

  • CBD: best for first-timers on a short trip, with the free tram zone, everything walkable and good transport links. Hostel dorms cost A$40-60 (€23-35), mid-range hotels A$180-280 (€105-160).
  • Southbank: close to the arts precinct and the river, with plenty of high-rise apartments. It's convenient but a bit soulless after dark. Expect A$200-300 (€115-170).
  • Fitzroy and Collingwood: for bars, food and character. Accommodation is limited, mostly boutique hotels and rentals at A$180-300 (€105-170).
  • St Kilda: for the beach, backpackers and nightlife, 30 minutes from the CBD by tram. Dorms run A$35-55, and hotels A$150-250 (€85-145).
  • South Yarra and Richmond: calmer, well connected by train and tram, good restaurants. Expect A$160-260 (€90-150).

What to eat in Melbourne

Melbourne food is less about a local dish and more about immigrant cuisines done brilliantly. The Italian, Greek, Vietnamese and Chinese communities shaped how the city eats, and the café culture is a cuisine in itself.

  • Flat white: about A$5-6 (€3). Coffee here is serious, and chain coffee is frowned upon.
  • Brunch: smashed avocado on sourdough, ricotta hotcakes or baked eggs. Budget A$20-28 (€12-16).
  • Croissants from Lune (Fitzroy or the CBD): genuinely excellent, though the queue can be long. Go on a weekday morning.
  • Dumplings and noodles on Little Bourke Street, A$15-20 (€9-12).
  • Pho and banh mi on Victoria Street in Richmond or in Footscray, A$12-20.
  • Souvlaki and Greek food on Lonsdale Street or in Oakleigh.
  • Chicken parma: a pub classic (schnitzel with tomato sauce and cheese, plus chips), around A$28-32 (€16-18).
  • Espresso and spaghetti at Pellegrini's on Bourke Street, an old-school institution since the 1950s.
  • Dim sims and a meat pie at the footy.

A casual lunch runs A$20-30 (€12-17). A decent dinner with a drink costs A$50-80 (€30-45) per person, while fine dining starts at A$150+. Tipping isn't expected, though rounding up for good service is common.

Airport to city centre and getting around

The fastest cheap way from Melbourne Airport (Tullamarine) is the SkyBus to Southern Cross Station: about 20-40 minutes, around A$25 (€14) one way, running frequently around the clock. There's no train to the airport yet. A taxi or Uber costs about A$60-80 (€35-45) and takes 25-45 minutes depending on traffic. Avalon Airport, used by some budget domestic flights, has its own shuttle bus to the city (about an hour).

Getting around, use trams, trains and buses with a myki card (about A$6 for the card, then top up). A daily cap means you won't pay more than roughly A$11 (€6) a day in the metro area. Check whether contactless bank card tapping is running by the time you visit, since the system has been rolling it out. Trams inside the CBD Free Tram Zone need no ticket, but tap on as soon as you leave it. Ticket inspectors are active and fines are steep.

Day trips from Melbourne

  • Phillip Island: the Penguin Parade at sunset is the main event, where hundreds of little penguins waddle ashore (about A$30-35 entry). It's roughly a 2-hour drive or an organised tour. Book ahead in summer.
  • Great Ocean Road: one of the world's great coastal drives, with Bells Beach, Lorne, the Otway rainforest and the Twelve Apostles. Do it over two days by car if you can.
  • Yarra Valley: wineries, cheese and the Healesville Sanctuary for native animals, about 1-1.5 hours by car. Don't drive yourself if you plan to taste properly. Take a tour.
  • Mornington Peninsula: beaches, wineries and Peninsula Hot Springs, about 1.5 hours by car. Book the springs in advance and go on a weekday.
  • Dandenong Ranges: tall forest, tea rooms in Sassafras and the Puffing Billy steam train, about an hour away. Train to Belgrave works if you don't have a car.

Common mistakes and scams

Melbourne is a safe city with very few scams aimed at tourists. The traps are mostly about money and logistics:

  • Not tapping on outside the Free Tram Zone. "I'm a tourist" won't get you out of a fine of a couple of hundred dollars.
  • Surprise surcharges. Many cafés add 10-15% on Sundays and public holidays, plus a small fee for paying by card. It's legal, so check the menu.
  • Packing for one season. Carry a light jacket even on a hot morning, because a cold change can drop the temperature 15 degrees in an hour.
  • Underestimating the sun. UV is brutal even when it's cool. Wear sunscreen and a hat.
  • Hook turns. If you drive in the CBD, some intersections require you to turn right from the left lane to keep clear of trams. Read up before you go, or simply don't drive in the city.
  • Treating distances as small. Day trips look short on a map but aren't. Don't drive the Great Ocean Road jet-lagged after a long-haul flight.

Melbourne FAQ

How many days do you need in Melbourne?

Four days is the sweet spot: three for the city and inner suburbs, plus one for a day trip. Add a fifth if you want to do the Great Ocean Road with an overnight stay.

Is Melbourne expensive to visit?

Yes, accommodation and eating out are pricey by European standards. Free trams in the centre, free museums and cheap Asian food help a lot.

What is Melbourne best known for?

Coffee, laneways, street art, sport (especially AFL and the Australian Open) and a strong food scene shaped by Italian, Greek, Vietnamese and Chinese communities.

Is Melbourne better than Sydney?

They're different. Sydney wins on beaches and big landmarks, while Melbourne wins on food, coffee, culture and nightlife.

Can you see penguins in Melbourne for free?

Yes, at the St Kilda breakwater at dusk, reached by tram 96 from the city. The bigger Penguin Parade on Phillip Island is paid and about two hours away.

Are trams free in Melbourne?

Only inside the CBD Free Tram Zone. Everywhere else you need to tap on with a myki or an accepted payment card.

What is the best month to visit Melbourne?

March or April, when the weather is mild and stable and the summer crowds have gone.

Is Melbourne safe for tourists?

Very. Normal city precautions apply late at night around Flinders Street and King Street, but violent crime against visitors is rare.

Is Melbourne worth visiting?

For the traveller who wrote this guide, yes: Melbourne doesn't do postcard landmarks the way Sydney does. 4 days are enough for the essentials. Budget: Shoestring: A$100-140 a day (€57-80).

What to see in Melbourne in one day?

If you only have one day, follow day 1 of this itinerary: CBD, laneways and the river. Morning: start with coffee on Degraves Street, then walk Centre Place, the Block Arcade and the Royal Arcade.

What to see in Melbourne in two days?

With two days, follow days 1 and 2 of this itinerary. Day 1: CBD, laneways and the river. Morning: start with coffee on Degraves Street, then walk Centre Place, the Block Arcade and the Royal Arcade. Day 2: Gardens, the MCG and Richmond. Morning: the Shrine of Remembrance balcony, then the Royal Botanic Gardens.
