Why go to Bogotá

Bogotá sits at about 2,600 metres in the Andes, and it feels like a serious capital rather than a postcard. It is grey on some mornings, loud, enormous, and then suddenly generous: a gold museum that stops you in your tracks, a hillside church above the clouds, a bowl of soup that fixes everything. Most visitors rush through on the way to Cartagena or the coffee region. That is a mistake. Give the city three days and it pays you back.

It is also one of the better-value capitals in South America for food, museums and street art, and flights are often cheap, which is why it shows up so often in our deals.

What to see in Bogotá

  • La Candelaria. The colonial old town, with steep cobbled lanes, colourful façades and a student crowd. Go early, around 8 a.m., when the light is soft and the streets are nearly empty.
  • Plaza de Bolívar. The formal heart of the city, framed by the cathedral, the Capitolio and the Palace of Justice. Pigeons, rain and politics in equal measure. Look, take a photo, move on.
  • Museo del Oro. The Gold Museum holds thousands of pre-Hispanic pieces, and the dark room where the gold glows around you is genuinely spectacular. It is closed on Mondays. Allow two hours.
  • Museo Botero. Fernando Botero's own donation, with his round, humorous figures next to a few Picassos and Monets. Entry has traditionally been free, but check before you go.
  • Monserrate. The church on the hill at roughly 3,150 metres, reached by cable car or funicular. Choose a clear morning on a weekday, because Sundays are packed with pilgrims. Take it slowly, since the air is thin.
  • Usaquén. A former village now swallowed by the north of the city. Its Sunday flea market and small plaza are pleasant for a lazy afternoon, though more polished than Candelaria.
  • Street art. Bogotá has some of the best murals in Latin America, concentrated in Candelaria and along Calle 26. A guided graffiti walk is one of the best ways to understand the city's politics and humour.
  • Quinta de Bolívar. A small, calm country house at the foot of the hills where Simón Bolívar lived. Good gardens and a break from the crowds.
  • Plaza de Mercado de Paloquemao. A working market, not a tourist set. Piles of flowers, fruit you may never have seen, and cheap breakfast counters.
  • Jardín Botánico José Celestino Mutis. Quiet, green and underrated, with a good Andean plant collection. Useful if you need a slower afternoon.

Things to do

If you are in town on a Sunday or public holiday, join the Ciclovía. Dozens of main roads close to cars and fill with cyclists, joggers and families, and you can rent a bike cheaply. It is the best free way to see the city's real rhythm.

Take a half-day trip to the Catedral de Sal in Zipaquirá, an underground church carved inside a salt mine about an hour north. It is touristy but impressive, and easy to reach by TransMilenio and a regional bus.

For nightlife, head to Zona T and Parque 93 for polished bars, or to Chapinero for a mix of cocktail spots and live music. Try a game of tejo, the traditional Colombian target game involving small explosive paper pouches. It is silly and loud, and well worth an evening.

Bogota itinerary: 3 days, day by day

Three days is the sweet spot. Two will do if you stay central, and four lets you add Zipaquirá or a slower pace.

3-day itinerary for Bogota
DayPlan
Day 1Morning in La Candelaria, then Plaza de Bolívar, Museo del Oro and Museo Botero. Early dinner near the old town, then bed. You will be tired from the altitude.
Day 2Monserrate early, then Paloquemao market for lunch, afternoon on the street art route, evening in Chapinero.
Day 3Ciclovía if it is Sunday, otherwise Quinta de Bolívar and the botanical garden. Finish at Usaquén for a long meal.

When to go

Bogotá has no real summer. Temperatures sit between about 8 and 19 degrees all year, so what changes is rain, not heat. The driest months are December to March and again July to August. April, May, October and November are the wettest, with afternoon downpours that can flood a street in minutes. Always carry a light waterproof and a warm layer, because mornings and evenings are cold. January and the July holidays are busy with domestic tourists, so book rooms earlier.

Where to stay

La Candelaria is the budget and backpacker base, with hostels, guesthouses and easy walking to museums. It is atmospheric but can feel rough after dark, so use taxis at night and avoid empty side streets.

Chapinero is the sweet spot for mid-range travellers. It has restaurants, cafés, good nightlife and plenty of apartment rentals, and the Chapinero Alto and Zona G areas are especially good for food.

Zona T, Parque 93 and Usaquén in the north are safer, quieter and pricier. They suit a comfortable stay with polished hotels and upmarket restaurants, but you will spend more time in cars.

As a rough guide, a hostel bed costs a few dollars to the high teens, a decent mid-range room costs around the price of a good dinner for two in a European city, and upscale hotels in the north start where you would expect them to.

What to eat and where

Start with ajiaco santafereño, the capital's signature soup. It is a thick chicken broth with three kinds of potato, corn on the cob, capers and cream. Order it on a cold afternoon, and go hungry.

For breakfast, try changua, a milk-and-egg soup that is stranger than it sounds, or tamal tolimense with a cup of hot chocolate. In Candelaria, La Puerta Falsa is a tiny, historic spot famous for its tamales and chocolate santafereño con queso, where you drop a piece of cheese into the cup. It sounds wrong and tastes right.

Snack on almojábanas and pandebono from any bakery, and finish with an oblea, a thin wafer filled with arequipe (caramel). At Paloquemao, try fruit such as lulo, granadilla and curuba. For a loud, theatrical night, Andrés Carne de Res in Chía is a Colombian institution, part restaurant, part party, and worth the taxi if you like that kind of thing. In Chapinero and Usaquén you will also find excellent specialty coffee, since Colombia takes it seriously.

Getting from the airport and getting around

El Dorado International Airport (BOG) is about 12 to 15 kilometres west of the centre. The easiest option is an official taxi from the airport booth, where you pay a fixed fare printed on a ticket. Expect roughly 40 minutes in traffic, more at rush hour. Ride-hailing apps are widely used, though the legal status is murky, so sit in the front or follow your driver's instructions on pickups. Public buses and TransMilenio connect to the airport, but they are slow with luggage.

Inside the city, TransMilenio, the rapid bus system, is cheap and reaches most areas, but it is crowded at peak hours and a pickpocket spot. Use it for long north-south trips and keep your phone out of sight. For short hops, taxis and apps are inexpensive by international standards. Candelaria and Chapinero are best on foot, within reason, and always with your phone tucked away.

Take the altitude seriously on arrival. Drink water, go easy on alcohol the first night, and do not plan Monserrate for hour one.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: about US$35 to 55, with a hostel bed, market meals, public transport and a museum or two.
  • Mid-range: about US$80 to 140, with a good private room, restaurant dinners, taxis and tours.
  • Comfortable: US$180 and up, with upscale hotels, fine dining and private transfers.

These are approximate and depend on exchange rates, which can shift a lot.

FAQs

Is Bogotá safe for tourists?

Mostly yes, if you use common sense. Petty theft is the main risk, so avoid walking with your phone out, skip empty streets at night and use taxis or apps after dark. Stay in Chapinero, Usaquén or the Zona T if you want a calmer base.

Do I need Spanish?

It helps a lot. English is limited outside upscale hotels and tourist spots, but people are patient and a translation app goes a long way.

How bad is the altitude?

Most people feel a mild headache or tiredness on day one. Rest, hydrate and take it easy, and it usually passes within a day or two. If you have a heart or lung condition, check with your doctor first.

What should I pack?

Layers, a light rain jacket, comfortable walking shoes and sunscreen, since the sun is strong at altitude. Leave the shorts at home unless you are heading to warmer areas.

Is Bogotá worth more than a stopover?

Yes. A long layover can cover Candelaria and the Gold Museum, but two to three full days lets you eat well, see Monserrate and get a feel for the neighbourhoods.

How many days do you need in Bogota?

3 days are enough to see the essentials of Bogota without rushing. The route in this guide is organised day by day.
