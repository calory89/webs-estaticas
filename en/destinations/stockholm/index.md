# Stockholm in 3 days: what to see, eat and how to save money

> Updated: 2026-10-21

## Why go to Stockholm

Stockholm is spread across 14 islands where Lake Mälaren meets the Baltic, so you are rarely more than a few minutes from water. It is clean, easy to read and walkable, and it has some of the best museums in northern Europe. It is not cheap, but flights are often the part you can fix, and that is where this site comes in. Fly in on a good fare and the rest of the trip feels a lot more relaxed.

## What to see in Stockholm

### Gamla Stan

The old town is touristy, and it is still worth it. Go early, before the cruise-ship groups, and walk Västerlånggatan with the crowds only when you must. The real reward is the side lanes, like Mårten Trotzigs Gränd, which is barely a shoulder wide.

### Vasa Museum

A 17th-century warship that sank on its maiden voyage in 1628 and was raised almost intact in 1961. It is far bigger than the photos suggest. Arrive at opening or in the last two hours to avoid the school groups.

### Skansen

An open-air museum on Djurgården with old farmsteads, Nordic animals and a good view of the city. It is best at Christmas markets or midsummer, but fine any day. Skip it in heavy rain.

### Stadshuset (City Hall)

The brick building where the Nobel banquet is held. Take the guided tour of the Golden Hall, and in season climb the tower for one of the best views over Gamla Stan and Riddarholmen.

### Fotografiska

A photography museum in an old customs house on the Södermalm waterfront. It stays open late, so it works well for a dark winter evening, and the café has a view across to Djurgården.

### Moderna Museet on Skeppsholmen

Modern art with Picasso, Dalí and a strong Swedish collection, on a calm island with a lovely walk around it. The ship hostel af Chapman is moored right next door.

### The Royal Palace

Hundreds of rooms, the Treasury and the changing of the guard. Worth an hour or two if you like royal interiors. If not, the exterior from the Slottsbacken side is enough.

### Drottningholm Palace

The private residence of the royal family, with baroque gardens and a well-preserved theatre, on Lake Mälaren. A UNESCO site that you can reach by boat from City Hall in summer or by metro and bus year round. Half a day.

### Monteliusvägen and Fjällgatan

Two free viewpoints on Södermalm. Monteliusvägen is a short cliff-top path with views toward City Hall and Gamla Stan. Fjällgatan looks over the harbour and is lovely around sunset.

### The metro art

The Stockholm tunnelbana is often called the longest art gallery in the world. Start at T-Centralen and Kungsträdgården, then ride the blue line out past rough rock walls painted in bright colours. It costs only your ordinary ticket.

## Things to do

- **Take a ferry to the archipelago.** Waxholmsbolaget boats go to Vaxholm and further out. For a short trip, Fjäderholmarna is close to the centre.

- **Do fika properly.** Coffee plus a cinnamon bun (kanelbulle) in the afternoon. Vete-Katten on Kungsgatan is a classic old-fashioned stop.

- **Browse Södermalm's SoFo.** Vintage shops, small cafés and record stores south of Folkungagatan.

- **Visit ABBA The Museum.** Cheesy by design and fun even if you only half like the band. Book ahead for weekends.

- **Swim or sauna.** In summer Swedes jump from rocks and jetties around Långholmen. In winter many bathhouses and saunas around the city are open to the public.

Three days is the sweet spot. Two works if you are fast and picky. Four lets you add the archipelago or Drottningholm without rushing.

## When to go

**Late May to early September** is the easiest season. Summer highs sit around 20 to 23°C and the light lasts very late, so you can do a full day and still be outside at 10 pm in June. It is also the busiest and priciest period for hotels. **April to mid-May** brings the cherry blossoms in Kungsträdgården and quieter streets, though the weather is mixed.

**September** is crisp and clear, and I like it more than July. **December** is cold and dark, with the sun setting before 3 pm, but Christmas markets in Gamla Stan and Skansen are lovely and hotel rates can be lower. January and February are freezing, often below 0°C, and cheaper, but short days limit sightseeing. Semlor, the cream-filled buns, appear in late winter.

## Where to stay

- **Norrmalm / City:** Best for transport. Central Station, the airport train and metro lines all meet here. Good for short stays. Less atmosphere in the evening.

- **Gamla Stan:** The most atmospheric, but also the most expensive and touristy. Rooms are small.

- **Södermalm:** My pick for most visitors. Great food, cafés and nightlife with quick metro links. Hotel rates are somewhat lower than in the centre.

- **Östermalm:** Smart, calm and pricey. Good if you like boutique shopping and the food hall.

- **Kungsholmen and Vasastan:** Quieter, more local, often cheaper. Good for longer stays.

For budget, hostels run roughly 35 to 55 € for a dorm bed. The ship hostel af Chapman on Skeppsholmen is a classic. Mid-range hotels usually cost 110 to 180 € a night. Higher-end places go well beyond that. Book early for summer.

## What to eat and where

Start with **köttbullar** (meatballs with mashed potato, lingonberries and pickled cucumber). Meatballs for the People in Södermalm is a modern take, and Pelikan, a beer hall in the same district, is an old-school classic. Try **räkmacka**, an open sandwich piled with prawns, and **stekt strömming** (fried Baltic herring), which you can grab from the stand near Slussen. For something sweeter, order a **kanelbulle** and a coffee.

For lunch, **Östermalms Saluhall** is a beautiful food hall, though it is pricier. **Hötorgshallen** is more casual and good for sampling international food. Many restaurants offer a **dagens rätt** (dish of the day) on weekdays, usually for a lot less than dinner. It is the smartest way to eat well on a budget.

## Getting from the airport and around

**Arlanda (ARN)** is the main airport, about 40 km north. The **Arlanda Express** reaches Stockholm Central in around 20 minutes and is fast but costly (roughly 300 SEK one way). The commuter train (pendeltåg) takes about 40 minutes and costs less, though it adds an airport fee. Airport coaches (Flygbussarna) are usually the cheapest and run to Cityterminalen, next to the Central Station.

Many low-cost flights use **Skavsta (NYO)** or **Västerås (VST)**, both about 100 km away. Coach transfers take roughly 80 minutes to 1 hour 15 minutes and cost much less than Arlanda Express, but add the time and fare to your total. **Bromma (BMA)** is close to the centre but handles mostly domestic flights.

In town, the city is compact. Most of the centre is walkable. For anything else, SL covers metro, buses, trams, commuter trains and some ferries, including the one to Djurgården. Use the SL app. A single ticket lasts about 75 minutes, and 24-hour and 72-hour travelcards usually pay off if you ride a lot.

## Rough daily budget

- **Budget:** about 70 to 100 € a day. Hostel dorm, supermarket food, a dagens rätt and free viewpoints.

- **Mid-range:** about 140 to 220 € a day. Midrange hotel, two sit-down meals and one or two museums.

- **Comfortable:** 250 € and up. Better hotels, dinners with drinks and extra excursions.

Alcohol is the budget killer, since a pint in a bar often costs 8 to 10 €. Museum entry usually costs 15 to 25 € each. Cards are accepted nearly everywhere, and many places are cashless.

## FAQ about Stockholm

### Is Stockholm expensive?

Yes, compared with southern and eastern Europe. Hotels, alcohol and restaurant dinners cost the most. Lunch deals, supermarkets and free sights keep it manageable.

### Is three days enough?

For the main sights, yes. Add a fourth day if you want the archipelago or Drottningholm.

### Do I need Swedish krona?

Sweden uses krona (SEK), not the euro. You will rarely need cash, because cards and phone payments work almost everywhere.

### What is the cheapest way from the airport?

From Arlanda, the airport coach or the commuter train. From Skavsta or Västerås, the airport coach to the city centre.

### Can I see the Northern Lights in Stockholm?

Very rarely. The city has too much light, and the aurora is much more likely in Swedish Lapland, far to the north.
