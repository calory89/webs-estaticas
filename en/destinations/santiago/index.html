Things to Do in Santiago: 3 Days of Hills, Wine and Food

Santiago doesn't try very hard to impress you at first. It's a sprawling, practical capital of seven million people, with smog that sometimes hides the Andes and a centre that empties out after office hours. Then the air clears after rain, the snow-capped wall of mountains appears at the end of every street, and you realise the setting is the city's best feature. The rest is food markets, good museums, cheap wine and neighbourhoods that are much nicer to walk than the guidebook photos suggest.

It suits travellers who like eating, drinking and wandering more than ticking off monuments, and it's the obvious base for wine valleys, the coast and the mountains. Three days is the right amount: two for the city and one for a day trip to Valparaíso or a vineyard. With less time you'll miss the best of the surroundings. With more, you'll be heading out of Santiago, not deeper into it.

What to see in Santiago: the places worth your time

Cerro San Cristóbal and the Parque Metropolitano

This is the hill with the white Virgin statue on top, and the view from it is the best in the city. Take the funicular up from Pío Nono in Bellavista, then the teleférico (cable car) down the other side towards Providencia for a different view. Allow 2-3 hours. Tickets run about €3-6 (CLP 3,000-6,000) depending on the combo. Go in the morning after rain, or on a clear winter day, when you actually see the Andes instead of a brown haze.

Cerro Santa Lucía

A small, overbuilt hill right off the Alameda, with stone staircases, fountains and terraces that look a bit like a stage set. It's free, and the climb to the top takes 20 minutes. You may need to sign in with your passport at the entrance. Go for sunset, but leave before dark: the park closes and the surroundings get dodgy.

Plaza de Armas and the Catedral Metropolitana

The historic square where the city was founded in 1541, with the cathedral, the Correo Central and the Museo Histórico Nacional around it. Allow 30-45 minutes, free. It's lively with chess players and street preachers by day, but keep your phone in your pocket here: this is pickpocket central.

Museo Chileno de Arte Precolombino

The best museum in Santiago, a block from the Plaza de Armas. It covers art from the whole continent, from Andean textiles to Mesoamerican ceramics, and the Chilean room with the chinchorro mummies is worth the visit alone. Allow 1.5-2 hours, about €9-10. Of the city's museums, this is the one to choose if you only have time for one.

Palacio de La Moneda

The presidential palace, bombed during the 1973 coup and now one of the defining images of modern Chile. The outside and the squares are free, and the underground Centro Cultural La Moneda hosts changing exhibitions (small fee). Allow an hour. Check the schedule for the changing of the guard, which happens on alternate days.

Museo de la Memoria y los Derechos Humanos

A heavy but essential museum about the Pinochet dictatorship, next to Quinta Normal metro (Line 5). It's free, and the audio guide in English is well done. Allow 2 hours and don't plan anything cheerful straight after.

Barrio Lastarria

Santiago's most pleasant few blocks: cobbled José Victorino Lastarria street, bookshops, cafés, the GAM cultural centre and terraces full of people drinking pisco sours. It's free to wander and takes an hour or two. The Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes and Parque Forestal are right next to it.

Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes

A grand Beaux-Arts building in Parque Forestal with Chilean art upstairs and a contemporary art museum (MAC) in the back half. Entry is free or close to it. Allow an hour, and it pairs well with a walk through the park and Lastarria.

La Chascona (Neruda's house)

Pablo Neruda's quirky house in Bellavista, built for his lover Matilde Urrutia and full of ship-themed rooms and odd collections. Visits are by audio guide, about €10, roughly an hour. Book ahead or go early, because groups fill it up. If you're going to Valparaíso, La Sebastiana there is the better of the two.

La Vega Central

The real food market, across the Mapocho river from the touristy Mercado Central. It has mountains of avocados, chirimoyas and chillies, plus cheap cocinerías upstairs serving cazuela and fried fish. Allow an hour, free to enter. Go in the morning, carry little, and watch your bag.

Cementerio General

A city of mausoleums where presidents, poets and Víctor Jara are buried, and the memorial wall to the disappeared hits hard. It's free, takes 1-2 hours, and is reached by metro Line 2 to Cementerios. Grab a map at the entrance because it's enormous.

Sky Costanera

The observation deck on the 62nd floor of Gran Torre Santiago, South America's tallest building, sitting above the Costanera Center mall. It costs about €18-20 and takes an hour. Only go on a clear day, ideally at sunset. On a smoggy day you're paying to look at grey.

Things to do in Santiago

  • Drink a terremoto at La Piojera: a cheap, loud, sticky old bar near the Mercado Central. The "earthquake" is pipeño wine with pineapple ice cream and grenadine. One is fun. Two is a mistake.
  • Walk Barrio Italia: furniture workshops, design shops and cafés around Avenida Italia in Providencia, best on a Saturday afternoon.
  • Watch a football match: Colo-Colo at Estadio Monumental or Universidad de Chile is an experience. Go with a local or a tour, and stay out of the barra brava end.
  • Take a cooking class or market tour: several outfits run them in Spanish and English, and they're a good way to understand Chilean food beyond empanadas.
  • Do a wine tasting in the city: wine bars in Lastarria and Providencia pour carménère and País by the glass. That's handy if you can't fit in a vineyard trip.
  • Browse the Persa Bío Bío: a huge weekend flea market south of the centre, with antiques, junk and vinyl. Go Saturday or Sunday morning and bring cash.
  • See street art in Barrio Bellavista and San Miguel: the Museo a Cielo Abierto in San Miguel (metro Line 2, Departamental) has whole apartment blocks covered in murals.
  • Catch a show at GAM or Teatro Municipal: tickets are often cheaper than you'd expect for opera and ballet.

Santiago for free (or almost)

Most of Santiago's best experiences cost nothing. The free list is long:

  • Cerro Santa Lucía, Plaza de Armas and the La Moneda changing of the guard.
  • Museo de la Memoria, the Cementerio General and Bellas Artes.
  • Walking Parque Forestal, Lastarria and the Bellavista street art.
  • Hiking up Cerro San Cristóbal on foot instead of taking the funicular (about an hour from Pío Nono).
  • Free walking tours from the centre, which run on tips. Give €10-15.
  • Barrio Yungay and Barrio Brasil, with faded mansions, cheap bars and Plaza Brasil.

Santiago itinerary: 3 days

Day 1: Historic centre and Lastarria

Morning: Start at La Moneda, walk up to the Plaza de Armas and the cathedral, then spend a couple of hours in the Museo Precolombino. Afternoon: Cross to the Mercado Central for a look, then lunch at La Vega Central or a terremoto at La Piojera. Walk back along the Mapocho to Bellas Artes and Parque Forestal. Evening: Climb Cerro Santa Lucía for sunset, then dinner and pisco sours in Lastarria.

Day 2: Bellavista, San Cristóbal and Providencia

Morning: Visit La Chascona early, then take the funicular up Cerro San Cristóbal. Afternoon: Ride the teleférico down towards Providencia, wander Barrio Italia, and go up Sky Costanera if the sky is clear. Evening: Dinner in Bellavista or Barrio Italia, with drinks around Patio Bellavista or the more local bars on Constitución.

Day 3: Day trip

Morning: Take an early bus to Valparaíso and spend the day on the hills, riding the ascensores and visiting La Sebastiana. Evening: Return to Santiago and eat late, like Chileans do. Wine lovers can swap Valparaíso for a Maipo or Casablanca vineyard. If you have a fourth day, use it for the Museo de la Memoria and the Cementerio General.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Eating at the Mercado Central: the building is lovely, but the central restaurants have aggressive touts, inflated prices and average seafood. Eat at the small stalls on the edges, or go to La Vega instead.
  • Concha y Toro's standard tour: it's polished and crowded, and the "Casillero del Diablo" show is pure marketing. Smaller wineries in Maipo or Casablanca give you a better tasting for similar money.
  • Sky Costanera on a hazy day: it's expensive for a view of smog. San Cristóbal is cheaper and more beautiful.
  • Patio Bellavista: a polished, pricey food court for tourists. Walk a few blocks to the bars on Constitución and Antonia López de Bello instead.

When to visit Santiago

The best months are spring (September-November) and autumn (March-May), with warm days, cool nights and fewer smoggy days. Autumn also brings the grape harvest, with vendimia festivals in the wine valleys. Summer (December-February) is hot and dry, often above 30°C. In February many santiaguinos head to the coast, so the city is quieter and some restaurants close.

Winter (June-August) is cold and grey, and smog is at its worst, but it's ski season in the nearby Andes and hotel prices drop. Around 18 September the whole country celebrates Fiestas Patrias with fondas, empanadas and a lot of chicha. It's fun, but expect closures. Flights from Europe are usually cheapest in the southern winter and late autumn.

Where to stay in Santiago: best areas

  • Lastarria / Bellas Artes: central, walkable and full of cafés, so it's the best choice for a first visit. It can be noisy on weekends and during protests near Plaza Italia (Plaza Baquedano). Expect about €45-120 a night.
  • Providencia: leafy, safe and well connected by metro Line 1, with plenty of restaurants. It's ideal for couples and anyone who wants calm. About €50-130.
  • Bellavista: nightlife, hostels and street art. It's good for younger travellers, though not for light sleepers. About €20-35 for hostel dorms and €50-90 for rooms.
  • Las Condes / El Golf: business hotels, malls and very safe streets, but far from the historic centre. About €80-180.
  • Barrio Brasil / Yungay: cheap and full of character, but sketchier at night. It's for budget travellers who don't mind that. About €30-60.

Our tip: foreign tourists who pay for a hotel in US dollars or with a foreign card can often avoid the 19% VAT (IVA). Ask at check-in.

What to eat in Santiago

Chilean food is hearty and seafood-heavy, and the portions are big. These are the dishes to order:

  • Empanada de pino: baked, filled with beef, onion, egg and an olive. Watch out for the olive stone.
  • Completo italiano: a hot dog buried in avocado, tomato and mayo. Fuente Alemana in Plaza Italia is the classic spot for one, and for the lomito sandwiches.
  • Pastel de choclo: a sweetcorn crust over beef and chicken, best in summer.
  • Cazuela: a broth with meat, potato, pumpkin and corn, ideal in winter.
  • Machas a la parmesana and caldillo de congrio: razor clams with cheese, and Neruda's favourite conger eel soup.
  • Chorrillana: chips piled with beef, onion and fried eggs. It's a dish to share.
  • Mote con huesillo: a street drink of husked wheat and dried peaches, sold from carts in summer.
  • Pisco sour: the Chilean version, lighter than the Peruvian one. Don't start that argument with a local.

A menú del día lunch (starter, main and drink) costs about €6-10 (CLP 6,000-10,000) in the centre. A mid-range dinner with wine runs €20-35 per person, and a carménère bottle in a restaurant starts around €12. A tip of 10% is suggested on the bill, so you'll be asked "¿le incluyo la propina?"

Airport to city centre and getting around

Arturo Merino Benítez Airport (SCL) is about 17 km west of the centre. Your options:

  • Airport bus: Centropuerto and Turbus run frequently to Los Héroes and Pajaritos metro stations. They take 30-45 minutes and cost about €2.50-3 (CLP 2,500-3,000). It's the cheapest and easiest option.
  • Shared shuttle: counters in arrivals offer door-to-door shared vans for about €10-15 per person.
  • Official taxi: book from the counters inside, not from people approaching you. Expect €25-35 to Providencia, 30-60 minutes depending on traffic.
  • Uber and Cabify: these work and are usually cheaper. The pickup point is outside on the departures level.

In the city, the metro is clean, fast and the best way to move. Buy a Bip! card (about €1.50-2) and top it up. Each ride costs roughly €0.80-1, with higher fares at peak times, and the card also works on the red RED buses. There's no tourist travel pass worth buying. Avoid the metro at rush hour, when Line 1 is crushingly full. Many central areas are walkable.

How much a trip to Santiago costs

  • Shoestring: €45-60 a day. That covers a hostel dorm (€15-25), menú del día lunches, completos and street food, metro, and free museums and hills.
  • Mid-range: €100-160 a day. That covers a comfortable hotel in Providencia or Lastarria (€60-100), a sit-down dinner with wine, paid sights like La Chascona, and taxis at night.
  • Extras: a Valparaíso day trip costs €20-35 including the bus and lunch. Vineyard tours run €25-80 depending on transport.

Day trips from Santiago

Valparaíso and Viña del Mar

Colourful hills, old funiculars, street art and Neruda's La Sebastiana make Valparaíso the best day trip. Buses from Terminal Pajaritos or Terminal Alameda (Turbus, Pullman) leave constantly, take about 1h45 and cost about €6-9 each way. Viña del Mar's beaches are a short local metro ride away, but Valparaíso is the reason to go.

Maipo Valley wineries

The Maipo Valley is reachable by metro and taxi or Uber. Concha y Toro is near Las Mercedes station (Line 4), and Cousiño Macul is inside the city. Plan for a half day and book tastings in advance.

Casablanca Valley

This valley produces cool-climate whites like sauvignon blanc and sits about an hour's drive towards the coast. A tour or rental car makes it easiest, and you can combine it with Valparaíso.

Cajón del Maipo

The Cajón del Maipo is a mountain canyon about 1.5-2 hours away, good for hiking, rafting and hot springs. The road up to Embalse El Yeso, a turquoise reservoir, is the highlight. Go with a tour or a 4x4 in winter.

Valle Nevado and Farellones

These ski resorts are about 1.5-2 hours up a road with 40 hairpin bends. They're open roughly June-September, and shuttles leave from Las Condes.

Common mistakes and scams

  • The mustard or bird-poo trick: someone "helpfully" cleans a stain off you while a partner takes your bag. Walk away fast.
  • Phone snatching: thieves on foot or motorbike grab phones from people using them on the pavement, especially around the Plaza de Armas, the Alameda and Bellavista. Step into a shop to check maps.
  • Taxi touts at the airport: ignore anyone approaching you inside arrivals and use the official counters or the bus.
  • Unmetered taxis: in the city, use apps or make sure the meter is running.
  • Walking the centre at night: the historic centre empties after 8 pm. Stick to Lastarria, Providencia or Bellavista's main streets.
  • Underestimating the sun and the dry air: summer UV is fierce, so bring sunscreen and water for the hills.
  • Getting short-changed: Chilean notes look alike, so check your change, especially between the 10,000 and 1,000 peso notes.

Santiago FAQ

How many days do you need in Santiago?

Three days is ideal: two to see the city and one for a day trip to Valparaíso or the wine valleys. Two days is enough if you skip the excursions.

Is Santiago safe for tourists?

Mostly yes. Petty theft is common but violent crime against tourists is rare. Watch your phone in the centre, and use apps for taxis at night.

What is Santiago, Chile, best known for?

Its setting at the foot of the Andes, its wine, Neruda's houses and its role in Chile's turbulent 20th-century history.

Can I use US dollars or euros in Santiago?

Not really. Pay in Chilean pesos or by card, which is widely accepted. Change money at casas de cambio on Agustinas street, which offer better rates than the airport.

Do I need to speak Spanish in Santiago?

It helps a lot, because English is limited outside hotels and tourist spots. Chilean Spanish is fast and full of slang, so even fluent speakers struggle at first.

Is Valparaíso worth a day trip from Santiago?

Yes. It's the most rewarding day trip, cheap and easy by bus, and very different from the capital.

What is the best month to visit Santiago?

March-April and October-November, for mild weather, clearer skies and the grape harvest in autumn.
