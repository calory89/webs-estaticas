Boston is small, walkable and old by American standards. Most of what you want to see sits within a few square kilometres, linked by brick pavements, a creaky but useful subway called the T, and a river with Cambridge on the other side. It is a city of students, sports fans and seafood, and it takes its revolutionary history very seriously. It is also one of the most expensive cities in the US for hotels, which matters more to your budget than anything else.

It suits history fans, museum people, walkers and anyone who likes oysters. Families do well here too, and it makes an easy add-on to a New York trip (about 4 hours by Amtrak or bus). Three days is the right amount: two for the city and Cambridge, one for a day trip or a slower museum day. Two days works if you are disciplined. A week is too long unless you are heading out to Cape Cod.

What to see in Boston: the places worth your time

The Freedom Trail

The Freedom Trail is a 4 km red-brick line on the pavement linking 16 historic sites, from Boston Common to Bunker Hill in Charlestown. Walking it yourself is free; the costumed guided tours (about €18 / $20) cover only the first half. Allow 3-4 hours with stops. Our tip: do it in sections over two days rather than as one slog, and start early before the tour groups clog the narrow North End streets.

Boston Common and the Public Garden

The Common is the oldest public park in the US, a big open lawn with the Frog Pond (skating in winter). Next door, the Public Garden is the prettier one: weeping willows, a lagoon and the "Make Way for Ducklings" bronze statues kids climb on. Both are free. The Swan Boats run spring to early autumn for about €5 / $6 and take 15 minutes, which is exactly long enough.

Beacon Hill

Gas lamps, brick townhouses and steep lanes make this the postcard Boston. Acorn Street is the famous cobbled one, and it is tiny, so expect a queue of people photographing it. Charles Street at the bottom has antique shops and cafés. Give it an hour, free, and go early in the morning or around sunset for the light.

The North End

Boston's Italian quarter is the oldest neighbourhood in the city and the best place to eat on a budget-ish. See the Old North Church (the "one if by land, two if by sea" lanterns, about €9 / $10 for the behind-the-scenes tour) and the Paul Revere House (about €6 / $6, 30 minutes, small inside). Hanover Street is the main drag; the side streets like Salem and North Street are where the better spots hide.

Charlestown: USS Constitution and Bunker Hill

The USS Constitution, nicknamed "Old Ironsides", is the oldest commissioned warship still afloat and it is free to board (bring ID, there is security). The museum next door asks for a donation. Up the hill, the Bunker Hill Monument is free and has 294 steps with a decent view at the top. Allow 2 hours total. Walk over the Charlestown Bridge from the North End or take the ferry from Long Wharf for a harbour view.

Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum

If you only do one museum, make it this one. A collector built a Venetian-style palazzo, filled it with Titian, Rembrandt and Sargent, and left instructions that nothing could ever be moved. The empty frames from the famous 1990 heist still hang on the walls. Tickets are about €20 / $22, 2 hours. Anyone named Isabella gets in free, as does anyone on their birthday.

Museum of Fine Arts

The MFA is huge and excellent: Egyptian galleries, a strong Impressionist collection and the Art of the Americas wing with Paul Revere's silver and Sargent portraits. Tickets are about €27 / $30, and you need at least 3 hours. It is a 10-minute walk from the Gardner, so pair them, but do not try to do both thoroughly in one afternoon.

Boston Public Library and Copley Square

The McKim Building on Copley Square is free and has Bates Hall, a long reading room with green lamps and a barrel-vaulted ceiling. Head to the courtyard for a quiet sit. Across the square, Trinity Church (about €11 / $12) is worth a look inside. Allow an hour for both.

Harvard and Cambridge

Take the Red Line to Harvard Square and walk through Harvard Yard (free). The student-led tours are fine but you can do it yourself. Rub the John Harvard statue's shoe if you must, though students have, let's say, other uses for it at night. The Harvard Museum of Natural History (about €14 / $15) has the glass flowers collection, which is stranger and better than it sounds. MIT is two stops away on the Red Line if you want the contrast.

Fenway Park

The oldest ballpark in Major League Baseball, home of the Red Sox and the Green Monster wall. A game is the real experience (bleacher seats from about €25-45 / $28-50 for weaker matchups, much more against the Yankees). The 1-hour tour runs year-round for about €23 / $25. Even if you do not care about baseball, the atmosphere on a summer evening on Lansdowne Street is worth it.

Seaport and the ICA

The Seaport is new Boston: glass towers, restaurants and harbour walks. The Institute of Contemporary Art sits on the water with a cantilevered gallery hanging over the harbour (about €18 / $20, free on Thursday evenings, check the details). Walk the Harborwalk back towards downtown for skyline views.

Charles River Esplanade

A riverside park running along the Back Bay, full of runners, sailing boats and people on the docks at sunset. Free. The best view of the Boston skyline and Beacon Hill is actually from the Cambridge side, on the Longfellow Bridge (Red Line runs over it) or along Memorial Drive.

Boston Harbor Islands

From late spring to early autumn, ferries leave Long Wharf for Spectacle Island and Georges Island (about €23 / $25 return, 20-45 minutes). Georges has Fort Warren, a Civil War fort you can wander through freely. A half-day out here feels like a different city. Check the schedule, it changes by month.

Things to do in Boston

Once you have ticked off the sights, these are the things that make Boston feel like a place rather than a museum.

  • Eat oysters at a raw bar. Local oysters from Duxbury, Wellfleet and Island Creek are the thing. Many places do happy-hour deals of roughly €1-2 / $1-2 per oyster on weekdays.
  • Watch a game. Red Sox at Fenway in summer, Celtics or Bruins at TD Garden in winter. Locals take this very seriously, so pick a side quietly.
  • Kayak or paddleboard on the Charles. Rentals operate in summer from Kendall Square and other spots, about €20-30 / $22-35 an hour.
  • Walk the Rose Kennedy Greenway. A park built over the old elevated highway, with food trucks, a carousel and fountains, linking the North End to Chinatown.
  • Browse Newbury Street. Back Bay's main shopping street, with brownstones turned into boutiques. Better for strolling than buying.
  • See a show in Cambridge. Club Passim in Harvard Square for folk music, or the American Repertory Theater for serious stuff.
  • Take a whale watch. Boats leave the waterfront for Stellwagen Bank from spring to autumn, about €60-70 / $65-75, 3-4 hours. Sightings are very likely in season, but seasickness is real.

Boston for free (or almost)

A surprising amount of Boston costs nothing, which helps offset the hotel bill.

  • The Freedom Trail walk, Boston Common and the Public Garden
  • Beacon Hill, Acorn Street and the Esplanade
  • Boarding the USS Constitution and climbing Bunker Hill Monument
  • Boston Public Library and its courtyard
  • Harvard Yard and the MIT campus (look for the Frank Gehry Stata Center)
  • The ICA on Thursday evenings
  • Arnold Arboretum in Jamaica Plain, especially in May when the lilacs bloom
  • Castle Island in South Boston, a fort and harbour walk where you watch planes land at Logan
  • The Silver Line bus from the airport (yes, free, more below)

Boston itinerary: 3 days

Day 1: Freedom Trail, North End and Charlestown

Morning: Start at Boston Common, walk the Public Garden, then loop up through Beacon Hill and Acorn Street. Join the Freedom Trail at the State House and follow it past the Granary Burying Ground and Old State House.

Afternoon: Lunch in the North End, then Paul Revere House and Old North Church. Cross into Charlestown for the USS Constitution and Bunker Hill.

Evening: Ferry back from the Navy Yard to Long Wharf, then dinner back in the North End or oysters near Faneuil Hall (not inside it).

Day 2: Back Bay, museums and Fenway

Morning: Copley Square, the Public Library and Trinity Church, then a walk down Newbury Street and Commonwealth Avenue's tree-lined mall.

Afternoon: Green Line E branch to the Fenway area for the Gardner Museum, plus a quick hour at the MFA if you have energy left.

Evening: A Red Sox game if it is the season. Otherwise, dinner in the South End around Tremont Street, one of the best eating neighbourhoods.

Day 3: Cambridge and the water

Morning: Red Line to Harvard Square. Walk the Yard, see the glass flowers, have coffee in the Square.

Afternoon: Red Line to Kendall/MIT, walk the campus, then cross the Longfellow Bridge on foot for the skyline view and drop down to the Esplanade.

Evening: Head to the Seaport for sunset on the Harborwalk and dinner. Swap this day for Salem or the Harbor Islands if you prefer.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Quincy Market's food hall. The building is historic, the food inside is mostly mediocre chain-level stuff at tourist prices. Walk 10 minutes to the North End or to the Boston Public Market (indoor local producers, near Haymarket) instead.
  • The Mike's vs Modern Pastry cannoli war. Mike's has the queue and the famous white box. Modern Pastry, down the street, fills cannoli to order and the shells stay crisp. Go to Modern, or skip both for Bova's late at night.
  • Cheers. The bar on Beacon Street inspired the TV show's exterior and that is about it. Take the photo outside and drink elsewhere.
  • Duck Tours. Fun for kids, pricey for adults (about €45-50 / $50-55) and you see little that you cannot walk past. A harbour ferry gives you better views for a fraction.

When to visit Boston

The best months are May-June and September-October. Late spring brings blossom and mild weather; autumn brings crisp days and the famous New England foliage, peaking around mid-October. Expect 15-25°C in both.

Summer is warm and humid (often 28-32°C) and busy, but good for harbour trips and baseball. Winter is cold, with snowstorms that can shut things down, and temperatures well below zero. It is also when hotels are cheapest, sometimes half the autumn price. Avoid graduation weeks in May and the Boston Marathon weekend in April if you are on a budget, as rooms sell out and prices spike. Late October also overlaps with university parents' weekends, so book early.

Where to stay in Boston: best areas

Hotels are the biggest cost. Book early and consider Cambridge or areas a few T stops out.

  • Back Bay: central, elegant, close to Newbury Street and the Common. Best for first-timers who can afford it. About €250-450 / $280-500 a night.
  • Downtown and the Waterfront: walkable to the Freedom Trail and North End, good for short trips. About €220-400 / $250-450.
  • Seaport: modern hotels, close to the airport via the Silver Line, but quiet at night and a bit corporate. About €230-400 / $260-450.
  • Cambridge: student energy, good bars and restaurants, 10 minutes to downtown on the Red Line. About €200-350 / $220-390.
  • Chinatown and around: the budget base. HI Boston hostel has dorm beds from about €55-80 / $60-90. Central and well connected.

What to eat in Boston

Seafood rules. A lobster roll comes two ways: cold with mayo or warm with butter (the Connecticut style). Neptune Oyster in the North End does the best-known one, with a queue to match; Row 34 in the Seaport and Fort Point is a good alternative. Expect about €30-40 / $35-45 for a proper one.

New England clam chowder is the creamy white one, never the tomato kind. Order it in a bread bowl only if you want lunch to end there. Fried clams with the bellies on are a local favourite. For dessert, try Boston cream pie at the Omni Parker House, where it was invented, and a cannoli in the North End.

Beyond seafood: dim sum in Chinatown, Italian in the North End, the North Shore roast beef sandwich (rare beef, sauce, mayo, cheese) and good local craft beer from breweries like Trillium and Harpoon.

A casual lunch costs about €15-25 / $17-28 per person. A mid-range dinner with a drink runs about €45-70 / $50-80. Prices do not include tax or tip: add roughly 7% meals tax and 18-22% tip, which surprises a lot of European visitors.

Airport to city centre and getting around

Logan Airport is unusually close, just across the harbour.

  • Silver Line SL1 bus: free from the airport terminals to South Station, about 20-30 minutes. The best budget option by far.
  • Blue Line: take the free shuttle bus to Airport station, then the subway downtown in about 15-20 minutes for about €2 / $2.40.
  • Water taxi: to the Waterfront or Seaport, around 10 minutes on the water, about €18-25 / $20-28. Lovely on a sunny day.
  • Taxi or Uber/Lyft: 15-30 minutes depending on tunnel traffic, about €25-45 / $28-50.

In the city, walk most of the time. The T (subway) has Red, Green, Orange and Blue lines; a single ride costs about €2 / $2.40. You can tap a contactless bank card or phone at the gates. A 7-day LinkPass is about €20 / $22.50 and pays off after roughly 10 rides. Do not rent a car for the city: parking is expensive and the streets are a medieval mess.

How much a trip to Boston costs

  • Shoestring: about €110-150 / $120-165 a day. Hostel dorm, free sights, food trucks and slices, transit only, one paid museum.
  • Mid-range: about €330-480 / $370-530 a day per person sharing a double. Central hotel, one or two museums, a sit-down lunch and a seafood dinner, a game or a harbour trip.
  • The big variable: accommodation. The same room can cost double in October compared with January.

Day trips from Boston

  • Salem: witch trials history, the Peabody Essex Museum and a seaside old town. Commuter rail from North Station, about 30 minutes, or a seasonal ferry (about an hour). October is chaotic with Halloween crowds; go any other month if you can.
  • Concord and Lexington: where the Revolutionary War started, plus Walden Pond and the homes of Thoreau, Emerson and Louisa May Alcott. Commuter rail to Concord takes about 40 minutes; a car helps for Walden.
  • Provincetown, Cape Cod: beaches, dunes and an easygoing artsy town. Fast ferry in summer from the waterfront in about 90 minutes. Doable as a long day trip.
  • Rockport: a pretty fishing village on Cape Ann. About an hour on the commuter rail. Pair with Gloucester for fried clams.
  • Plymouth: Plymouth Rock is famously underwhelming, but the Mayflower II replica and Plimoth Patuxet Museums are worthwhile. About an hour by car or commuter rail.

Common mistakes and scams

Boston is a safe, low-hassle city, so most mistakes here are about money and timing rather than scams.

  • Booking hotels late in autumn. Foliage season, college events and conferences push prices to absurd levels.
  • Forgetting tax and tip. Menu prices are not what you pay. Budget roughly 25-30% on top for restaurants.
  • Driving into the city. Hotel parking can be €45-65 / $50-70 a night.
  • Buying overpriced "official" Freedom Trail maps and tours from street hawkers. The trail is marked on the ground and the visitor centre on the Common has free information.
  • Fake ticket sellers outside Fenway. Buy through the Red Sox site or reputable resale apps, not from someone on Brookline Avenue.
  • Underestimating winter. Bring proper boots and layers between December and March; the wind off the harbour bites.
  • Taking the Green Line as if it were fast. It is a slow streetcar above ground for long stretches. Walking is often quicker across Back Bay.

Boston FAQ

How many days do you need in Boston?

Three days is ideal: two for the main sights and Cambridge, one for museums or a day trip. Two days is enough for the highlights if you move fast.

Is Boston walkable?

Yes, it is one of the most walkable cities in the US. Most central sights are within a 30-minute walk of each other, and the T covers the rest.

Is the Freedom Trail worth it?

Yes, and it is free if you walk it yourself. Do it in two halves if you are short on stamina, and skip paying to enter every site.

What is Boston famous for?

American revolutionary history, Harvard and MIT, seafood like lobster rolls and clam chowder, and its sports teams, especially the Red Sox.

What is the cheapest way from Logan Airport to downtown Boston?

The Silver Line SL1 bus, which is free from the airport terminals to South Station and takes about 20-30 minutes.

Is Boston expensive to visit?

Yes, mainly because of hotels. Many sights are free, though, so staying in a hostel or visiting in winter brings costs down a lot.

When is the best time to visit Boston?

May-June and September-October for the weather, with mid-October best for foliage. Winter is cheapest but cold and snowy.

Can you do Boston as a day trip from New York?

It is possible by train (about 4 hours each way) but rushed. Stay at least one night, ideally two or three.
