Bergen is a compact harbour city wedged between seven mountains and the North Sea. That means two things: you can walk almost everywhere, and it rains a lot. The old wooden wharf of Bryggen gets the postcards. The real pleasure, though, is how fast you can get from a cobbled lane to a mountain trail with views over the fjord, often in under half an hour. It is expensive, like the rest of Norway, but more relaxed than Oslo and far more scenic.

It suits hikers, seafood lovers, rainy-day museum people and anyone using it as the gateway to the fjords. Two days covers the city properly. We recommend 3 days: two in Bergen and one for a fjord trip, because coming this far and skipping the fjords would be a waste of a flight.

What to see in Bergen: the places worth your time

Bryggen

Bryggen is the row of crooked wooden Hanseatic warehouses on the east side of the Vågen harbour, and it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The front facades are what everyone photographs. The best part is behind them, in the narrow timber passages that lead to small courtyards, craft studios and the odd café. Give it an hour, and it is free to wander. Our tip: go before 9am or after 6pm, when the cruise crowds have gone and the alleys smell of tar and old wood instead of sunscreen.

Mount Fløyen and the Fløibanen funicular

Fløyen is the city's front-row viewpoint, 320 metres up, reached by the Fløibanen funicular from a station a block behind Bryggen. A return ticket costs about NOK 200 (around €17) in high season. The view over the harbour, the islands and the sea is the one you have seen in every Bergen photo. Allow two hours if you add a short walk to the lake at Skomakerdiket. Our tip: take the funicular up and walk down (about 40 minutes). You save money and get better angles on the way.

Mount Ulriken

Ulriken is the highest of the seven mountains at 643 metres, and it feels properly alpine compared with Fløyen. The Ulriken643 cable car costs roughly NOK 400 return (about €35). That is steep, and the shuttle bus from the centre adds to it. On a clear day it is worth every krone. On a cloudy day you will see the inside of a cloud. Allow half a day. Our tip: check the webcams before you pay, and if the weather holds, hike the Vidden trail across to Fløyen (see below).

Fisketorget, the Fish Market

The Fish Market sits at the head of the harbour, with an outdoor summer market and an indoor hall (Mathallen) open year-round. Come to look at the king crab, smoked salmon and whale meat on display. Eating here is another matter (see the overrated section). Twenty minutes is plenty. Browsing is free.

Bergenhus Fortress, Haakon's Hall and Rosenkrantz Tower

At the northern end of Bryggen, Bergenhus is one of Norway's oldest fortifications. Its centrepiece is Haakon's Hall, a 13th-century royal banqueting hall, while Rosenkrantz Tower has steep stairs and a rooftop view. The grounds are free. Entry to each building is around NOK 150 (about €13). Allow an hour and a half. Our tip: the grounds are a quiet place to sit with a coffee while the harbour fills up.

KODE art museums

KODE is a cluster of four art buildings along the Lille Lungegårdsvann lake, a few minutes from the bus station. It holds one of the best Edvard Munch collections outside Oslo, plus Norwegian landscape painting by J.C. Dahl and Nikolai Astrup. One ticket covers all the buildings for about NOK 175 (around €15). It is the perfect rainy-day plan. Allow two to three hours.

The Hanseatic Museum and Schøtstuene

The Hanseatic Museum tells the story of the German merchants who ran Bryggen's cod trade for four centuries. The original museum building is under long restoration, so check what is open. The Schøtstuene assembly rooms, where the merchants ate and kept warm, are the part to see. Expect about NOK 150-200 (€13-17) and an hour.

St Mary's Church (Mariakirken)

Just behind Bryggen, St Mary's is Bergen's oldest surviving building, a Romanesque stone church from the 12th century used by the Hanseatic merchants. It has been under restoration in recent years, so check whether it has reopened before you plan around it. Even from outside, the twin towers are worth a two-minute detour.

Nordnes and Nordnesparken

Nordnes is the peninsula on the west side of the harbour, full of white wooden houses and steep lanes. At its tip, Nordnesparken looks over the fjord, with a totem pole gifted by Seattle and an outdoor seawater pool (Nordnes Sjøbad) where locals swim year-round. It is free and gets a fraction of Bryggen's crowds. Allow an hour or two, longer at sunset.

Bergen Aquarium

Also on the tip of Nordnes, the Aquarium is a solid choice with kids or in a downpour: penguins, sea lions, and a decent look at Norway's coastal fish. Tickets run about NOK 350 (around €30) for adults, which is pricey for what you get. Allow two hours.

Troldhaugen, Edvard Grieg's home

Troldhaugen is the composer's lakeside villa about 20 minutes south of the centre on the Bybanen light rail (stop Hop, then a 20-minute walk). You get the house, his tiny composing hut and a concert hall. Entry is around NOK 150-200. In summer, lunchtime piano recitals are the reason to go. Allow half a day.

Fantoft Stave Church

Fantoft is a reconstruction of a 12th-century wooden stave church, rebuilt after an arson attack in 1992. It sits in pine woods near the Fantoft stop on Bybanen. Entry is about NOK 80 (€7). It is small, so 30-45 minutes is enough. Combine it with Troldhaugen, since they are on the same line.

Old Bergen Museum (Gamle Bergen)

Gamle Bergen is an open-air museum of around 50 wooden houses moved here from the city, set up as an 18th and 19th-century town in Sandviken, north of Bryggen. It is charming in summer when costumed guides are around and pretty dead outside the season. Allow one to two hours and about NOK 150.

Things to do in Bergen

The best things to do in Bergen happen outdoors, so pack a proper rain jacket and accept that you will get wet.

  • Hike Vidden from Ulriken to Fløyen: about 15 km over open mountain plateau, 5-6 hours, with views of fjords and islands the whole way. Only do it in clear weather with proper shoes, because fog rolls in fast.
  • Take the Mostraumen fjord cruise: a three-hour boat trip from the harbour into the narrow Osterfjord, about NOK 900 (€80). It is the easiest fjord fix if you only have a few hours.
  • Swim at Nordnes Sjøbad: a heated seawater pool plus fjord access for the brave. It is cheap and very local.
  • Kayak the harbour or nearby fjords: several outfitters run half-day paddles from around NOK 700-900.
  • Drink local beer: 7 Fjell Bryggeri's taproom and the bars around Skostredet and Marken serve Bergen craft beer. Expect NOK 120-140 (€10-12) a pint.
  • Catch a concert: the Bergen Philharmonic, one of the world's oldest orchestras, plays at Grieghallen. In late May and early June, the Bergen International Festival takes over the city.
  • Walk the Fjellveien road: a flat path cut into the side of Fløyen above the city, ideal for views without a climb.

Bergen for free (or almost)

Norway punishes your wallet, but Bergen's best views cost nothing if you use your legs.

  • Walk up Fløyen instead of taking the funicular: 45-60 minutes on a well-marked gravel path from behind the funicular station.
  • Explore the alleys behind Bryggen and the Bergenhus Fortress grounds.
  • Wander Nordnes and sit in Nordnesparken at sunset.
  • Browse the Fish Market and Mathallen without buying.
  • Walk the lanes of Marken and Skostredet, and look in at the Johanneskirken, the big red brick church above the city.
  • Ride Bybanen out to Fantoft and look at the stave church from outside the fence, then walk the woods.
  • Buy picnic food at Rema 1000, Kiwi or Coop supermarkets. A sandwich lunch costs NOK 80-100 instead of NOK 250.
  • Tap water is excellent. Never pay for bottled.

Bergen itinerary: 3 days

3-day itinerary for Bergen
DayPlan
Day 1Bryggen, Fløyen and the harbour. Morning: Start early at Bryggen before the cruise passengers arrive, then explore the back alleys and Schøtstuene. Walk to Bergenhus Fortress and Haakon's Hall. Afternoon: Look around the Fish Market, then grab lunch away from the stalls. Take Fløibanen up (or hike), wander to Skomakerdiket lake and walk down through the residential lanes. Evening: Dinner at Pingvinen on Vaskerelven for traditional Norwegian food at fair prices, then a beer around Skostredet.
Day 2Ulriken or museums, then Nordnes. Morning: If it is clear, take Ulriken643 and either come back down or hike Vidden to Fløyen (in which case it becomes a full day). If it is raining, spend the morning in KODE. Afternoon: Ride Bybanen south to Troldhaugen and Fantoft, or walk across to Nordnes and the Aquarium. Evening: Sunset in Nordnesparken, then dinner at a fish restaurant on the harbour such as Enhjørningen in Bryggen if you want to splurge.
Day 3A fjord day. Morning to evening: Do the Norway in a Nutshell route (train to Myrdal, the Flåm Railway down to Flåm, the Nærøyfjord boat to Gudvangen, bus to Voss, train back). It takes 10-12 hours. For a shorter day, do the Mostraumen cruise in the morning and spend the afternoon in Sandviken and Gamle Bergen.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Eating at the Fish Market: the seafood platters often cost NOK 400-700 (€35-60) and prices are sometimes per 100 grams, which catches people out. Instead, eat fish soup at a proper restaurant or buy shrimp at a supermarket for a harbour picnic.
  • Fløibanen at midday on a cruise day: queues can hit 45 minutes. Instead, go first thing or in the evening, or just walk up.
  • Ulriken in cloud: you pay about €35 to see grey. Instead, check the webcam and switch to KODE if it is socked in.
  • Bryggen's front-row souvenir shops: they are full of trolls and overpriced wool. Instead, look for the craft workshops in the back passages, or buy knitwear at Dale of Norway or Oleana stores in town if you want the real thing.

When to visit Bergen

The best time is May to early June or September: long days, fewer cruise ships and slightly lower hotel prices. July and August are warmest (often 15-20°C) and busiest, with several cruise ships a day and peak hotel rates.

Rain is not a question of if but how much. Bergen gets well over 200 rainy days a year, with autumn the wettest. Winter (November to March) is dark, damp and around 0-5°C, but it is the cheapest time, and December brings a Christmas market and the famous gingerbread town. Cruise season runs roughly April to October, so check the cruise schedule online and plan Bryggen for days with fewer ships.

Where to stay in Bergen: best areas

  • Bryggen and Sentrum: best for first-timers who want to walk everywhere. Expect €150-250 a night in summer for a mid-range hotel.
  • Nordnes: quieter, residential and close to the harbour, mostly apartments. Expect €120-200.
  • Around the bus and train stations (Nygård side): the most practical choice for fjord trips and the airport bus. Chain hotels run €110-180, and hostels like Marken Gjestehus or YMCA Bergen have dorm beds from €40-60.
  • Sandviken: wooden houses north of Bryggen, a 15-minute walk out, with better-value apartments from €100-160.
  • Møhlenpris: near the university and Nygårdsparken, studenty and relaxed, with rentals from €90-150.

What to eat in Bergen

Bergen eats fish, and the dish to order is Bergensk fiskesuppe: a creamy, slightly sweet fish soup with vegetables and white fish. Expect NOK 200-250 (about €17-22) a bowl. Also try fiskekaker (fish cakes) from a fishmonger, and the city's signature pastry, the skillingsbolle, a giant cinnamon bun. Godt Brød and Baker Brun are reliable bakeries at about NOK 40-50 each.

For something heartier, Thursday is traditionally raspeballer day (potato dumplings with salted meat and swede mash), and Pingvinen serves it along with meatballs and other homestyle food. In winter, look for pinnekjøtt (salted, dried lamb ribs) and lutefisk if you are curious. Fresh shrimp, smoked salmon and brunost (sweet brown cheese) make an excellent picnic.

A sit-down lunch costs NOK 200-300 (€17-26). Dinner with a main and a drink runs NOK 400-700 (€35-60) at a normal restaurant, and much more for upscale harbour fish places. A beer is NOK 120-140. Takeaway alcohol stronger than 4.7% is sold only at Vinmonopolet, which closes early and is shut on Sundays.

Airport to city centre and getting around

The cheapest option from Flesland airport is the Bybanen light rail (line 1) to Byparken in the centre. It takes about 45 minutes and costs around NOK 45-50 (€4) with the Skyss app. The Flybussen airport bus is faster, around 30 minutes, and costs about NOK 200 (€17) one way, with stops at the main hotels. A taxi costs NOK 500-700 (€45-60).

The centre is walkable, though hilly. Buses and Bybanen are run by Skyss: buy tickets in the Skyss Billett app, and consider a 24-hour or 7-day ticket if you plan several rides. The Bergen Card (from about NOK 400 for 24 hours) includes city buses and Bybanen plus free or discounted entry to many museums. It pays off if you do KODE, Bergenhus and an outlying museum on the same day.

Day trips from Bergen

  • Norway in a Nutshell (Flåm and Nærøyfjord): train to Myrdal, the Flåm Railway, a boat through the UNESCO-listed Nærøyfjord and a bus back via Voss. It takes 10-12 hours. You can book the package or the legs separately, which can be slightly cheaper.
  • Mostraumen fjord cruise: a three-hour round trip from Bergen harbour. Easiest and shortest.
  • Hardangerfjord: take a bus to Norheimsund (about 1.5 hours) for the Steinsdalsfossen waterfall, which you can walk behind, plus orchards and fjord views. It is a full day.
  • Voss: about 1 hour 15 minutes by train, for lakes, adventure sports and hiking. It is good if you want mountains without a tour.
  • Rosendal: an express boat south to a small fjord village with a 17th-century barony manor. It is a full day, best in summer.

Common mistakes and scams

Bergen is very safe, and outright scams are rare. Most of the money you lose here comes from bad planning.

  • Bad rain gear: umbrellas die in Bergen wind. Bring a hooded waterproof jacket and shoes that can get wet.
  • Price per 100g at the Fish Market: always ask for the full price before they weigh your king crab.
  • Ignoring the cruise schedule: on days with three or four ships, Bryggen and Fløibanen are packed from 9am to 5pm.
  • Booking the fjord trip last-minute in July: train seats on the Flåm Railway sell out, so book ahead.
  • Hiking in fog without the right kit: people regularly get lost on Vidden. Check the forecast and turn back if visibility drops.
  • Buying cash: Norway is almost entirely card-based, and airport exchange counters give poor rates. You can skip cash completely.

Bergen FAQ

How many days do you need in Bergen?

Two days covers the main sights in the city, and 3 days lets you add a fjord trip. Stay longer only if you plan serious hiking or several fjord excursions.

Is Bergen worth visiting?

Yes, especially if you want fjords. It is the most scenic city in Norway and the natural base for Flåm, Hardanger and Sognefjord trips.

Is Bergen expensive?

Very. Budget at least €90 a day on a shoestring and €220-300 mid-range. Supermarkets and hiking are your best savings.

Is Fløyen or Ulriken better?

Fløyen is cheaper, quicker and closer to the centre. Ulriken is higher and wilder, and worth it only on a clear day.

Can you see the fjords from Bergen?

You can see fjord inlets and islands from Fløyen and Ulriken, but not the dramatic, steep-sided fjords. For those, take the Mostraumen cruise or go to Flåm.

Does it always rain in Bergen?

Not always, but often: more than 200 days a year see some rain. May and early June tend to be the driest months.

How do I get from Bergen airport to the city centre?

Take Bybanen light rail line 1 for about €4 (45 minutes) or Flybussen for about €17 (30 minutes). Taxis cost €45-60.

Can I do Norway in a Nutshell on my own?

Yes. You can book each train, boat and bus separately, which gives you more flexibility and sometimes a lower price. Just book the Flåm Railway early in summer.

What to see in Bergen in one day?

If you only have one day, follow day 1 of this itinerary: Bryggen, Fløyen and the harbour. Morning: Start early at Bryggen before the cruise passengers arrive, then explore the back alleys and Schøtstuene.
