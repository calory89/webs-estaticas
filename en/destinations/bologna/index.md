# Bologna travel guide: what to see, eat and plan in 3 days

## Why go to Bologna

Bologna is the Italian city people tend to skip on the way to Florence or Venice, and then regret skipping. It has the oldest university in the Western world, about 40 kilometres of porticoes, a medieval centre in shades of terracotta and ochre, and what many Italians will tell you is the best food in the country. It is also a real city with students, offices and bicycles, not a museum with a gift shop attached.

It works well for a short break. Everything central is walkable, the trains are excellent, and you can eat very well without spending much.

## What to see in Bologna

### Piazza Maggiore and the Basilica di San Petronio

The main square is where the city sits, argues and drinks spritz on the steps. San Petronio looks half-finished because it is: the marble facade stops partway up. Inside, look for the long brass meridian line set into the floor in the 17th century.

### Piazza del Nettuno

Right next door, the Fountain of Neptune is a 16th-century bronze by Giambologna. Locals call the god "il Gigante". Students are said to avoid walking round it before exams. Nobody admits to believing this, yet people do avoid it.

### The Two Towers

Asinelli and Garisenda lean over the crossroads at the east end of the old centre. You can climb the Asinelli, 498 steps up a wooden staircase, for a sea of red roofs. Book ahead in high season. The Garisenda is the more dramatically tilted one, but it is closed to visitors.

### The Quadrilatero

This tangle of lanes east of Piazza Maggiore, around Via Pescherie Vecchie and Via Drapperie, has been a food market since the Middle Ages. Go before noon, when the fishmongers, cheese shops and fruit stalls are at their busiest. Many shopfronts also sell cured meats and fresh pasta.

### Archiginnasio and its Anatomical Theatre

This was the university's main building from the 16th century. The walls of the courtyard are covered in coats of arms left by generations of students. Upstairs, the carved wooden Anatomical Theatre, built for public dissections, is small and slightly eerie.

### Piazza Santo Stefano

A cluster of interlinked churches, often called the Seven Churches, opens onto a sloping, triangular square. It is one of the calmest corners of the centre, especially early in the morning.

### Portico di San Luca

The longest covered walkway in the world runs about 3.8 kilometres and 666 arches uphill to the Sanctuary of the Madonna di San Luca. It is a proper walk, steady and mostly in shade. The view over the city at the top is worth the effort, and an aperitivo on the way back down is well earned.

### The little window on Via Piella

Bologna once had a network of canals. Today you can glimpse one through a small shuttered window on Via Piella, which looks onto the Canale delle Moline. It takes thirty seconds, it is photogenic, and it surprises people.

## Things to do

- **Take a pasta class.** Learning to roll sfoglia and fold tortellini by hand is the most useful souvenir you can bring home.

- **Walk the university quarter.** Via Zamboni and the streets around it are full of students, cheap bars and bookshops. Via del Pratello, a short walk west, is the street to head for on a Friday night.

- **Climb a tower or a hill.** Choose the Asinelli for a short, steep burst, or San Luca for a longer, slower walk.

- **Do an aperitivo.** Order a glass of local wine or a spritz and the snacks arrive without fuss.

- **Visit the Giardini Margherita** on a warm evening, when half of Bologna seems to be there with a bottle and a picnic.

## Bologna itinerary: 3 days, day by day

Two days covers the main sights, but three is the right number if you want to eat properly and add a day trip.

## When to go

April to early June and September to October are the sweet spots: mild days, long evenings, outdoor tables full. July and August are hot and humid, often well above 30°C. Many family-run places close for a few weeks around Ferragosto in mid-August, so check before you plan a specific dinner. Winter is cold and can be foggy, but the porticoes keep the rain off, and a bowl of tortellini in broth has never tasted better. December has Christmas markets and a festive feel without the crowds of the bigger cities.

## Where to stay

### Around Piazza Maggiore and the Quadrilatero

The most convenient base, with the highest prices and the most noise on weekend nights. Good if you want to walk everywhere.

### University quarter

Lively, younger and cheaper, with plenty of bars. Expect more late-night noise.

### Near Bologna Centrale

Practical for early trains and the airport shuttle, and usually better value. The centre is a 15 to 20 minute walk away.

### Santo Stefano and the southern side

Quieter, more residential, still within easy walking distance of the main sights.

As a rough guide, hostel beds start around €25 to €40, a decent mid-range hotel or apartment runs about €90 to €150 a night, and top-end places go well beyond that. Prices climb during trade fairs, so check the fair calendar if rates look odd.

## What to eat and where

The nickname "La Grassa" (the fat one) is deserved. Look for **tagliatelle al ragù**, the real version of what the world calls bolognese, served on fresh egg pasta and never on spaghetti. **Tortellini in brodo**, small stuffed pasta in clear broth, is the classic comfort dish. **Lasagne verdi** use spinach pasta and a thick béchamel. **Mortadella**, sliced paper-thin, is far better than the packaged slices you may know. For a snack, try **tigelle** or **crescentine**, little breads split and filled with cured meats and soft cheese.

For a quick lunch, the delis and market stalls of the Quadrilatero will build you a plate or a sandwich. For a sit-down meal, the traditional osterie and trattorie in the centre and around the university are the best bet. Book dinner, especially from Thursday to Saturday, and check whether lunch service stops mid-afternoon. For dessert, find a gelateria that stores its gelato in covered metal tubs rather than piled high in bright mounds.

## Getting from the airport and getting around

Bologna's Guglielmo Marconi airport is about 6 kilometres from the centre. The Marconi Express, an elevated people mover, links the terminal to Bologna Centrale in around seven minutes for roughly €10 to €13. The airport bus is slower but cheaper, a few euros, and drops you near the station. A taxi is faster at night but costs noticeably more.

The historic centre is compact and flat, so you will mostly walk. Local buses run by TPER cover the rest, and tickets are inexpensive. Bologna Centrale is a major rail hub: Florence is about 35 minutes by high-speed train, Milan about an hour, Venice about 1 hour 20 and Rome about 2 hours 20. The centre has a limited traffic zone, so do not drive in unless your hotel confirms access.

## Rough daily budget

- **Budget:** €60 to €85 with a hostel bed, market lunches, one pasta dinner and local transport.

- **Mid-range:** €130 to €200 with a comfortable hotel, sit-down meals, tower ticket and a few drinks.

- **Comfortable:** €250 and up, with a good hotel and long dinners.

Day trips by regional train usually cost under €10 each way.

## FAQ

### Is Bologna worth visiting for a weekend?

Yes. Two days are enough for the main sights, the food and a walk up to San Luca.

### Do I need to book the Asinelli Tower?

In spring, summer and on weekends, yes. Tickets are limited and sell out, so book a day or more ahead.

### Is Bologna expensive?

Cheaper than Florence, Venice or Rome. Food is excellent value, and accommodation is moderate except during fairs.

### Can I use Bologna as a base for day trips?

Easily. Modena, Parma, Ferrara, Ravenna and Florence are all reachable by train in one hour or less.

### What is the best time of day to see the centre?

Early morning, before the shops open, when the porticoes are quiet and the light on the red brick is at its best.

### How many days do you need in Bologna?

3 days are enough to see the essentials of Bologna without rushing. The route in this guide is organised day by day.
