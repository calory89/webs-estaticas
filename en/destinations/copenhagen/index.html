Copenhagen in 3 days: what to see, eat and where to stay

🔥 2 deals to Copenhagen🏨 Hotels in Copenhagen

Why go to Copenhagen

Copenhagen is a capital you can actually relax in. It's flat and compact, and the cycle lanes are better than most roads elsewhere. The harbour is clean enough that locals swim in it, and the food scene has moved well beyond the pastry-and-herring cliché. It's expensive, no question, but a long weekend here costs far less than the reputation suggests if you plan around bikes, bakeries and free green space.

What to see in Copenhagen

Nyhavn

Yes, it's the postcard, with its coloured townhouses and wooden boats. Go at 8 a.m. for photos without the crowd, then leave. The restaurants along the quay are overpriced, so eat elsewhere.

Tivoli Gardens

An amusement park from 1843, right beside the Central Station. It's worth it for the atmosphere, the lights after dark and the old wooden rollercoaster rather than for the big rides. Check opening dates, as it closes for part of the year and reopens for Halloween and Christmas.

Rosenborg Castle and the King's Garden

A Renaissance castle holding the crown jewels, set in the city's oldest royal park. Even if you skip the interior, the park is where Copenhageners eat lunch on the grass on the first warm day.

Christiansborg Palace and the Round Tower

Climb the tower at Christiansborg for a free-ish view over the rooftops (the ticket is cheap). The Round Tower (Rundetaarn) is better value: a spiral ramp, no stairs, and a view over the old centre.

Amalienborg and the Little Mermaid

Amalienborg is the royal residence, and the guard change at noon is pleasant if you happen to be passing. The Little Mermaid is small and often surrounded by tour groups. Walk the waterfront there if the weather's good, and don't go out of your way otherwise.

Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek

My pick for a rainy afternoon. Ancient sculpture, French Impressionists and a palm-filled winter garden under a glass dome. It's often cheaper or free on certain days, so check before you go.

Designmuseum Denmark

Danish chairs, lamps and everything that made the country's design reputation. Even people who think they don't care about furniture end up enjoying it.

Christianshavn and Freetown Christiania

Canals, houseboats and the spiral spire of the Church of Our Saviour. Next door, Christiania is a self-governing community with its own rules. Be respectful, and don't take photos in the area around Pusher Street.

Louisiana Museum of Modern Art

About 35 minutes north by train to Humlebæk. A superb collection, a sculpture garden and a view of the Øresund. It deserves half a day.

Things to do

  • Rent a bike. The best way to understand the city. Stay in the lanes, signal when turning and lock it every time.
  • Swim in the harbour. In summer, the harbour baths at Islands Brygge and the Kalvebod Bølge are free and popular.
  • Take a canal boat tour. Touristy but genuinely good for seeing the Opera House, Christianshavn and the new architecture from the water.
  • Climb CopenHill. A power plant with a ski slope and rooftop walking route on Amager. Quirky and very Copenhagen.
  • Browse Torvehallerne. A covered food market near Nørreport station, good for a snack stop between sights.
  • Walk Jægersborggade in Nørrebro. A single street of small shops, ceramics, coffee and a good bakery.

How many days and a suggested route

Three days is the sweet spot. Two will let you hit the main sights but leave you rushed, while four lets you add Louisiana or a beach day.

Day 1: Nyhavn early, Amalienborg, the waterfront walk, lunch at Torvehallerne, Rosenborg and the King's Garden, then Tivoli in the evening.

Day 2: Christiansborg and the Round Tower, a wander along Strøget (skip the shops, enjoy the side streets), Glyptotek or Designmuseum, then dinner in Vesterbro.

Day 3: Christianshavn and Christiania in the morning, a canal boat, then an afternoon in Nørrebro or Refshaleøen, where the Reffen street-food area is a good place to end the trip.

When to go

June to August is the easy season: around 17 to 22 °C, very long evenings and outdoor tables everywhere. It's also the most expensive time and the busiest. May and September are my favourites, with lower prices, decent weather and fewer people, though the odd rainy day is likely. Winter is cold, dark and damp, typically 0 to 4 °C, but December has Christmas markets and Tivoli lit up, and the city's candlelit cafés suit the mood. Pack a waterproof at any time of year.

Where to stay

  • Indre By (Old Town): the most central option, walkable to everything. Pricey, and quiet after dark.
  • Vesterbro: next to the Central Station, with the Meatpacking District (Kødbyen) full of restaurants and bars. Great for food and nightlife, and a bit rough around the station itself.
  • Nørrebro: multicultural, lively and cheaper, with good cafés and the Assistens Cemetery for a quiet walk.
  • Christianshavn: canals, calm streets and a village feel, a short walk from the centre.
  • Frederiksberg: leafy and residential, ideal if you want peace.

As a rough guide, hostel dorms start around 40 to 60 euros a night, mid-range hotels typically run 130 to 200, and top-end places climb well above that. Book early for summer.

What to eat and where

Start with smørrebrød, the open rye-bread sandwiches topped with herring, roast beef or egg and shrimp. Classic places such as Schønnemann or Aamanns do them properly, while Torvehallerne is the cheaper, faster route. Try a flæskesteg sandwich (roast pork with crackling) and a pølse from a hot dog stand, the proper budget meal here.

For pastry, order a kanelsnegl (cinnamon swirl) or a wienerbrød at a local bakery. Hart Bageri and Juno the Bakery are both well known and worth the queue. Dinner in Vesterbro or Nørrebro will give you the best of the new Nordic cooking at fair prices, and lunch menus are usually cheaper than dinner. Tap water is excellent, so you don't need to buy bottles.

Getting from the airport and getting around

Copenhagen Airport (Kastrup) is unusually close. The Metro (M2) reaches Kongens Nytorv and Nørreport in about 15 minutes, running day and night. Trains to Central Station take around 13 minutes. A taxi is rarely worth the money.

Within the city, use a combination of walking, bikes, the Metro and S-trains. Tickets work across zones and can be bought in the DOT Billetter app or at machines. If you plan to visit many sights, the Copenhagen Card can pay off, but do the maths first.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: about 90 to 120 euros, with a hostel, bakery breakfasts, hot dogs and market lunches.
  • Mid-range: about 180 to 250 euros, with a decent hotel, a sit-down dinner and a couple of attractions.
  • Comfortable: 300 euros and up, with a design hotel, tasting menus and taxis.

Alcohol is expensive in bars, so a supermarket beer by the harbour is a local habit and a decent saving.

FAQ

Is Copenhagen expensive?

Yes, among the pricier cities in Europe, especially for restaurants and hotels. Bakeries, hot dog stands, free parks and cycling keep costs down.

Do I need to speak Danish?

No. English is widely spoken, and staff will usually switch when they hear you struggle. A simple "tak" (thanks) is appreciated.

Is Copenhagen safe?

Very. Watch for pickpockets in crowded areas and around the Central Station, and for bikes in the lanes.

Can I pay by card everywhere?

Almost everywhere. Many places are cashless, so you rarely need Danish kroner in cash.

What is the best way to see the city?

By bike and on foot. Most major sights sit within a short ride of each other, and the Metro handles longer trips.
