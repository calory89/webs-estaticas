Belgrade travel guide: what to see, eat and where to stay

🏨 Hotels in Belgrade

Why go to Belgrade

Belgrade is not pretty in the way Prague or Vienna are. It has been bombed, rebuilt and argued over for centuries, and it shows in the mix of Habsburg facades, Brutalist blocks and Orthodox domes. That is exactly the appeal. It is a city where people actually live in the centre, sit in cafés for three hours and eat late. Add cheap flights from much of Europe, low prices and some of the best nightlife on the continent, and a long weekend makes sense.

What to see in Belgrade

Kalemegdan Fortress and park

Start here, where the Sava meets the Danube. The fortress walls, the Roman Well and the Victor statue are fine, but the real reason to come is the view over the confluence at sunset, with half the city doing the same thing.

Knez Mihailova and Republic Square

The pedestrian spine of the old centre runs from Republic Square to the fortress. It is touristy, but worth a slow stroll. Meet-me-at-the-horse is the local habit: the Prince Michael monument in the square.

Skadarlija

A cobbled bohemian street with restaurants that lean hard on folk music and tradition. Come for one drink or an early lunch, when it is quieter and the prices feel less theatrical.

Church of Saint Sava

One of the largest Orthodox churches in the world, white and enormous on the Vračar hill. The interior has been decorated in stages, so go in and look up at the gold mosaics.

Nikola Tesla Museum

Small, on Krunska street, and visited by guided tour in English. The demonstrations with electricity are the highlight, and it takes about an hour.

Zemun and Gardoš Tower

Once a separate Austro-Hungarian town, Zemun has red-roofed lanes, a riverside quay and the Gardoš (Millennium) Tower, which has a great view over the Danube. Walk the quay at dusk and stop for fish.

House of Flowers and the Museum of Yugoslavia

Tito's tomb sits in a quiet green setting in Dedinje, next to the museum that explains the Yugoslav years. It is the best single stop for understanding why older people here get wistful.

Belgrade Cathedral and the Sava waterfront

The Saborna crkva is a calm 19th-century church near Kalemegdan, good for a short stop. Then head to Savamala and the old Beton Hala warehouse, now restaurants on the river.

Things to do

  • Go out on a splav. These floating clubs and bars on the Sava and Danube fill up from about midnight in summer. Even if clubbing is not your thing, a drink on the water at 9pm is.
  • Spend an afternoon at Ada Ciganlija. A river island with a lake, a pebbly beach and bike paths. Locals swim here from June to September.
  • Do the kafana crawl. Traditional taverns with live tamburica or accordion are a living tradition. Znak pitanja (the Question Mark), near Kalemegdan, is one of the oldest.
  • Walk Strahinjića Bana and Cetinjska. The first is a bar-lined street in the centre, the second a former brewery complex now full of clubs and galleries.
  • Shop the markets. Kalenić market in Vračar has fruit, cheese and honey, and is calmer than the central ones.

How many days and a suggested route

Three days is the sweet spot. Two works if you skip the museums. Four gives time for a day trip, for example to Novi Sad.

Day 1: Republic Square, Knez Mihailova, Kalemegdan at sunset, dinner in Dorćol or Skadarlija.

Day 2: Church of Saint Sava in the morning, Tesla Museum, lunch in Vračar, then the House of Flowers. Evening on Strahinjića Bana or at a splav.

Day 3: Zemun and Gardoš Tower, a fish lunch on the quay, and Ada Ciganlija if it is warm.

When to go

Belgrade has a continental climate. Summers are hot, often above 30°C, and the river life peaks then, though the city can feel airless in July. Spring (April to June) and early autumn (September to October) are the best compromise: mild days and terraces open. Winters are cold, around freezing in January, with occasional snow and a gusty wind called košava. It is less pretty, but cheaper, and the kafanas are warmest and best then.

Where to stay

Stari Grad (Old Town)

Best for a first visit: walk to almost everything. Slightly noisy on weekends. Expect hostels from about €12 to 20 a bed and decent hotels from €50 to 100.

Dorćol

Quieter, with good cafés and a more local feel, and a short walk to Kalemegdan. Good mid-range apartments.

Vračar

Residential and leafy, close to Saint Sava and Kalenić market. A good choice if you want sleep and breakfast spots.

Savamala and the waterfront

Best for nightlife and riverside bars. Choose carefully if you are a light sleeper.

What to eat and where

This is a meat-and-bread country, and I mean that fondly. Ćevapi (small grilled minced-meat sausages) come in flatbread with chopped onion and often kajmak, a creamy dairy spread. Pljeskavica is the large grilled patty. Burek, a flaky cheese or meat pie, is a breakfast, best with plain yogurt from any pekara. Try karađorđeva šnicla, a rolled breaded cutlet stuffed with kajmak, sarma (cabbage rolls) in winter and gibanica (cheese pie) for something lighter. Ajvar, the roasted red pepper relish, goes on everything.

For rakija, the fruit brandy, plum (šljivovica) is the classic. Sip it, do not shoot it. Eat at kafanas rather than at tourist menus: look for a daily list on paper and locals at the next table. In Zemun, the quay is the place for grilled fish and river fish soup.

Typical prices: burek and yogurt around €2, a ćevapi portion €5 to 8, a sit-down dinner with a drink €12 to 20, a beer €2 to 3.

Getting from the airport and getting around

Nikola Tesla Airport (BEG) is about 12 km west of the centre. The A1 bus runs to the city centre in around 30 to 40 minutes, and it is the cheapest option. A taxi takes 20 to 30 minutes outside rush hour. Agree on the fare first or use a ride-hailing app, since this is where overcharging happens. Expect roughly €15 to 25.

The centre is walkable. For longer distances there are buses, trams and trolleybuses, which are cheap, and a rechargeable card is easier than buying single tickets. Taxis by app are inexpensive for short trips. Serbia uses the dinar (RSD), not the euro, so keep some cash for markets and kafanas, though cards are widely accepted.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: about €35 to 50 with a hostel, bakery breakfasts and simple meals.
  • Mid-range: about €70 to 100 with a decent hotel or apartment and restaurant dinners.
  • Comfortable: €150 and up, with a nicer hotel, taxis and nights out.

FAQ

Is Belgrade safe for tourists?

Generally yes. Normal city precautions apply: watch your phone in crowded spots, and be careful with unlicensed taxis late at night.

Do I need a visa?

Many nationalities, including most EU, UK, US, Canadian and Australian passport holders, can visit for short stays without a visa. Rules change, so check before booking.

Is English widely spoken?

Yes, especially among younger people and in hotels and restaurants. Learning "hvala" (thank you) goes a long way.

Is Belgrade expensive?

No. It is cheaper than most of Western Europe for food, drink and transport. Accommodation is the main cost, and it rises in summer.

Is the tap water safe?

Yes, Belgrade tap water is generally drinkable.
