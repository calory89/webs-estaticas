Things to Do in Strasbourg: An Honest 2-Day Guide

Strasbourg is a French city that often feels German. The houses are half-timbered, the menus list choucroute and spätzle, and the river Ill wraps the old town into an island you can cross on foot in 20 minutes. It is also a working European capital, home to the European Parliament and full of students, so it feels lived-in rather than like an open-air museum. The cathedral is one of the most striking in Europe, and you see it almost everywhere you go.

Two days is the right amount for most people. That covers the Grande Île (the UNESCO-listed centre), Petite France, a couple of museums and two proper Alsatian dinners. Add a third day if you want to see Colmar or the wine villages. The city suits couples, food lovers, slow walkers and anyone flying cheaply into Basel or Frankfurt. It suits party travellers less, and in December it can be overwhelming for anyone who dislikes crowds.

What to see in Strasbourg: the places worth your time

Strasbourg Cathedral (Cathédrale Notre-Dame)

This is the main sight, and it lives up to it. The pink sandstone façade looks almost like lace, and the single spire made it the tallest building in the world for over two centuries. Entry to the nave is free. Allow 45 minutes inside, longer if you climb.

Our tip: look at it twice. Once from Rue Mercière, where it fills the end of the street, and once at dusk, when the stone turns deep red.

The cathedral platform

The 332 steps up to the viewing platform cost about €8. On a clear day the views reach the Black Forest and the Vosges. It is a narrow spiral staircase, so skip it if you struggle with stairs or tight spaces. Go first thing, because the queue builds by late morning, and allow about 45 minutes.

The astronomical clock

Inside the cathedral, this 19th-century mechanism has a parade of apostles that runs at 12:30. You need a ticket of about €4, and the area fills well before the show. Honestly, the procession is short and you watch it over a crowd. It is worth doing once, but the clock is just as impressive outside show time.

Petite France

This old tanners' and millers' quarter sits where the Ill splits into canals. It has the half-timbered houses, flower boxes and swing bridges that appear on every postcard. Walking it is free and takes about an hour. Between 10:00 and 17:00 it is packed with tour groups, so come at 8:00 or after dinner. Rue des Moulins and the little Place Benjamin Zix are the prettiest corners.

Ponts Couverts and Barrage Vauban

The Ponts Couverts are three bridges guarded by medieval towers at the western tip of Petite France. Right behind them is the Barrage Vauban, a 17th-century dam with a free rooftop terrace. That terrace gives the best free view in the city: the towers in front, with the cathedral spire behind them. Allow 20 minutes, and go at sunset.

Palais Rohan

The bishops' 18th-century palace sits beside the cathedral and holds three museums: Decorative Arts (the state apartments), Fine Arts and Archaeology. Each costs about €7.50. If you only pick one, choose the apartments. Allow about an hour per museum.

Musée de l'Œuvre Notre-Dame

This museum holds the cathedral's original statues, stained glass and medieval drawings, and it is underrated. Seeing the real Synagogue and Church figures up close makes the cathedral make more sense. Entry is about €7.50, and an hour is enough.

Musée Alsacien

Set in old houses on Quai Saint-Nicolas, it shows how rural Alsatians lived: painted furniture, kitchens and costumes. It is small, charming and about €7.50. Allow an hour, and do it on a rainy morning.

Maison Kammerzell and Place de la Cathédrale

The Kammerzell is the carved, dark-timbered house on the cathedral square, dating from the 15th century with later decoration. Look at the façade carvings for five minutes, which costs nothing. Eating there is another matter (see below).

The Neustadt

The Germans built this district after 1871, and it was added to the UNESCO listing in 2017. It is a world away from the timber houses: grand boulevards, the Palais du Rhin, Place de la République and the National Library. Walk from Place de la République along Avenue de la Liberté to the university. It is free, quiet and takes about an hour.

Parc de l'Orangerie

This is the city's main park, with a lake, rowing boats and storks nesting in the trees. Entry is free. It is about a 25-minute walk or a short tram ride from the centre, and it combines well with the European quarter.

European Parliament and the European quarter

The glass Parliament building, the Council of Europe and the Human Rights building sit side by side near the Orangerie. Visits to the Parliament are free but depend on sessions, so check the official site before you go. Even from outside, the architecture is worth 30 minutes. Tram E stops right there.

MAMCS (Modern and Contemporary Art Museum)

This big glass museum sits across the river from the Barrage Vauban. Its collection runs from the Impressionists to today, with a good room on Jean Arp, who was born in Strasbourg. Entry is about €7.50, or €12 for major exhibitions. It also has a rooftop café terrace.

Things to do in Strasbourg

The best things to do in Strasbourg mostly involve water, wine and bread.

  • Take a Batorama boat tour. It costs about €17 for roughly 70 minutes around the Grande Île and out to the European quarter, with an audio guide. It is touristy but genuinely useful for getting your bearings, and the lock at Petite France is fun. Book online in summer.
  • Eat a proper winstub dinner. These small wood-panelled Alsatian taverns are the city's soul. Book ahead on weekends.
  • Cycle the river paths. Strasbourg is one of France's best cycling cities. Rent a Vélhop bike (from about €6 for a few hours) and ride the canal paths, or cross into Germany.
  • Cross the Rhine to Kehl. Tram D takes you over a bridge into Germany in about 20 minutes. Locals go for cheaper groceries and drugstores. The Jardin des Deux Rives park sits on the border.
  • Taste Alsace wine. Order a flight at a wine bar or a pichet of Riesling or Gewurztraminer with dinner. Crémant d'Alsace is the local sparkling wine and costs a fraction of Champagne.
  • Browse the markets. The Saturday food market on Boulevard de la Marne is local and great. The second-hand book market on Place Gutenberg/Rue des Hallebardes area runs on certain days, so check before you go.
  • Visit the Christmas market (late November to late December). It is one of Europe's oldest and is spread across a dozen squares. It is lovely, but it is very busy at weekends.
  • Drink in Krutenau. The old student quarter around Rue de Zurich and Place de Zurich has cheap bars and a younger crowd.

Strasbourg for free (or almost)

A lot of the best of Strasbourg costs nothing.

  • The cathedral interior is free.
  • The Barrage Vauban terrace is free.
  • Walking Petite France, the Neustadt and the river quays costs nothing.
  • The Orangerie park, with its storks, is free.
  • The European quarter is free from outside, and Parliament visits are free when available.
  • City museums are free on the first Sunday of each month, though crowds follow.
  • A bretzel from a bakery costs about €1.50, and it makes a decent breakfast.

The Pass Strasbourg (around €25) bundles discounts, including a museum, the platform and the boat. It is only worth buying if you plan to do most of those.

Strasbourg itinerary: 2 days

Day 1: Cathedral, Petite France and a winstub

Morning: Be at the cathedral when it opens. Climb the platform first, then walk the nave. Afterwards, see the Œuvre Notre-Dame museum next door.

Afternoon: Have lunch around Place Gutenberg, then tour the Palais Rohan state apartments. Walk along the quays to the Musée Alsacien, or take the Batorama boat from its landing beside Palais Rohan.

Evening: Head to Petite France as the crowds leave. Watch sunset from the Barrage Vauban, then have dinner at a winstub. Walk back past the lit-up cathedral.

Day 2: Neustadt, Europe and Krutenau

Morning: Walk Petite France again early, when it is empty, then cross to MAMCS. On Saturday, swap this for the Boulevard de la Marne market.

Afternoon: Walk from Place de la République through the Neustadt toward the Orangerie, then on to the European Parliament. Take tram E back.

Evening: Have tarte flambée and beer in Krutenau, then drinks around Place de Zurich. With a third day, take the train to Colmar.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Eating at Maison Kammerzell and the cathedral square terraces. You pay for the view, and the food is average. Instead, eat at a winstub a few streets away and look at the Kammerzell for free.
  • The astronomical clock show. It lasts a few minutes and you watch it over heads. Instead, see the clock at a quiet moment and spend the time on the platform.
  • The Christmas market on a Saturday. Expect shoulder-to-shoulder crowds and queues for mulled wine. Instead, go on a weekday morning, or try Colmar's market midweek.
  • The little tourist train. It is slow and mostly retraces what you can walk in 20 minutes. Instead, take the boat, which actually gives you new angles.

When to visit Strasbourg

The best months are May, June and September. Expect 18-25°C, terraces open and manageable crowds. July and August are warm and sometimes humid, busy but not crushing. Winters are cold and grey, around 0-5°C.

December is the peak: hotels can double or triple in price, so book months ahead. January to March is the quietest and cheapest time, when you can find rooms at half the December rate, though some days are bleak. Also check the European Parliament's plenary weeks (roughly monthly, Monday to Thursday), when hotels fill with officials and prices jump.

Where to stay in Strasbourg: best areas

  • Grande Île (cathedral, Petite France): the most atmospheric area, and everything is walkable. It suits first-timers and couples. Expect about €110-200 a night mid-range, and much more in December.
  • Krutenau: 10 minutes' walk from the cathedral, with good bars and slightly lower prices, roughly €90-150. It suits younger travellers and anyone who wants nightlife.
  • Gare (station) area: convenient for day trips and airport trains, with chain hotels from about €70-120. It is functional rather than pretty, and a few streets feel scruffy at night.
  • Neustadt / République: quiet, elegant and residential, around €90-160. It is good for longer or calmer stays.
  • Kehl (Germany): just across the Rhine via tram D, and often cheaper, about €60-100. It makes sense if central Strasbourg is booked out.

What to eat in Strasbourg

Alsatian food is hearty, porky and best with a cold Riesling. Here is what to order:

  • Tarte flambée (flammekueche): a thin, crisp base with crème fraîche, onions and lardons, about €10-14. The gratinée version comes with cheese. Share several.
  • Choucroute garnie: sauerkraut with sausages, pork and potatoes, about €20-25. It is huge.
  • Baeckeoffe: meats marinated in wine and slow-baked with potatoes in a sealed pot. Some places need it ordered in advance.
  • Bouchée à la reine and spätzle: a classic winstub pairing.
  • Kougelhopf: a ring-shaped brioche-style cake, best from a bakery in the morning.
  • Bredele: small Christmas biscuits, sold everywhere in December.
  • Munster: the pungent local cheese, served warm or with cumin.

A weekday lunch menu (plat du jour) costs about €13-18. A winstub dinner with wine runs €30-45 per person. Tarte flambée and a beer comes to about €20. Chez Yvonne is a long-standing classic winstub, and the official "Winstub" label helps you spot authentic places. Be wary of any restaurant with photo menus in five languages on Place de la Cathédrale or Rue du Maroquin.

Airport to city centre and getting around

Strasbourg Airport (Entzheim) is small, and the train shuttle to the main station takes about 10 minutes. A combined train-plus-tram ticket costs about €4-5. Most low-cost travellers fly elsewhere, though:

  • Basel-Mulhouse (EuroAirport): take the bus to Saint-Louis station, then a TER train to Strasbourg. Allow about 1h30-2h and around €25-30.
  • Karlsruhe/Baden-Baden (FKB): about 1 hour by shuttle bus or car.
  • Frankfurt: about 2-2.5 hours by direct bus or by train with a change.
  • From Paris: the TGV takes about 1h50 from Gare de l'Est, with fares from about €30 if booked early.

In the city you will mostly walk. The CTS tram network (lines A-F) is excellent. A single ticket is about €2, and a 24-hour pass costs about €5. Buy from machines or the CTS app, and validate every time, because inspectors are common. Bikes (Vélhop) and the cycle paths are superb. Don't drive into the centre: parking is expensive and the low-emission zone has rules for foreign cars.

How much a trip to Strasbourg costs

Shoestring (per person per day):

  • Hostel dorm or budget room in Kehl: €30-60
  • Bakery breakfast and bretzels: €5
  • Lunch menu or tarte flambée: €12-15
  • Free sights plus one museum: €8
  • Tram pass: €5
  • Total: about €65-95

Mid-range (per person, sharing a double):

  • Central hotel: €60-100 per person
  • Café breakfast: €10
  • Lunch menu: €18
  • Winstub dinner with wine: €40
  • Platform, museum and boat: €30
  • Total: about €160-200

Day trips from Strasbourg

  • Colmar: this is the obvious one, with canals in Little Venice and the Unterlinden Museum. It is 30 minutes by TER with frequent trains, about €13 each way. Spend half a day or a full day.
  • Alsace wine villages (Riquewihr, Eguisheim, Kaysersberg): these are some of the prettiest villages in France, but public transport is patchy. Rent a car, join a tour, or combine with Colmar buses. It takes a full day.
  • Obernai: a lively wine town about 30 minutes by train. It is easy and less crowded than Colmar.
  • Haut-Koenigsbourg castle: a restored mountain fortress with huge views. Take the train to Sélestat (about 20-30 minutes), then the seasonal shuttle bus. Entry is about €10-12. Allow half a day.
  • Baden-Baden (Germany): the spa town, with thermal baths at Caracalla or Friedrichsbad. It is about 1 hour by train via Kehl or Offenburg.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Booking December late. Rooms vanish and prices soar. If you are late, look at Kehl or Colmar.
  • Forgetting your Christmas market mug deposit. Mulled wine comes in a reusable cup with a deposit of a couple of euros. Return it to get your money back, or keep it as a souvenir knowingly.
  • Not validating your tram ticket. Fines are steep and inspectors don't accept "I'm a tourist."
  • Pickpockets in crowds. They work around the cathedral, the clock queue and the Christmas market. Use zipped pockets.
  • Walking into a winstub without a booking on Friday or Saturday. The good ones will be full.
  • Cycling on pedestrian streets. Some central streets ban bikes at certain hours, and police do hand out fines.
  • Cash-only surprises at small market stalls. Carry a few euros.

Strasbourg FAQ

How many days do you need in Strasbourg?

Two days covers the main sights comfortably. Add a third if you want to see Colmar or the Alsace wine route.

Is Strasbourg worth visiting?

Yes. It combines one of Europe's great cathedrals, a beautiful walkable old town and some of France's best regional food. It is especially rewarding in spring, early autumn and December.

Is Strasbourg expensive?

It is mid-priced by French standards, cheaper than Paris. Prices rise sharply in December and during European Parliament sessions.

What is Strasbourg famous for?

It is known for its Gothic cathedral, the Petite France quarter, its Christmas market, tarte flambée and choucroute, and for hosting the European Parliament.

Do people speak German in Strasbourg?

French is the main language. Many locals understand some German, and older people may speak Alsatian. English is fine in hotels and tourist areas.

What is the best airport for Strasbourg?

Strasbourg-Entzheim is closest, at 10 minutes by train. Basel-Mulhouse often has cheaper low-cost flights and is about 1h30-2h away.

When is the Strasbourg Christmas market?

It usually runs from late November to late December. Check the exact dates each year, and visit on weekdays to avoid the worst crowds.

Can you visit Germany from Strasbourg?

Easily. Tram D crosses the Rhine to Kehl in about 20 minutes, and Baden-Baden is about an hour by train.
