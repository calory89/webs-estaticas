Valencia is Spain's third city, but it feels smaller and easier than Madrid or Barcelona. You get a walkable old town, a nine-kilometre park built in an old riverbed, a long city beach and the best rice dishes in the country, all at prices that still look reasonable next to the bigger names. It is flat, sunny for most of the year and made for cycling.

It suits food lovers, couples, families and anyone who wants a city break with a beach attached. Three days is the right amount: two for the old town, the park and the City of Arts and Sciences, and one for the beach and the Albufera lagoon. Add a fourth day if you want a day trip to Xàtiva or Sagunto.

What to see in Valencia: the places worth your time

Mercado Central

This is one of the best food markets in Europe, housed in a Modernist building of iron, glass and tiles near Plaza del Ayuntamiento. Go for the building itself, then stay for the jamón, the local tomatoes and the fish counters. Allow 45 minutes to an hour. Entry is free. Our tip: go in the morning on a weekday, because stalls start packing up after lunch and it is closed on Sundays. Central Bar, the counter run by chef Ricard Camarena, is worth a stop for a sandwich if you can get a stool.

La Lonja de la Seda

Right opposite the market, the old silk exchange is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and the finest Gothic civil building in Valencia. The trading hall, with twisting columns that look like palm trees, is the reason to go. You need about 30 minutes. Entry is about €2, free on Sundays.

Valencia Cathedral and the Miguelete

The cathedral mixes Romanesque, Gothic and Baroque, and one of its chapels holds a chalice the Church claims is the Holy Grail. Entry is about €9 with an audioguide, around an hour. Climbing the Miguelete bell tower costs a few euros extra and involves roughly 200 narrow steps, but the view over the rooftops is the best in the old town. Go first thing, before the tower gets crowded.

Plaza de la Virgen

The prettiest square in the city, with the cathedral's back door, the Basilica de la Virgen de los Desamparados and the Turia fountain. On Thursdays at noon the Water Tribunal, a farmers' court that has settled irrigation disputes for centuries, meets at the cathedral's Apostles' Door. It lasts a few minutes and costs nothing. Skip the terraces here for anything but a quick drink.

Torres de Serranos and Torres de Quart

Two surviving gates of the medieval walls, both climbable for about €2 (free on Sundays). Serranos gives you the better view over the Turia park; Quart still shows cannonball damage from the Napoleonic wars. Each takes 20 to 30 minutes. If you only do one, do Serranos at golden hour.

Barrio del Carmen

The old town's most atmospheric quarter: narrow lanes, street art, old palaces and plenty of bars. Walk Calle Caballeros, Calle Alta and Plaza del Tossal, and look out for the graffiti on side streets. Give it a couple of hours, more if you stay for drinks. By day it is relaxed; on weekend nights it gets loud.

Jardín del Turia

After a deadly flood in 1957, the river was diverted and the old bed turned into a nine-kilometre park running through the city. It is the best thing Valencia has: joggers, football pitches, orange trees and 18 bridges overhead. It is free. Rent a bike (about €10-15 for a few hours) and ride it end to end, from Parque de Cabecera to the City of Arts and Sciences, in under an hour.

City of Arts and Sciences

Santiago Calatrava's white complex at the southern end of the park is Valencia's postcard. Walking around the outside is free and is honestly the best part, especially at sunset and after dark when the buildings reflect in the pools. Inside you have the Museu de les Ciències (about €10), the Hemisfèric cinema and L'Umbracle walkway. Allow two hours for the outside plus one building.

Oceanogràfic

Europe's largest aquarium, in the same complex, with underwater tunnels, belugas, sharks and Mediterranean habitats. It is genuinely good and the best pick if you travel with children. Expect three to four hours and about €35-40 per adult. Buy online in advance, as it is usually cheaper and the ticket queues in summer are long.

Museo de Bellas Artes

One of Spain's best fine art museums, with Goya, Velázquez, El Greco and a strong collection of Valencian painters including Sorolla. It is free, rarely busy and sits just across the Turia from the old town. An hour and a half is enough. Most visitors skip it, which is their loss.

Palacio del Marqués de Dos Aguas

The over-the-top alabaster doorway of this Rococo palace is worth a look even if you do not go in. Inside is the National Ceramics Museum, with tiles, ballroom interiors and carriages. Entry is about €3, free on Saturday afternoons and Sundays. Allow 45 minutes.

Ruzafa

South of the station, Ruzafa is the city's most fashionable neighbourhood: brunch cafés, vintage shops, its own covered market and the best concentration of good-value restaurants. Come for an evening, wander Calle Sueca and Calle Cádiz, and eat here.

El Cabanyal and Malvarrosa beach

El Cabanyal is the old fishermen's quarter, with low houses covered in coloured tiles. Behind it, Malvarrosa and Las Arenas beaches are wide, clean and sandy. The beach is free; a sunbed costs around €6-10. Head for the Cabanyal market and a beer at Casa Montaña, one of the city's oldest bodegas, before the sand.

Estación del Norte and Mercado de Colón

Two quick Modernist stops. The main train station, next to the bullring, has a ticket hall lined with tiles and orange motifs; it takes ten minutes and is free. Mercado de Colón, in the Eixample district, is a converted market full of cafés, good for a horchata.

Things to do in Valencia

The best memories here tend to come from eating, cycling and sitting outside, not from ticking off monuments.

  • Ride the Turia by bike and keep going to the beach along the port. Valenbisi, the public bike scheme, works for residents; visitors are better off with a rental shop (€10-15 a day).
  • Drink horchata with fartons, the tiger-nut drink with long sugar-glazed buns, at Horchatería Santa Catalina in the old town (about €4-5). For the real thing, go to Alboraya, where tiger nuts are grown.
  • Take a boat on the Albufera at sunset. The lagoon south of the city is surrounded by rice fields, and a 30-45 minute boat trip from El Palmar costs about €5-8.
  • Eat paella where locals do it, at lunch on a Sunday, ideally on the beachfront or in El Palmar.
  • See Las Fallas if you are here around 15-19 March: giant satirical sculptures, daily firecracker shows called mascletàs at 2pm in Plaza del Ayuntamiento, then everything burned on the last night. Spectacular, deafening and very crowded.
  • Try agua de Valencia, a cocktail of cava, orange juice, vodka and gin, best shared in a jug in El Carmen. It is stronger than it tastes.
  • Browse the Sunday flea market around Plaza Luis Casanova near the Mestalla stadium, or watch a Valencia CF match if your dates line up.

Valencia for free (or almost)

A lot of the best of Valencia costs nothing.

  • The whole Turia park and the exterior of the City of Arts and Sciences.
  • Museo de Bellas Artes, always free, and IVAM, the modern art museum, which has free hours (check the website).
  • La Lonja, the medieval towers and the Ceramics Museum are free on Sundays.
  • Mercado Central and Ruzafa market to look around.
  • The Water Tribunal on Thursdays at noon.
  • Malvarrosa and Las Arenas beaches, and the promenade walk to the port.
  • Street art in El Carmen.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

  • Terraces on Plaza de la Reina. Photo menus, microwaved paella, high prices. Walk five minutes into El Carmen or Ruzafa instead.
  • Paying to go inside every building at the City of Arts and Sciences. The architecture is the show. Pick one, the Oceanogràfic, and enjoy the rest from outside for free.
  • The beachfront restaurant strip with touts. Some places along Paseo de Neptuno are excellent, others live off passers-by. Book a known name such as Casa Carmela or La Pepica rather than sitting wherever someone waves a menu.
  • Bioparc for adults without kids. It is a well-designed zoo, but at around €25-30 your time is better spent on a boat in the Albufera.

When to visit Valencia

April to June and September to October are the best months: warm days around 22-28°C, swimmable sea from late May, and fewer crowds than in summer. July and August are hot and humid, often above 30°C, and many locals leave in August.

Winter is mild, usually 15-18°C by day, with cheap flights and hotels and very few tourists. It is the bargain season. Avoid mid-March unless you are coming for Las Fallas on purpose: hotel prices can triple and the centre is noisy around the clock. Autumn can bring short, heavy rainstorms, so check the forecast.

Where to stay in Valencia: best areas

  • Ciutat Vella (old town, including El Carmen): best for first-timers who want to walk everywhere. Expect about €90-160 a night for a double, hostel beds from €25-35. El Carmen gets noisy at weekends, so ask for an inside room.
  • Ruzafa: for food and nightlife, near the station and 15 minutes' walk from the old town. About €80-140.
  • Eixample (Pla del Remei, around Mercado de Colón): elegant, quieter, close to everything. About €100-180.
  • El Cabanyal and Malvarrosa: for beach lovers and summer stays. About €80-150; the old town is 20-25 minutes by tram or bus.
  • Benimaclet: a student village feel near the universities, on metro and tram lines. Cheapest option, often €60-100.

What to eat in Valencia

Paella valenciana is made with chicken, rabbit, green beans and garrofó (large white beans), never with chorizo. Eat it at lunch: locals consider dinner paella a tourist habit. It is usually served for a minimum of two people, about €15-25 per person. If you want seafood, order arroz a banda (rice cooked in fish stock), arroz negro with squid ink, or fideuà, the noodle version, with alioli.

Also try all i pebre, an eel and potato stew from the Albufera; esgarraet, roasted red pepper with salt cod; clóchinas, small local mussels in season from May to August; and horchata with fartons. Valencian oranges are best in winter.

The menú del día is your best friend: three courses with bread and a drink for about €12-16 on weekdays. A tapas dinner with wine costs €20-30 per person, a proper rice restaurant €30-45. Almuerzo, the mid-morning sandwich with a drink and coffee, is a local ritual worth trying for €6-9.

Airport to city centre and getting around

Valencia airport is about 8 km from the centre. Metro lines 3 and 5 run from the terminal to the centre (Xàtiva or Colón stations) in about 20-25 minutes, for roughly €5 one way including the airport supplement. A taxi takes about 20 minutes and costs €20-25. There is also a cheaper airport bus line, but it is slower.

Inside the city you will mostly walk. EMT buses cost about €1.50 a ride, and trams run to the beach. Get a rechargeable SUMA card for metro, tram and bus. The Valencia Tourist Card (24, 48 or 72 hours, roughly €15-30) includes unlimited public transport, airport metro rides and museum discounts; it pays off if you use the metro to and from the airport and visit a few paid sites. Bikes are the fastest way across the city thanks to a good lane network.

Day trips from Valencia

  • Albufera and El Palmar: the lagoon and rice village, 30 minutes by bus (lines 24 and 25 from the centre) or by car. Half a day, ideally ending at sunset.
  • Sagunto: a Roman theatre and a huge hilltop castle, about 30 minutes by Cercanías train (line C6). Half a day.
  • Xàtiva: an impressive castle stretched across a ridge above a pretty town, about an hour by train. A full day, and take water for the climb.
  • Chulilla: a white village above a river canyon with hanging bridges, about an hour by car. Go on a weekday; on weekends the parking fills up.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Ordering paella at dinner or at a place with pictures of it on a board outside. Real paella takes time, and many good places want you to order in advance.
  • Pickpockets on the metro, around Mercado Central, Plaza del Ayuntamiento and during Fallas. Keep your phone out of back pockets.
  • Bread and appetisers you did not order may appear on the bill. Send them back if you do not want them.
  • Buying Oceanogràfic tickets at the door in high season, when online is usually cheaper and quicker.
  • Leaving bags on the beach while you swim. Thefts on Malvarrosa are common in summer.
  • Booking last-minute for Fallas. Reserve months ahead or prices will be painful.
  • Planning a big Sunday market trip. Mercado Central is closed on Sundays.

Valencia FAQ

How many days do you need in Valencia?

Three days is ideal to see the old town, the City of Arts and Sciences, the beach and the Albufera. Add a day if you want to visit Xàtiva or Sagunto.

Is Valencia worth visiting?

Yes. It combines a historic centre, modern architecture, a city beach and excellent food, and it is generally cheaper and less crowded than Barcelona.

Is Valencia cheaper than Barcelona?

Usually, yes. Hotels, menús del día and drinks tend to cost noticeably less, though prices rise sharply during Las Fallas.

What is Valencia famous for?

Paella, which was born in the rice fields around the city, the Las Fallas festival in March, the City of Arts and Sciences and horchata.

Can you swim at the beach in Valencia?

Yes, Malvarrosa and Las Arenas are clean, sandy city beaches. The sea is comfortable from late May to October.

How do I get from Valencia airport to the city centre?

Take metro line 3 or 5, which takes about 20-25 minutes and costs around €5. A taxi costs €20-25.

Is the Valencia Tourist Card worth it?

It is worth it if you take the metro to and from the airport and use public transport daily. If you mainly walk and visit free museums, skip it.

Where is the best paella in Valencia?

Locals often head to El Palmar in the Albufera or to long-established beachfront restaurants such as Casa Carmela. Book ahead, especially for Sunday lunch.

What to see in Valencia in one day?

If you only have one day, follow day 1 of this itinerary: The old town. Morning: start at Mercado Central for breakfast, cross to La Lonja, then walk to the cathedral and climb the Miguelete.

What to see in Valencia in two days?

With two days, follow days 1 and 2 of this itinerary. Day 1: The old town. Morning: start at Mercado Central for breakfast, cross to La Lonja, then walk to the cathedral and climb the Miguelete. Day 2: The Turia and the City of Arts and Sciences. Morning: Museo de Bellas Artes when it opens, then rent bikes and ride the Turia south.
