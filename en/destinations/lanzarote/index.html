Lanzarote looks like nowhere else in Spain. Its black lava fields, red craters, white villages and green-shuttered windows are mostly the work of the 1730s eruptions and of one man, the artist César Manrique, who persuaded the island to ban high-rises and billboards. It is windy, dry and quieter than Tenerife or Gran Canaria. The beaches are good rather than spectacular, and the landscape is the real reason to come.

It suits couples, hikers, surfers, wine drinkers and families who want sun without the full resort circus. You need 5 days to see it properly, and you need a car. Two days covers Timanfaya and a couple of Manrique sites. Five lets you add the north, La Geria, the Papagayo coves and a day trip to La Graciosa without rushing.

What to see in Lanzarote: the places worth your time

Timanfaya National Park (Montañas del Fuego)

This is the top sight on the island: a national park of craters and frozen lava where the ground a few metres down is still hot enough to cook on. Entry is about €20 and includes a 35-minute coach tour along the Ruta de los Volcanes, plus staff demonstrations where water poured into a pipe shoots out as steam. You can't wander freely, so allow about two hours. Our tip: arrive first thing. By 11am the car queue at the entrance on the LZ-67 can take 30-45 minutes in high season.

Ruta de Tremesana (guided walk in Timanfaya)

This is the only way to walk inside the park, and it is free. Rangers lead a roughly 3 km, two-hour walk through the lava, and it is a very different experience from looking out of a coach window. Places are limited, so book well ahead through the national park's official booking system. Departures are from the Mancha Blanca visitor centre.

Jameos del Agua

Manrique turned a collapsed lava tube in the north into a garden, a saltwater lagoon full of tiny blind white crabs, a pool and a concert auditorium. It is the most photographed of his works, and for once the hype is fair. Allow 1-1.5 hours. Tickets are about €15. Some evenings it opens late with dinner and music, which is more atmospheric than the midday crowds.

Cueva de los Verdes

This is the same lava tube as Jameos, a few hundred metres up the road, explored on a 45-minute guided tour. It costs about €15. There is a clever trick near the end that we won't spoil, so don't let anyone tell you about it. Tall people should be ready to duck. Buy a combined ticket if you plan to visit several Manrique sites (see below).

Mirador del Río

This viewpoint was built into a cliff at the island's northern tip, 475 metres above the strait, and it looks straight down onto La Graciosa. The building is lovely, but the café coffee is not the reason to pay. Entry is about €6. Our tip: the free viewpoint at the Bosquecillo picnic area near Haría gives a similar sweep of the Famara cliffs. Go to the Mirador del Río on a clear morning only, because when cloud sits on the ridge you will see nothing.

Fundación César Manrique (Taro de Tahíche)

Manrique's former home is built into five volcanic bubbles, with sunken rooms, a pool in the rock and a gallery that includes Picasso and Miró works from his collection. It is the best place to understand why the island looks the way it does. Allow 1.5 hours. Entry is about €10. It is just outside Arrecife, so it pairs well with a lunch in town.

Casa Museo César Manrique, Haría

This is the house in the Valley of a Thousand Palms where Manrique spent his last years. It is more personal than Taro de Tahíche: his studio has been left with paint pots and clothes still in place. Entry is about €10 and a visit takes an hour. Combine it with the Saturday craft market in Haría's plaza.

Jardín de Cactus

Around 4,500 cacti grow in an old quarry in Guatiza, with a windmill on the rim. Manrique designed it, and it was his last major project. Entry is about €9 and you need about 45 minutes. It is excellent if you like plants or photography, and skippable if you don't.

El Golfo and the Charco de los Clicos

This half-eroded crater on the west coast holds a lagoon coloured bright green by algae, between a black sand beach and red cliffs. It is free. Walk up the marked path from the car park south of the village for the classic view; it takes 15 minutes. Afterwards, eat fish in El Golfo village. The seafront terraces are touristy but the fish is fresh.

Los Hervideros and Salinas de Janubio

These two free stops sit on the same coast road (LZ-703), just south of El Golfo. At Los Hervideros, Atlantic waves explode through lava caves, and paths take you right above them. Janubio is a chequerboard of salt pans that turn pink and white in the afternoon light. Allow 30 minutes for each, and go late afternoon if you can.

La Geria wine region

Vines grow in pits dug into black volcanic ash, each one protected by a semicircular stone wall. Nobody else farms like this. Drive the LZ-30 between Uga and Masdache and stop at a couple of bodegas: El Grifo (with a small wine museum), Stratvs and Bodega La Geria are well established. A tasting is about €10-20. Order the dry Malvasía Volcánica. The driver should spit.

Playas de Papagayo

These golden coves sit inside the Los Ajaches protected area, east of Playa Blanca, and they are the best beaches on the island. Driving in costs about €3 per car, and the last stretch is a bumpy dirt track. Playa Mujeres is the biggest, Papagayo itself the prettiest. Arrive before 11am in summer, take water and food, and expect wind.

Caleta de Famara and Famara beach

This is a sandy-street surf village under 400-metre cliffs, with a 5 km beach that is wild, windy and beautiful at sunset. It is great for surf lessons. It is not good for swimming, because the currents are dangerous. Have a beer at one of the bars facing the water.

Teguise

The old capital is the best-preserved town on the island, with cobbled streets, colonial houses and the Castillo de Santa Bárbara on the crater above it (it houses a small piracy museum, about €3). Come on a weekday to see the town itself. The Sunday market is another story, covered below.

Things to do in Lanzarote

Beyond the sights, these are the experiences we would actually plan a day around:

  • Hike Caldera Blanca: a free walk of about 9 km return from Mancha Blanca, across lava to a huge white crater you can circle on its rim. Allow 3-4 hours.
  • Learn to surf in Famara: a group lesson costs about €40-50 with board and wetsuit. Beginners use the gentler beach breaks.
  • Dive or snorkel in Playa Chica, Puerto del Carmen: this is the island's dive hub, with try dives from around €60.
  • See the Museo Atlántico: Jason deCaires Taylor's underwater sculpture museum off Playa Blanca. You can reach it only on a dive, and it is a strange, moving place.
  • Cycle the coast: the seafront promenade from Arrecife towards Costa Teguise is flat. Road cyclists come for the climbs to Femés and Haría.
  • Do a wine-and-cheese afternoon: pair La Geria bodegas with the local smoked goat's cheese, which most bodegas sell.
  • Go to a concert in Jameos del Agua: check the programme, as a gig in a lava cave is hard to beat.
  • Climb Montaña Colorada: an easy, free, one-hour loop around a rust-red cone near Masdache, with lava bombs the size of cars.

Lanzarote for free (or almost)

A lot of the island costs nothing but petrol. All of these are free:

  • The coast at El Golfo, Los Hervideros and Janubio
  • The Caldera Blanca and Montaña Colorada hikes, plus the Tremesana walk if you book it
  • Famara beach, the viewpoint above Haría and the Bosquecillo lookout
  • Arrecife's Charco de San Ginés lagoon and the old town
  • The Saturday morning market in Haría
  • Most beaches, apart from the €3 car fee at Papagayo (walking in is free)

If you plan several Manrique sites, buy a combined ticket from the island's tourist centres (CACT). The bundles of 3, 4 or 6 sites save roughly 20-30% over individual tickets.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

The Puerto del Carmen strip. The Avenida de las Playas is a long row of English breakfasts, sports bars and touts. Base yourself there if you want convenience, but eat in the old harbour (La Tiñosa) at the western end, which is far better.

The Timanfaya camel rides. They cost about €20 for a short, bumpy plod. Plenty of visitors aren't comfortable with how the animals are worked. Spend the time on the Caldera Blanca hike instead.

Teguise Sunday market. It is a crush of coaches, cheap sunglasses and imported "local" souvenirs, and parking is a nightmare. Visit Teguise on a weekday and go to Haría's smaller Saturday market for real crafts and produce.

Playa Blanca's town beach. It is small, packed and backed by restaurants. Drive ten minutes to Papagayo.

When to visit Lanzarote

Lanzarote is a year-round destination. Winter days run around 20-22°C, summer around 28-30°C, and the sea never really warms past 22°C. Rain is rare. The constant trade wind is not: summer is the windiest season, which surfers love and beach umbrellas hate. Now and then the calima, a hot, dusty wind from the Sahara, turns the sky beige for a few days.

The busiest and priciest times are Christmas to February (northern Europeans escaping winter), Easter, and July-August, when Spanish families arrive. The best value is in May, June, October and the first half of November, when the weather is warm, flights are cheap and Timanfaya is easier to get into.

Where to stay in Lanzarote: best areas

  • Puerto del Carmen: central, with the most choice and buses. Best for first-timers without a car and for divers. Apartments run €70-150 a night.
  • Playa Blanca: in the south, quieter, close to Papagayo and the Fuerteventura ferry. Good for families and couples. Expect €90-200 for hotels and resort apartments.
  • Costa Teguise: near the airport and Arrecife, windier and more low-key, and good for windsurfing. Prices are €70-140.
  • Caleta de Famara: a surf village with sandy streets and holiday rentals. It is for surfers and anyone who wants the island's wild side. Rentals cost €60-120.
  • Rural houses in Haría, Yaiza or La Geria: casas rurales and boutique fincas, ideal if you have a car and want silence. They cost €80-180.

Arrecife, the capital, is cheaper (€60-100) and has the best local food, but most visitors find it too plain for a whole stay.

What to eat in Lanzarote

Canarian food is simple and built around fish, potatoes and goat. Start with papas arrugadas, small potatoes boiled in salty water until the skins wrinkle, served with mojo rojo (spicy red pepper sauce) and mojo verde (coriander). For fish, order whatever is fresh: cherne (wreckfish), vieja (parrotfish) or sama, grilled or in sancocho, a salted-fish stew eaten with sweet potato and gofio (toasted grain flour).

Also look for lapas (grilled limpets with green mojo), cabra (stewed goat), the smoked goat's cheese, and Malvasía wine from La Geria. For dessert, have bienmesabe, an almond and honey cream. At El Diablo restaurant in Timanfaya, the meat is grilled over volcanic heat: it's a gimmick, but a fun one.

A set menú del día in Arrecife or inland costs about €12-16. A fish lunch at El Golfo or in a harbour runs €20-30 per person. A dinner with wine in a good restaurant costs €35-50. Avoid the laminated picture menus on the resort promenades, where frozen fish is sold as "catch of the day".

Airport to city centre and getting around

César Manrique-Lanzarote Airport (ACE) sits between Arrecife and Puerto del Carmen, which is handy for the whole island. The public bus (Arrecifes Bus / IntercityBus Lanzarote) connects the airport with Arrecife and Puerto del Carmen. The ride takes 15-25 minutes and fares are a couple of euros. Check current lines on the operator's site before you fly. A taxi costs about €15-20 to Arrecife, €20-25 to Puerto del Carmen or Costa Teguise, and €45-55 to Playa Blanca.

For seeing the island, rent a car. Buses link the main resorts and towns but barely touch Timanfaya, La Geria or the north. Rentals cost €20-40 a day off-season and more in August. Local firms such as Cicar and Cabrera Medina include full insurance with no excess, which saves you the upsell at the desk. If you rely on buses, a rechargeable bono card makes each ride cheaper than paying cash.

Day trips from Lanzarote

La Graciosa: a ferry from Órzola takes about 25 minutes and costs €25-30 return (Líneas Romero and Biosfera Express). The island has sand streets, no paved roads and empty beaches. Make it a full day.

Fuerteventura (Corralejo): ferries from Playa Blanca (Fred. Olsen and Naviera Armas) take about 25-35 minutes. Return fares run roughly €50-70. You get the Corralejo dunes and long white beaches. Take a full day, and bring the car if your rental contract allows it.

Isla de Lobos: a small boat from Corralejo takes 15 minutes. The island has a crater hike and a snorkelling lagoon. You can combine it with Fuerteventura, but you need to start early and book the island's free access permit online.

Boat and catamaran trips to Papagayo: they leave from Puerto Calero and Playa Blanca, last 3-5 hours, and cost €50-80 with lunch. Pick a smaller operator over the party boats.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Arriving at Timanfaya at noon. The queues are long, and entry closes before the official end of the day. Go first thing.
  • Leaving bags visible in the car. Break-ins at viewpoints and beach car parks (Papagayo, El Golfo, Los Hervideros) are the most common crime against tourists. Leave the car empty.
  • Buying the rental desk's extra insurance when you could have booked a firm with full cover included.
  • Swimming at Famara or on the north and west coasts. The currents are strong, people drown most years, and red flags mean it.
  • Timeshare and "free gift" touts on the Puerto del Carmen and Playa Blanca promenades. A scratch card that always wins is a sales pitch.
  • Underestimating the sun. The wind keeps you cool while you burn. Wear factor 50 and a hat on any hike.
  • Trying to do the north and Timanfaya in one day. Distances are short, but you will spend your day in queues.

Lanzarote FAQ

How many days do you need in Lanzarote?

Five days is ideal. That covers Timanfaya, the Manrique sites, La Geria, the beaches and a day on La Graciosa. Three days works if you skip the day trip and keep long drives together.

Do you need a car in Lanzarote?

Realistically, yes. Buses connect the resorts and Arrecife, but Timanfaya, La Geria and most viewpoints are very hard to reach without a car or an organised tour.

What is the best part of Lanzarote to stay in?

Playa Blanca suits families and beach lovers, and Puerto del Carmen is the most convenient if you don't drive. Caleta de Famara or a rural house in Haría suits those who want quiet and landscape.

Is Lanzarote worth visiting?

Yes, especially if you care more about landscapes than nightlife. Its volcanic scenery and Manrique architecture are unique in Europe, though its beaches are smaller than Fuerteventura's.

Is Lanzarote warm in winter?

Mostly. Days are around 20-22°C with plenty of sun, but evenings are cool and the sea is fresh at about 19°C. Pack a jacket.

Do you need to book Timanfaya in advance?

The standard entry and coach tour are paid at the gate, so arriving early is what matters. The free Tremesana guided walk does need booking, often weeks ahead.

Is Lanzarote expensive?

It is cheaper than mainland Spanish beach towns for petrol and alcohol, thanks to lower Canary taxes. Accommodation in peak season is not cheap, though. Most visitors spend €70-180 per person per day.

What to see in Lanzarote in one day?

If you only have one day, follow day 1 of this itinerary: Timanfaya and the west coast. Morning: Timanfaya at opening time, then the Tremesana walk if you have a booking.

What to see in Lanzarote in two days?

With two days, follow days 1 and 2 of this itinerary. Day 1: Timanfaya and the west coast. Morning: Timanfaya at opening time, then the Tremesana walk if you have a booking. Day 2: La Geria and the centre. Morning: the Montaña Colorada loop, then a coffee in Uga.
