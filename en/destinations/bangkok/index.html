Bangkok travel guide: what to see, eat and where to stay

🔥 1 deal to Bangkok🏨 Hotels in Bangkok

Why go to Bangkok

Bangkok is loud, hot and sprawling, and it's one of the most rewarding big cities in Asia. Gilded temples sit a few minutes from a plastic-stool noodle stall. A long-tail boat crosses the river while a Skytrain glides overhead. The food alone justifies the flight, and it's cheap enough that you can eat very well on a modest budget.

It's also the easiest hub in Southeast Asia, with onward flights to the islands, Chiang Mai and the rest of the region at low fares. Many people treat it as a stopover and wish they'd booked more nights.

What to see in Bangkok

  • Grand Palace and Wat Phra Kaew. The most spectacular compound in the city, home to the Emerald Buddha. Go at opening time, cover shoulders and knees, and expect to pay around 500 baht. Ignore anyone nearby who tells you it's closed today. That's the classic tuk-tuk scam.
  • Wat Pho. Next door, with the enormous reclining Buddha. It's calmer than the palace and also the home of traditional Thai massage training.
  • Wat Arun. The riverside temple with its steep, porcelain-studded tower, reached by a short cross-river ferry. Late afternoon light is best.
  • Yaowarat (Chinatown). Narrow lanes, gold shops and temples by day. After dark the main road turns into one long open-air restaurant.
  • Jim Thompson House. A collection of traditional teak houses beside a quiet garden, built by the American who revived the Thai silk trade. A good, cool break from the streets.
  • Chatuchak Weekend Market. Thousands of stalls selling clothes, ceramics, plants and snacks. Arrive early, before the heat builds, and take cash.
  • Lumphini Park. The city's green lung. Watch the monitor lizards, join the evening joggers and see how Bangkok relaxes.
  • Talat Noi and the Thonburi side. Old shophouses, street art, small shrines and mechanics' workshops on the river, away from the main tourist circuit.

Things to do

Ride the Chao Phraya Express Boat. It's cheap, cools you down and gives you a skyline you can't see from the road. The orange-flag line is the one most visitors use.

Take a street food walk through Yaowarat or a cooking class, which is worth it if you want to understand why Thai food balances sweet, sour, salty and spicy so precisely. Book a traditional massage, ideally at a proper school rather than a street-corner tout. Cycle through Bang Krachao, the green island across the river, for a surprising hour of silence.

At night, try a rooftop bar in Silom or Sathorn for the skyline, or wander the lively streets of Ari for local cafes and small bars. Muay Thai fans can see a fight at Rajadamnern Stadium.

How many days and a suggested route

Three full days is the sweet spot. Two will feel rushed, and four lets you add a day trip.

  • Day 1, old city. Grand Palace in the morning, Wat Pho, then the ferry to Wat Arun at sunset. Dinner in Yaowarat.
  • Day 2, modern Bangkok. Jim Thompson House, then Siam's malls and the BTS elevated walkways, then Lumphini Park in the late afternoon.
  • Day 3, local side. Chatuchak if it's the weekend, otherwise Talat Noi and Bang Krachao. End with a massage and a long dinner.

A fourth day could go to Ayutthaya, the ruined former capital, by train from Hua Lamphong or the newer Krung Thep Aphiwat station, or to a floating market. The famous ones are tourist-heavy, so go early.

When to go

The best months are November to February, when it's dry and days sit around 30°C with pleasant evenings. It's also the busiest and priciest period.

March to May is brutally hot, with 35-40°C afternoons. Plan outdoor sights for the early morning and spend midday in a cafe or museum. The rainy season runs roughly June to October, usually with a heavy afternoon downpour rather than all-day rain. Prices drop and the temples are quieter. September and October can bring street flooding in some areas.

Songkran, the water festival in mid-April, is wonderful if you like being soaked, and a headache if you don't. Loy Krathong in November brings floating lanterns and candlelit offerings on the water.

Where to stay

  • Riverside and Old City (Rattanakosin). Close to the main temples. Guesthouses are cheap and a few luxury riverfront hotels sit at the top end. Transport is the weak point, so you'll rely on boats and taxis.
  • Silom and Sathorn. Excellent for first-timers: BTS and MRT access, great food, mid-range hotels and good nightlife.
  • Siam and Ratchathewi. Central, with shopping, and ideal if you want to be on the Skytrain.
  • Sukhumvit (Asok to Phrom Phong). Plenty of restaurants and bars, with many hotels at all price levels.
  • Khao San Road area. The classic backpacker base. It's noisy, but hostels are cheap. The quieter lanes of nearby Phra Arthit are nicer.

As a rough guide, a hostel dorm costs very little, a good mid-range hotel is reasonable by European standards, and a five-star riverside room can still cost far less than in most world capitals.

What to eat and where

Start with pad thai at Thip Samai, near the Giant Swing, wrapped in a thin egg omelette. Order khao man gai (chicken rice) at a simple street counter, and boat noodles, small intense bowls, around Victory Monument. Grilled pork skewers (moo ping) are a perfect breakfast on the move.

Try som tam (green papaya salad) and ask for it less spicy unless you're confident. Finish with mango sticky rice when mangoes are in season, roughly March to June. Jay Fai, the Michelin-starred street kitchen famous for its crab omelette, needs a reservation and a good budget, but it's a remarkable experience.

Rule of thumb: eat where there's a queue of locals and the food is cooked in front of you. Stomachs are rarely troubled by busy stalls, and bottled water is all you need to drink.

Getting from the airport and around

Suvarnabhumi (BKK) is the main airport. The Airport Rail Link takes around 30 minutes to Phaya Thai, where you can change to the BTS Skytrain. Taxis use the official public queue, with a meter plus airport fee and tolls. Don Mueang (DMK) handles many low-cost carriers. From there, take the A1 or A2 bus to Mo Chit, or the Red Line train towards Bang Sue.

In town, use the BTS and MRT for long distances, boats for the river and Grab or metered taxis for the rest. Traffic is serious. Tuk-tuks are fun for a short ride, but negotiate before getting in.

Rough daily budget

Backpackers can manage on roughly 1,000 to 1,500 baht a day, which is about 25 to 40 euros. A comfortable mid-range day, with a decent hotel, taxis and restaurant meals, comes to about 3,000 to 4,500 baht. Add more for luxury hotels, rooftop cocktails and private tours. Prices are approximate and shift with exchange rates.

FAQ

Is Bangkok safe for tourists?

Generally yes. The main risks are petty scams, taxis refusing the meter and overcharging, and crossing busy roads. Stay alert in crowded places.

Do I need a visa?

It depends on your passport. Many nationalities receive visa-free entry for a limited stay, but rules change, so check the official Thai e-visa and immigration pages before booking.

What should I wear at the temples?

Cover shoulders and knees, and wear shoes you can slip off easily. Lightweight long trousers or a skirt work well in the heat.

Is tap water safe to drink?

Most visitors stick to bottled or filtered water. Ice in restaurants is usually made from purified water.

Is Bangkok good for first-time Asia travellers?

Yes. It's easy to navigate, cheap and well connected, and English is widely understood in tourist areas. It's a gentle but exciting introduction to the region.
