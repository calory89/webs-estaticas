Why go to the Azores

Nine volcanic islands sit in the middle of the Atlantic, about a three-hour flight from Lisbon and a little over four from Boston. Nobody builds a trip here around museums. You come for crater lakes, cows grazing on cliff edges, hydrangea hedges along every road, and water hot enough to sit in while it rains. And it will rain, at least for ten minutes, usually when you've just parked at a viewpoint.

It's also one of the easier places in Europe to travel slowly. The islands are small, the roads are quiet, and the food is honest. Ryanair and SATA Azores Airlines have made Ponta Delgada reachable cheaply from Lisbon, Porto and a handful of other European cities, so fares are often the least painful part of the budget.

What to see: the places worth your time

Sete Cidades, São Miguel

Twin lakes, one blue and one green, inside a huge caldera. The Vista do Rei viewpoint gets the postcard shots, but go early, before the tour vans, or you'll be photographing other people's selfie sticks. Walk down into the village and along the lake instead.

Lagoa do Fogo, São Miguel

A wild crater lake high in the island's centre, often swallowed by cloud. If it's clear when you arrive, stop everything. If it isn't, wait twenty minutes. The weather changes that fast.

Furnas valley, São Miguel

Steam vents, bubbling mud and the brown, iron-rich pools of Terra Nostra Park, which has a lovely old botanical garden attached. Poça da Dona Beija is the cheaper, more atmospheric alternative, especially after dark.

Nordeste coast, São Miguel

The greenest, least visited side of the island. Stop at the Ponta do Sossego viewpoint and at Gorreana, the tea plantation that has been running since the 19th century. You can walk through the rows and drink a pot for almost nothing.

Angra do Heroísmo, Terceira

A UNESCO-listed town of pastel houses and old forts, with Monte Brasil rising behind it. Nearby, Algar do Carvão lets you descend into an extinct volcanic chimney, which is stranger than it sounds.

Mount Pico, Pico

At 2,351 metres, it's the highest point in Portugal. The climb takes most of a day and needs advance registration, since daily numbers are limited. Even if you skip it, the black-lava vineyards at the island's edge, also UNESCO-protected, are worth a slow drive.

Horta and Capelinhos, Faial

Horta's marina is covered in painted messages left by yachts that crossed the Atlantic. Have a drink at Peter Café Sport, then drive to Capelinhos, where a 1957 eruption created a lunar landscape beside a lighthouse.

Flores

The far-west island is all waterfalls and deep valleys. It takes more effort to reach, which keeps it calm. Give it two nights if you can.

Fajãs of São Jorge

Narrow flats of land at the foot of steep cliffs, reached by hairpin roads or hiking trails. Fajã dos Cubres and Fajã do Santo Cristo are the ones people talk about, and with good reason.

Things to do

  • Whale and dolphin watching from Ponta Delgada, Horta or Pico. Sperm whales and dolphins are around most of the year; blue whales pass mostly in spring.
  • Hiking along the official marked trails. Many are short, and most are free.
  • Thermal bathing at Caldeira Velha or the ocean hot spring at Ponta da Ferraria. Check tide times for the latter.
  • Canyoning, diving and surfing, with operators on São Miguel and Faial.
  • Tasting Pico wine, particularly the crisp Verdelho, and São Jorge cheese straight from the producer.

The Azores itinerary: 7 days, day by day

Three days on São Miguel alone is a fair introduction. Seven days lets you add a second island without feeling rushed. Ten gives you a proper three-island trip.

7-day itinerary for The Azores
DayPlan
Days 1-4São Miguel. Day one in Ponta Delgada, day two at Sete Cidades and the west, day three around Furnas and the Nordeste, with a hot soak in the evening.
Days 5-7Fly to Faial or Pico. Whale watching and the Capelinhos drive on Faial, then the short ferry across to Pico for vineyards or the climb.

Avoid trying to see five islands in a week. Inter-island flights are short, but the transfers eat whole mornings.

When to go: weather and seasons

The climate is mild all year, roughly 14-17°C in winter and 22-25°C in summer. The real rule is that you may get four seasons in a day, so bring layers and a light rain jacket whenever you visit.

July and August are the driest and warmest months, and also the busiest and most expensive. June and September are the sweet spot for most people. Hydrangeas flower from roughly June to August, and the sea is warm enough for a swim by late summer. Spring is best for blue whales. Winter is quiet, wetter and sometimes disrupts flights and ferries, so leave some slack in your schedule.

Where to stay by area and budget

Ponta Delgada is the practical base on São Miguel: restaurants, airport 10 minutes away, and easy day trips by car. Budget guesthouses and hostels are available; mid-range hotels and renovated townhouses cost more in summer.

Furnas suits a night or two if you want to soak in the evening and wake up to steam rising from the ground. Rooms are fewer, so book early.

Ribeira Grande and the north coast are good for surfers and anyone who prefers a smaller town. Angra do Heroísmo is the best base on Terceira, and Horta works well on Faial. On Pico, a rural guesthouse near Madalena or São Roque is a better bet than anything central.

Rural self-catering houses, often with a kitchen and garden, are good value if you're in a group.

What to eat and where

The signature dish is cozido das Furnas, a stew of meat and vegetables slow-cooked for hours in the hot volcanic ground at Furnas. It's heavy, a bit theatrical, and worth doing once. Seafood is the other draw: lapas (grilled limpets with garlic and butter), fresh tuna steak, and octopus stewed in wine.

On Terceira, look for alcatra, a clay-pot beef dish. Anywhere on the islands, try bolo lêvedo, a soft, slightly sweet griddled muffin, and cheese from São Jorge, sharp and slightly peppery. São Miguel also grows its own pineapples under glass, and they taste better than the ones at home.

In Ponta Delgada, wander the Mercado da Graça for fruit and local products, then eat at a small tasca on the old town streets. Ask what the fish of the day is rather than reading the menu. Lunch is usually the cheapest meal.

Getting from the airport and getting around

Ponta Delgada's João Paulo II airport is only a few kilometres from the centre. A taxi costs around ten to fifteen euros, and it takes about ten minutes. Public buses exist but are limited and not designed for visitors.

For the rest of the island, rent a car. It's the only sensible way to reach the lakes and viewpoints, and booking ahead in summer matters. Roads are generally good, but narrow and foggy in the mountains.

Between islands, SATA Air Açores flies small planes on short hops, and Atlânticoline runs ferries, with the Faial-Pico crossing the most reliable and quickest, about half an hour. Other ferry routes are more seasonal, so check timetables before building a plan around them.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: around 60-90 euros a day with a hostel or guesthouse, supermarket lunches and shared car costs.
  • Mid-range: around 100-170 euros with a car, a comfortable hotel and restaurant dinners.
  • Comfortable: 200 euros or more for boutique stays and guided activities.

Whale watching usually costs somewhere around 50-70 euros. Thermal pools cost a few euros to roughly the price of a nice lunch, depending on the venue. Prices rise in July and August.

FAQ

How many islands should I visit on a first trip?

One or two. São Miguel alone fills a week; adding Pico or Faial gives you contrast without constant transfers.

Do I need a car?

Yes, on most islands. Public transport won't reach the main viewpoints, trailheads or lakes in a reasonable time.

Is the Azores good for a winter trip?

It's mild but wet and windy, and some boat trips and ferries run less often. It suits relaxed travellers who don't mind changing plans.

Can I swim in the sea?

Yes, mostly in summer. Many swimming spots are rocky natural pools or small beaches, and water temperature is cooler than the Mediterranean.

Is the Azores expensive?

It's cheaper than much of Western Europe, especially for food. Car rental and accommodation are the main costs, particularly in peak summer.

How many days do you need in The Azores?

7 days are enough to see the essentials of The Azores without rushing. The route in this guide is organised day by day.

Is The Azores worth visiting?

For the traveller who wrote this guide, yes: Nine volcanic islands sit in the middle of the Atlantic, about a three-hour flight from Lisbon and a little over four from Boston. 7 days are enough for the essentials.
