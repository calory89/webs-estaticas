Berlin travel guide: where to go, eat and sleep in 4 days

🔥 1 deal to Berlin🏨 Hotels in Berlin

Why go to Berlin

Berlin is big, flat, a bit scruffy and endlessly interesting. Few European capitals let you stand in front of a Prussian palace, a Cold War border strip and a former airfield turned public park on the same afternoon. The city was split for 28 years, and you can still feel it in the street layout, the architecture and the way each district has its own character.

It is also good value for a Western European capital. Museums are reasonable, food can be very cheap, and flights from across Europe are often among the best deals we see. Come for the history, stay for the cafés, the canals and the long summer evenings.

What to see in Berlin

Brandenburg Gate and the Reichstag

Go to the gate early, before the tour groups arrive, then walk to the Reichstag. The glass dome is free, but you must register online in advance. Book a slot near sunset if you can.

Holocaust Memorial and Topography of Terror

The field of concrete slabs near the Brandenburg Gate is best walked slowly. A few minutes away, the Topography of Terror sits on the site of the old Gestapo headquarters. It is free, serious and well explained in English.

Museum Island

Five museums on a small island in the Spree. The Neues Museum, home to the bust of Nefertiti, is the one to prioritise. Check which galleries are open before you buy a ticket, as parts of the island have been under renovation for years.

Berlin Wall Memorial, Bernauer Strasse

This is the best place to understand the Wall. You can see a preserved stretch of the border strip, the watchtower and the documentation centre. It is more moving than Checkpoint Charlie, which is a photo stop at best.

East Side Gallery

About 1.3 km of the Wall covered in murals, along the river in Friedrichshain. Walk it in the morning, when it is quieter and the light is good.

Gendarmenmarkt and Unter den Linden

The square with the French and German cathedrals either side of the concert hall is Berlin at its most formal. From here, walk along Unter den Linden towards the cathedral and the TV tower.

Charlottenburg Palace

The largest royal palace in Berlin, in the west of the city. The gardens are free to wander and a lovely break from the east-side crowds.

Tempelhofer Feld

The old airport is now a vast open field where locals cycle, skate, fly kites and barbecue. Walk on the former runway and you will understand why Berliners love this place.

Jewish Museum

Daniel Libeskind's zigzag building is worth the visit for the architecture alone. The exhibitions cover two thousand years of German-Jewish history.

Things to do

  • Join a free walking tour on day one. Tip your guide at the end. It gives you the historical thread that makes the rest of the trip make sense.
  • Browse the Sunday flea market at Mauerpark in Prenzlauer Berg. Come for the second-hand stalls and street food, and stay for the open-air karaoke in the amphitheatre in the afternoon.
  • Hit the Turkish Market along the Maybachufer canal in Neukölln, on Tuesdays and Fridays. Cheap olives, bread, cheese and a great atmosphere.
  • Rent a bike and cycle through the Tiergarten. Berlin has good cycle lanes and few hills.
  • Go out late. Berlin's club scene is famous, and the door policy at some places, Berghain above all, is strict and unpredictable. Smaller bars in Kreuzberg and Neukölln are an easier way in.
  • Climb Teufelsberg, the abandoned Cold War listening station on a hill in Grunewald, for views and graffiti. Check opening times before you go.

How many days and a suggested route

Three days covers the headline sights. Four lets you slow down and see a couple of neighbourhoods properly. Five is better if you want day trips, such as Potsdam with its Sanssouci Palace.

Day 1: Brandenburg Gate, Reichstag, Holocaust Memorial, Topography of Terror, then Gendarmenmarkt and an evening around Hackescher Markt.

Day 2: Museum Island in the morning, Berlin Cathedral, lunch in Mitte, then Bernauer Strasse and Mauerpark (on Sunday) in the afternoon.

Day 3: East Side Gallery, a wander through Kreuzberg, a döner, canal-side drinks, then dinner in Neukölln.

Day 4: Tempelhofer Feld, the Jewish Museum, or Charlottenburg Palace, and a slow final afternoon.

When to go

May to September is the easiest time. Summer days reach 22 to 26 degrees, with the odd hotter spell, and the sun sets late. The beer gardens and lakeside spots are in full swing. Spring and early autumn are milder and a little cheaper, and for many people they are the best compromise.

Winter is cold and grey, often around zero, but the museums, cafés and Christmas markets from late November make up for it. Pack a proper coat. Hotel prices are usually lowest in January and February.

Where to stay

Mitte is central and convenient for the main sights, though pricier and less characterful at night. Kreuzberg is lively, multicultural and great for food and bars. Friedrichshain is younger, louder and close to the East Side Gallery and the clubs. Prenzlauer Berg is quieter, with leafy streets and cafés, and suits couples and families. Charlottenburg feels more traditional, and is a good option if you want calm and big shopping streets.

On a budget, expect dorm beds from about 25 to 40 euros and simple private rooms from 60. Mid-range hotels are typically 90 to 140 a night, and upscale places start around 180. Prices rise during big events and trade fairs, so book early.

What to eat and where

Start with currywurst, a sausage with curry-spiced ketchup. Konnopke's Imbiss, under the elevated U2 tracks at Eberswalder Strasse, is a classic, and Curry 36 on Mehringdamm is the other usual suspect. Both can have queues.

The döner kebab is arguably Berlin's real signature dish. Mustafa's Gemüse Kebap, also on Mehringdamm, is famous for its vegetable version and often has a long line. Plenty of smaller shops nearby are nearly as good with no wait.

For something more local, try Königsberger Klopse (meatballs in caper sauce), schnitzel, or Eisbein if you are hungry and curious. On a sunny afternoon, order a Berliner Weisse, a sour wheat beer often served with a shot of raspberry or woodruff syrup. Sweet tooths should look for a Berliner, the jam-filled doughnut, which locals call a Pfannkuchen.

Markthalle Neun in Kreuzberg hosts a street food night on Thursdays, and it is a good, relaxed place to try many things at once. Late-night corner shops, known as Spätis, sell cold beer and snacks at all hours.

Getting from the airport and getting around

Berlin Brandenburg (BER) is about 18 km south-east of the centre. The fastest option is the Airport Express (FEX) or the regional trains RE7 and RE9, which reach central stations in around 30 minutes. The S9 and S45 S-Bahn lines are slower but go to different parts of the city. A single ticket covering zones A, B and C costs roughly 4 to 5 euros. Taxis cost much more and are not faster in traffic.

In the city, the network of U-Bahn, S-Bahn, trams and buses run by BVG is excellent. Buy tickets in the BVG app or at machines. If you have a paper ticket, validate it before boarding. A day or multi-day pass pays off quickly. Central Berlin is spread out, so expect to use transport every day, but it is also great for walking and cycling.

Rough daily budget

  • Shoestring: about 55 to 75 euros, with a hostel dorm, street food, and free sights.
  • Mid-range: about 110 to 160 euros, with a private room or hotel, sit-down meals, a museum and a bar.
  • Comfortable: 200 euros and up, with a boutique hotel, restaurant dinners and paid activities.

These are rough numbers and do not include flights. A döner or currywurst typically costs 5 to 8 euros, a beer in a bar 4 to 6, and most major museums 10 to 20.

Frequently asked questions

Is Berlin expensive?

Less than Paris, London or Amsterdam, though prices have risen. Food, transport and entertainment are reasonable, and accommodation is the main variable.

Is three days enough?

It is enough for the main sights and a taste of a few neighbourhoods. Four days is more comfortable, and you will want longer if you like museums.

Do I need German?

No. English is widely spoken, especially by younger people and in tourist areas. A few words such as bitte and danke are appreciated.

Is Berlin safe for tourists?

Generally yes. Watch for pickpockets around Alexanderplatz, major stations and crowded sights, and stay alert late at night around busy transport hubs.

Can I pay by card everywhere?

Not always. Germany is still more cash-friendly than many countries, and some small cafés, bars and market stalls prefer or require cash. Carry some euros.
