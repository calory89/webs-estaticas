New York in 5 days: what to see, where to eat and sleep

🏨 Hotels in New York

Why go to New York

New York is loud, expensive and tiring, and it is still one of the few cities that lives up to its reputation. You've seen it in a thousand films, yet standing on the Brooklyn Bridge at sunrise or watching the steam rise from a street grate in January still feels odd and thrilling. The city rewards walking, curiosity and a high tolerance for crowds. Five boroughs, hundreds of neighbourhoods and food from every corner of the planet, often for the price of a sandwich.

What to see: the places worth your time

  • Central Park. Skip the idea of "seeing" it and just wander. Bethesda Terrace, the Ramble and the Great Lawn make a good loop. Go on a weekday morning, when it belongs to joggers and dog walkers.
  • The Metropolitan Museum of Art. You cannot do it in one visit, so pick three wings. The Egyptian Temple of Dendur and the European paintings are an easy start. Plan for three hours at most.
  • Brooklyn Bridge and DUMBO. Walk from Manhattan to Brooklyn, not the other way round, so the skyline is in front of you. Early morning avoids the cyclist-and-selfie traffic jam. Finish under the Manhattan Bridge for the classic photo.
  • The High Line and Chelsea Market. A former rail line turned elevated park, running through Chelsea. Walk it north to south, then eat at the market below.
  • Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island. Book the ferry from Battery Park ahead of time. If you only want the view, the free Staten Island Ferry passes the statue at a distance and costs nothing.
  • 9/11 Memorial and Museum. Quiet, heavy and well done. The memorial pools are free to visit. The museum needs a couple of hours and some emotional energy.
  • A skyline view. Top of the Rock gives you the Empire State Building in the shot, which the Empire State Building itself cannot. Summit One Vanderbilt and Edge are the more modern alternatives. Go near sunset.
  • Grand Central Terminal. Even if you are not catching a train, look up at the star-painted ceiling in the main concourse. Ten minutes is plenty, and the Oyster Bar downstairs is a good lunch stop.
  • Greenwich Village and the West Village. Tree-lined streets, brownstones, jazz clubs and Washington Square Park. This is where New York feels most like a place people actually live.
  • Times Square. Pass through once, at night, for the neon. Then leave. No one needs more than twenty minutes here.

Things to do beyond the sights

See a Broadway show, and use the TKTS booth in Times Square or a same-day lottery for cheaper seats. Catch live jazz in a small Village club, which beats any tourist trap. Rent a bike along the Hudson River Greenway, or spend a lazy afternoon in Williamsburg's vintage shops and rooftop bars. Baseball fans should try a Yankees game in the Bronx or a Mets game in Queens. A summer evening at one of these is hard to beat.

How many days and a suggested route

Four to five days is the sweet spot. Three covers the greatest hits at a sprint, while a week lets you explore the outer boroughs properly.

  • Day 1, Midtown: Grand Central, Fifth Avenue, Top of the Rock at sunset, Times Square after dark.
  • Day 2, Central Park and Upper side: Central Park in the morning, the Met after lunch, dinner on the Upper West Side.
  • Day 3, Downtown: 9/11 Memorial, Battery Park, the Statue of Liberty ferry, then wander the Lower East Side for dinner.
  • Day 4, Brooklyn: Brooklyn Bridge walk, DUMBO, lunch in Williamsburg, an evening on the waterfront.
  • Day 5, Village and Chelsea: West Village, the High Line, Chelsea Market, then a show or jazz set.

When to go: weather and seasons

Spring (April to early June) and autumn (September to early November) are the best times. Temperatures are comfortable, the light is good and the parks look great. Summer is hot and humid, often above 30°C, with sticky subway platforms, though the city empties a little in August. Winter is cold, often around 0°C with the odd snowfall, but December brings the Rockefeller tree and the ice rinks. It also brings the highest hotel prices and the heaviest crowds. January and February are the cheapest months to fly, and you'll pay for it in wind chill.

Where to stay by area and budget

Midtown is the practical choice for first-timers, with subway lines everywhere and easy access to the major sights. It is also the most expensive and the least charming after dark. Lower Manhattan and the Financial District are quieter at night and often cheaper at weekends.

For atmosphere, try the Lower East Side or Greenwich Village, which are great for food and nightlife but pricier. Brooklyn, especially Downtown Brooklyn and Williamsburg, offers more space for the money and a short subway ride into Manhattan. Long Island City in Queens is one stop from Midtown and often has the best deals on larger rooms.

Roughly, hostels and shared rooms start around $60 to $100 a night. Decent mid-range hotels usually run $200 to $350. Anything with a view or a famous name goes well beyond that.

What to eat and where

Start with the dollar-slice-style pizza: a foldable, greasy slice from a counter, eaten standing up. Joe's Pizza on Carmine Street in the Village is a reliable classic. For a proper bagel with lox and cream cheese, go to Russ & Daughters on the Lower East Side. Katz's Delicatessen, on Houston Street, is the place for pastrami on rye. It is touristy and absolutely worth it.

The halal chicken over rice from a street cart is cheap, filling and everywhere in Midtown. In Chinatown, try soup dumplings at a place like Joe's Shanghai. Queens is the best food borough: Jackson Heights for Indian, Nepali and Colombian, Flushing for Chinese and Korean. In Brooklyn, Smorgasburg on weekends gathers dozens of food stalls. In summer, a hot dog at Nathan's in Coney Island is a rite of passage.

Getting from the airport and getting around

JFK: the AirTrain connects to the subway and the Long Island Rail Road at Jamaica, and the total to Manhattan is roughly $10 to $15, taking 60 to 75 minutes. A taxi from JFK to Manhattan has a flat fare of around $70 plus tolls and tip. Newark (EWR): the AirTrain links to NJ Transit trains to Penn Station in about 30 minutes. LaGuardia (LGA): no train, but the Q70 bus to Jackson Heights links to the subway, and taxis are cheap by comparison.

The subway runs 24 hours and is the fastest way to get around. A single ride costs about $3, and you can tap a contactless card or phone at the turnstile with OMNY. Weekly fare caps apply automatically. Buses are slower but good for crosstown routes. Yellow cabs and ride-hail are fine for late nights. Otherwise, wear comfortable shoes. You will walk a lot.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: around $120 to $170 a day, with a hostel, street food and free sights.
  • Mid-range: around $250 to $400, with a decent hotel, sit-down meals and a paid attraction a day.
  • Comfortable: $500 and up, with a central hotel, Broadway tickets and good restaurants.

Tipping is expected: roughly 18 to 20 percent in restaurants. Sales tax is added at the till, so listed prices are never final.

FAQ

Is five days enough for New York?

Yes, for the main sights and a couple of neighbourhoods. You'll leave wanting more, which is normal.

Is New York safe for tourists?

Generally yes, especially in the areas visitors frequent. Use normal big-city sense: watch your bag on crowded trains and be careful late at night in unfamiliar areas.

Do I need to book attractions in advance?

For the Statue of Liberty ferry, observation decks and popular shows, yes, particularly in high season. Museums are usually fine on the day.

What is the cheapest time to visit?

January and February, outside holiday periods, bring the lowest hotel and flight prices. Expect cold weather.

Should I rent a car?

No. Parking is costly, traffic is heavy and the subway is faster. Only consider a car for day trips beyond the city.
