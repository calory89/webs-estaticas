Hong Kong is two cities stacked on top of each other. One is glass towers, harbour views and €20 cocktails. The other is plastic-stool noodle shops, wet markets and temples full of incense smoke. The surprise for most first-timers is the third layer: about 40% of the territory is country park, so you can be on a ridge trail above the South China Sea 30 minutes after leaving your hotel. It suits food lovers, hikers, people who like big cities that actually work, and anyone who enjoys a good view from cheap public transport.

Give it 4 days. Three is enough for the classic sights, but the fourth lets you fit in Lantau Island or a proper hike, which is where Hong Kong pulls ahead of other Asian cities. It is not cheap: hotels are among the priciest in Asia. Food and transport, though, can be very affordable if you eat where locals eat and use the Octopus card.

What to see in Hong Kong: the places worth your time

Victoria Peak

The view from the Peak over Central, the harbour and Kowloon is the postcard shot, and it really is that good on a clear evening. Allow 2-3 hours. The Peak Tram costs about €11-14 (HK$90-120) return, and the queue at the bottom on Garden Road can run past an hour at weekends. Our tip: skip the paid Sky Terrace and walk the flat Lugard Road loop instead (free, about an hour), which gives a better view anyway. Go up just before sunset and stay for the lights.

Star Ferry

The cheapest great experience in Asia: a 10-minute crossing between Tsim Sha Tsui and Central for well under €1 (about HK$5). The boats are over 60 years old and the skyline view from the water beats most paid viewpoints. Sit on the upper deck, and do it once by day and once at night.

Tsim Sha Tsui Promenade

The waterfront walk from the Clock Tower east towards the Avenue of Stars faces the Hong Kong Island skyline straight on. Free, and 45 minutes is plenty. The Symphony of Lights show runs every evening at 8pm, but the skyline without the show is the real draw. Come at blue hour.

Tian Tan Buddha and Po Lin Monastery

The 34-metre bronze Big Buddha sits on a hilltop on Lantau Island, reached by 268 steps. Seeing it is free; the monastery serves a decent vegetarian lunch for about €12. Getting there is half the fun: the Ngong Ping 360 cable car (about €25-30 return, more for the glass-floor Crystal cabin) or MTR to Tung Chung, then bus 23 for a few euros. Allow half a day. Go on a weekday morning, because cable car queues at weekends are brutal, and check whether it is running: it closes for maintenance and in bad weather.

Tai O fishing village

Stilt houses, dried seafood stalls and a slower pace, on Lantau's west coast. Combine it with the Big Buddha (bus 21 from Ngong Ping, about 20 minutes). Allow 1-2 hours. The dolphin boat trips (about €5) rarely show you the pink dolphins these days, so treat it as a short harbour ride and nothing more.

Man Mo Temple and Hollywood Road

A smoky 19th-century temple in Sheung Wan, hung with giant incense coils that burn for weeks. Free, 20-30 minutes. Pair it with the antique shops along Hollywood Road, the curio stalls on Upper Lascar Row (Cat Street) and the street art on the lanes around PMQ.

Central-Mid-Levels Escalator

The world's longest outdoor covered escalator system climbs 800 metres up the hill from Queen's Road Central. Free. It runs downhill in the morning rush and uphill the rest of the day. Ride it up, hop off at Staunton Street or Elgin Street in SoHo for a drink, then walk down through the side lanes.

Temple Street Night Market

Fortune tellers, Cantonese opera singers, cheap gadgets and outdoor seafood and claypot rice stalls in Yau Ma Tei. Free to wander, 1-2 hours. The shopping is mostly junk; come for the atmosphere and a claypot rice dinner (about €8-10). It gets going after dark.

Mong Kok street markets

Mong Kok is the densest neighbourhood on earth, and it feels like it. Skip most of Ladies' Market and head for the specialist streets: the Goldfish Market on Tung Choi Street North, the Flower Market on Flower Market Road and the Yuen Po Street Bird Garden next to it. All free, 1.5-2 hours together. Mornings are best for flowers and birds.

Chi Lin Nunnery and Nan Lian Garden

A wooden Tang-style Buddhist complex built without a single nail, with a manicured classical garden across the road. Free, about an hour, right by Diamond Hill MTR. It is the calmest spot in Kowloon. Combine it with Wong Tai Sin Temple, one stop away, where locals shake fortune sticks.

M+ and the Hong Kong Palace Museum

The West Kowloon Cultural District has two serious museums side by side. M+ covers modern Asian art and design (about €15); the Hong Kong Palace Museum shows treasures on loan from Beijing's Forbidden City (about €6-8, more for special exhibitions). Allow 2-3 hours each. The M+ rooftop garden and the harbourfront park are free and great for sunset.

Dragon's Back

The best urban hike in Asia, and that is not an exaggeration. A ridge trail with sea views that ends at Big Wave Bay beach. Take MTR to Shau Kei Wan, then bus 9 to To Tei Wan. Allow 3-4 hours with a swim. Free. Start early in summer and bring more water than you think you need.

Sham Shui Po

Old Hong Kong, unpolished: fabric and button streets, electronics stalls on Apliu Street, and some of the city's best cheap food. Free, 2 hours. Go hungry.

Things to do in Hong Kong

Beyond the big sights, these are the experiences that make the trip.

  • Do a dim sum breakfast. Order har gow (shrimp dumplings), siu mai, cheung fun and baked char siu buns. Weekend mornings are when Cantonese families go, so expect to queue or share a table.
  • Ride the "ding ding" tram. The double-decker trams along the north of Hong Kong Island cost about €0.40 a ride. Sit upstairs at the front and ride from Sheung Wan to Causeway Bay.
  • Go to the Happy Valley races. Wednesday night horse racing in a stadium surrounded by towers, about €1-2 entry, cheap beer and a loud crowd. Racing season runs roughly September to July.
  • Take a junk boat or harbour cruise. The red-sailed Aqua Luna runs evening trips from Tsim Sha Tsui and Central for about €25-30. Touristy, but pretty.
  • Spend a day on the beaches. Shek O, Repulse Bay and Big Wave Bay are free, clean and have lifeguards in season.
  • Have a drink in Lan Kwai Fong or Sai Ying Pun. LKF is loud and boozy; Sai Ying Pun and Sheung Wan have better cocktail bars. Expect €12-18 a cocktail.
  • Hike Lion Rock or the MacLehose Trail. Lion Rock gives a view over the whole of Kowloon in about 2 hours return.

Hong Kong for free (or almost)

Hong Kong is expensive to sleep in, but a lot of the best bits cost nothing or close to it.

  • Lugard Road walk at the Peak (take bus 15 up for about €1.50 instead of the tram)
  • Star Ferry crossing, under €1
  • Dragon's Back and Lion Rock hikes
  • Chi Lin Nunnery, Nan Lian Garden, Man Mo Temple and Wong Tai Sin
  • Tian Tan Buddha (if you take the bus instead of the cable car)
  • Tai Kwun, the former Central Police Station turned arts complex on Hollywood Road, mostly free
  • The free 43rd floor viewing level at the Bank of China Tower has not been reliable in recent years, so instead try Sky100 alternatives such as the harbourfront at West Kowloon at sunset
  • Mid-Levels Escalator and the trams for pennies

Hong Kong itinerary: 4 days

Day 1: Central, Sheung Wan and the Peak

Morning: dim sum, then walk from Central to Sheung Wan via the Mid-Levels Escalator, Tai Kwun, Hollywood Road and Man Mo Temple. Afternoon: dried seafood shops on Des Voeux Road West, then the tram east for the ride. Evening: bus 15 or the Peak Tram up around sunset, Lugard Road loop, back down for dinner in SoHo.

Day 2: Kowloon

Morning: Star Ferry to Tsim Sha Tsui, then MTR to Diamond Hill for Chi Lin Nunnery and Nan Lian Garden, plus Wong Tai Sin. Afternoon: Prince Edward MTR for the Flower Market, Bird Garden and Goldfish Market, then Sham Shui Po for snacks. Evening: Temple Street Night Market and claypot rice, then the TST promenade for the skyline.

Day 3: Lantau Island

Morning: MTR to Tung Chung, cable car up early to the Big Buddha and Po Lin Monastery. Afternoon: bus to Tai O for snacks and stilt houses, then bus back to Tung Chung. Evening: West Kowloon for sunset at the harbourfront park, M+ if you still have energy, dinner in Jordan.

Day 4: Hike and beach

Morning: Dragon's Back from To Tei Wan down to Big Wave Bay. Afternoon: swim, then bus to Shek O for a lazy lunch, or head back for the Palace Museum if it is raining. Evening: Happy Valley races if it is a Wednesday; otherwise drinks in Sai Ying Pun and a final roast goose dinner.

What is overrated (and what to do instead)

The Peak Tram queue. The ride itself is short and the wait can be over an hour. Take bus 15 from Central, or a taxi, and spend the saved time on Lugard Road.

Symphony of Lights. Ten minutes of lasers that look faint next to the buildings themselves. Watch the skyline, but do not plan your evening around the show.

Ladies' Market. Fake handbags, phone cases and the same T-shirts you can buy anywhere. The Goldfish Market and Flower Market a few blocks away are far more interesting.

Hong Kong Disneyland. Small, pricey (around €75-90) and nothing you cannot see at a bigger Disney park. Unless you have kids desperate to go, spend the day on Lantau's trails instead.

When to visit Hong Kong

The best time to visit Hong Kong is October to early December: dry, sunny, 20-27°C and clear skies for views. March and April are also pleasant but often hazy. January and February are cool and grey (around 15°C), fine for city walking. June to September is hot, very humid and typhoon season, with sudden storms that can shut ferries and cable cars for a day; hotel prices dip a little, but the heat makes hiking hard. Hotels spike around Chinese New Year, Golden Week in early October and big events like the Rugby Sevens in spring. The quietest and cheapest stretch is usually late spring and midsummer outside school holidays.

Where to stay in Hong Kong: best areas

  • Tsim Sha Tsui: the best all-rounder for first-timers. Harbour views, transport everywhere, wide hotel range. Mid-range €110-180 a night.
  • Central and Sheung Wan: for bars, restaurants and walking to the sights. Pricier and smaller rooms; €140-250 for mid-range.
  • Causeway Bay and Wan Chai: shopping, trams, good food, slightly better value than Central. €100-170.
  • Mong Kok and Yau Ma Tei: the budget pick, close to street food and markets, noisy at night. €70-120, hostels €25-40 a bed.
  • Sai Ying Pun: quieter, local feel, good cafés and its own MTR station. €100-160.

Avoid Chungking Mansions guesthouses unless you are on a very tight budget and have read recent reviews: rooms are tiny and quality varies a lot.

What to eat in Hong Kong

Cantonese food is the reason many people come back. Here is what to order:

  • Dim sum: har gow, siu mai, cheung fun, baked char siu bao. Tim Ho Wan is the famous cheap Michelin option; any busy local dim sum hall works.
  • Roast meats: roast goose, char siu (barbecue pork) and crispy pork belly over rice. Yat Lok in Central is known for its goose.
  • Wonton noodles: small bowls of thin egg noodles with shrimp wontons in broth. Mak's Noodle in Central is the classic.
  • Cha chaan teng staples: Hong Kong milk tea, pineapple bun with a slab of butter, French toast drowned in syrup, macaroni soup with ham.
  • Egg tarts: warm, from any bakery, under €1.
  • Claypot rice: in winter especially, around Temple Street.
  • Street snacks: curry fish balls, egg waffles (gai daan jai), siu mai on a stick.

A cha chaan teng lunch costs about €6-9 (HK$50-80). Dim sum for two runs €20-30. A mid-range dinner with a drink is €25-40 per person; a smart restaurant with harbour views, €80 and up. Where not to eat: the harbourfront chains aimed at tour groups in TST, where you pay double for average food.

Airport to city centre and getting around

The fastest route is the Airport Express: 24 minutes to Hong Kong station in Central, about €13 (HK$115) one way, with a free shuttle bus to many hotels. The cheapest is the A21 bus to Mong Kok and Tsim Sha Tsui (about €4-5, 50-70 minutes), or the A11 to Central and Causeway Bay. A taxi to Central costs roughly €35-45 and takes 30-40 minutes.

Getting around is easy. The MTR is clean, fast and cheap (€1-3 a ride). Get an Octopus card, or just tap a contactless credit card or phone at MTR gates. Octopus also works on buses, trams, ferries and in 7-Elevens. Taxis are cheap by European standards: red ones serve the urban area, green ones the New Territories, blue ones Lantau. Day passes rarely beat pay-as-you-go unless you ride a lot.

How much a trip to Hong Kong costs

  • Shoestring: €60-80 a day. Hostel bed €25-40, local food €15-20, transport €5, mostly free sights.
  • Mid-range: €160-250 a day. Hotel €110-180, food and drinks €40-60, transport and taxis €10, one paid attraction.
  • Typical extras: Peak Tram €11-14, Ngong Ping 360 €25-30, harbour cruise €25-30, cocktail €12-18.

Day trips from Hong Kong

Macau: Portuguese colonial streets, casinos and Portuguese egg tarts. TurboJET ferry from Sheung Wan takes about an hour (€20-25 each way), or take the bus across the Hong Kong-Zhuhai-Macau Bridge. A full day. Bring your passport.

Lamma Island: no cars, a 1.5-hour walk between Yung Shue Wan and Sok Kwu Wan, then seafood by the water. Ferry from Central Pier 4, about 30 minutes, €2-3.

Cheung Chau: a fishing island with beaches, temples and giant fish balls. Ferry from Central Pier 5, 35-60 minutes depending on the boat, a few euros.

Sai Kung: seafood town, island boat trips and the Sai Kung geopark coastline. Minibus from Hang Hau or Diamond Hill, around an hour. Hike to Tai Long Wan if you have stamina.

Shenzhen: MTR East Rail line to Lo Wu or Lok Ma Chau, about 45 minutes from Kowloon. Check current mainland China entry rules for your passport before you go.

Common mistakes and scams

  • Tailor touts in Tsim Sha Tsui. Men on Nathan Road offering "copy watch" or suits. Ignore them; good tailors do not chase people on the street.
  • Fake monks. People in robes handing out amulets and then demanding a donation. Real monks in Hong Kong do not do this.
  • Electronics shops in tourist zones. Prices that change after you agree, swapped boxes. Buy at chain stores or the official brand shops.
  • Packing for constant heat. Malls, MTR and restaurants are aggressively air-conditioned. Carry a light layer.
  • Underestimating hikes. Summer heat and humidity knock out fit people. Start early, carry water, watch typhoon and heat warnings.
  • Expecting cards everywhere. Small noodle shops and markets often take only cash or Octopus.
  • Trying to see everything at the Peak by day only. Go late afternoon so you see both daylight and night.

Hong Kong FAQ

How many days do you need in Hong Kong?

Four days is ideal: two for Hong Kong Island and Kowloon, one for Lantau, one for a hike or beach. Three works if you skip the outdoors.

Is Hong Kong expensive for tourists?

Accommodation is expensive, but food and transport are cheap if you eat locally. Budget at least €60 a day on a shoestring and €160 or more mid-range.

Is Hong Kong safe to visit?

Yes, it is one of the safest big cities in the world for street crime. The main risks are touts, overpriced shops and summer weather.

Do I need a visa for Hong Kong?

Most EU, UK, US, Canadian and Australian passport holders can visit visa-free for 90 days or more. Check the Hong Kong Immigration Department website for your nationality.

What currency is used in Hong Kong?

The Hong Kong dollar (HKD), roughly HK$8-9 to €1. Cards work widely, but carry some cash for small eateries and markets.

Is the Peak Tram worth it?

The ride is nice but short, and queues can be long. Bus 15 is cheaper and quicker on busy days.

Can I visit Macau and Hong Kong in one trip?

Yes, Macau is an hour by ferry and makes an easy day trip. Add a night there if you want to see it lit up.

What is the best area to stay in Hong Kong for first-time visitors?

Tsim Sha Tsui, for its harbour views, transport links and range of hotels. Central suits you better if nightlife and restaurants matter more than budget.
