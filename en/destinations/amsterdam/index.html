Amsterdam travel guide: what to see, eat and skip in 3 days

🔥 3 deals to Amsterdam🏨 Hotels in Amsterdam

Why go to Amsterdam

Amsterdam is small enough to understand in an afternoon and rich enough to keep you busy for a week. The centre is a half-moon of 17th-century canals, lined with leaning brick houses and crossed by hundreds of bridges. Add world-class museums, a food scene that goes well beyond cheese, and easy day trips, and you have one of the best short breaks in Europe.

It is also a city that rewards getting up early. At 8 a.m. the canals are quiet, the light is soft, and the only traffic is cyclists with a coffee in one hand. By midday, the main streets are packed. Plan around that.

What to see in Amsterdam

  • Rijksmuseum. Rembrandt's Night Watch is the headline, but the Dutch Golden Age rooms, the ship models and the library are worth the ticket on their own. Go at opening time, or after 3 p.m.
  • Van Gogh Museum. Chronological and well laid out, so you see how his style changes. Tickets are timed and sell out, so book online a few days ahead.
  • Anne Frank Huis. Moving, short and quietly devastating. Tickets are released online in advance and go fast, so don't leave this to the last minute.
  • The canal ring. Walk the Herengracht, Keizersgracht and Prinsengracht rather than only looking at them from a boat. The stretch around the Westerkerk and the Jordaan is the prettiest.
  • Jordaan. A former working-class district of narrow lanes, small galleries and brown cafés. Good for aimless wandering, especially on weekday mornings.
  • Begijnhof. A courtyard of old almshouses just off the busy Spui. Step through the gate and the noise disappears. Keep your voice down, people still live here.
  • Vondelpark. The city's living room. Bring a picnic in summer, or walk through it on the way to the Museum Quarter.
  • Rembrandthuis. The painter's house and studio in the old Jewish quarter. Smaller and more intimate than the big museums.
  • Amsterdam Noord and NDSM. Take the free ferry from behind Centraal Station. On the other side you get street art, former shipyard warehouses and a city that feels less polished. The EYE Film Museum is right by the ferry landing.
  • Albert Cuyp Market in De Pijp. A long street market, open Monday to Saturday, with food stalls, cheap clothes and a very local atmosphere.

Things to do

Rent a bike, but be honest with yourself about your confidence. Amsterdam's cycle lanes are fast, and locals do not slow down for tourists. If you are nervous, stick to quieter areas such as Vondelpark, the Eastern Islands or the Amstel riverside path. Always lock the bike properly.

A canal cruise is touristy but genuinely useful on day one, because it shows you how the city is laid out. Choose a small boat with a live guide over a large glass-roofed one, and go in the evening if you can.

Spend an evening in a brown café, the old wood-panelled pubs where beer comes in small glasses and nobody rushes you. Café Chris and 't Smalle, both in the Jordaan, are classics. Walk through De Wallen (the Red Light District) once, in daylight if you want it calmer, and do not take photos of the windows. In the evening it is crowded and loud.

For a change of pace, cross to the A'DAM Lookout for views over the IJ, or browse the boutiques in the Nine Streets, a grid of small shops between the main canals.

How many days and a suggested route

Three days is the sweet spot. Two works if you skip museums, and four lets you add a day trip.

  • Day 1: the old centre. Early canal walk, Begijnhof, the Nine Streets, lunch near Spui, then a canal cruise or the Rembrandthuis. Evening drinks in the Jordaan.
  • Day 2: museums and parks. Rijksmuseum at opening, a walk through Vondelpark, Van Gogh Museum in the afternoon. Dinner in De Pijp.
  • Day 3: Jordaan, Anne Frank and Noord. Anne Frank Huis at your booked slot, a slow Jordaan lunch, then the ferry to Noord for an afternoon at NDSM or EYE.
  • Day 4, if you have it: Haarlem (about 15 minutes by train) for a calmer, more compact version of Amsterdam, or Zaanse Schans for windmills. In spring, Keukenhof's tulip gardens are the big draw.

When to go

Amsterdam is a maritime climate: mild, changeable, and rainy at any time of year. Pack a light waterproof whatever the season.

April to May is the pretty window. Tulips bloom, terraces fill up, and temperatures sit around 10 to 18°C. King's Day on 27 April turns the whole city orange and is wild fun, but hotels are expensive and the streets are crowded.

June to August brings long days and highs around 20 to 24°C, with the odd hot spell. It is also the busiest and priciest time.

September and early October are underrated: fewer crowds, softer light, and still comfortable. November to February is cold and grey, typically 2 to 7°C, but flights and hotels are cheaper, museums are quiet, and the cafés feel at their best. Late November to January has a light festival along the canals.

Where to stay

Amsterdam is expensive for accommodation, and rooms are often small, with steep stairs. A tourist tax of around 12 percent is added to most stays.

  • Jordaan and the canal ring: the most beautiful base and the most central. Good for couples and first-timers. Mid-range to high prices.
  • De Pijp: lively, full of restaurants and bars, close to Albert Cuyp Market. A good all-rounder for food lovers.
  • Museum Quarter and Vondelpark: quieter, residential and elegant, with easy tram links.
  • Amsterdam Oost: more local and a bit cheaper, with parks and good cafés. Handy for the Amstel and the Eastern Islands.
  • Noord: trendy, creative and often better value, with a short free ferry ride to the centre.

As a rough guide, a hostel dorm bed runs about €40 to €80, a simple hotel €130 to €200, and a comfortable central hotel from €200 upwards. Weekends and spring cost more. If prices look silly, check Haarlem or a hotel near a metro station outside the centre.

What to eat and where

Start with the snacks. A fresh stroopwafel, warm and still gooey, is best from a market stall. Bitterballen (crisp meat croquettes with mustard) go with a beer in any brown café. Herring with onions and pickles is the traditional street-food rite of passage, and the stalls around town do it well. If that is too much, try kibbeling, battered fried white fish.

For something sweet, order poffertjes (tiny fluffy pancakes) or a proper Dutch pancake, thinner and bigger than the American kind. Frites with mayonnaise are a serious matter here, and Vleminckx near Spui has been doing them for decades.

Indonesia was a Dutch colony, and rijsttafel, a spread of small spicy dishes with rice, is one of the city's best meals. Cheese shops will give you tastes of aged Gouda and Old Amsterdam. For a casual lunch, Foodhallen in the Oud-West district gathers lots of stalls under one roof. De Pijp has the best density of everyday restaurants.

Getting from the airport and getting around

Schiphol Airport is one of the easiest in Europe to leave. Trains run to Amsterdam Centraal in roughly 15 to 20 minutes, every few minutes through the day, for about €5 to €6. Buy a ticket at the machines or tap in with a contactless bank card or phone at the gates. Taxis are quick but cost far more, usually around €45 to €60.

Inside the city, walk as much as you can. Beyond that, GVB trams, buses and metro cover everything else. You can tap in and out with a contactless card, or buy a day ticket if you plan on using it a lot. Free ferries cross to Noord. Taxis rarely make sense, and driving a car in the centre is a bad idea: parking is expensive and the streets are narrow.

Watch for bikes everywhere, and never stand in a red-surfaced cycle lane.

Rough daily budget

  • Budget: around €90 to €130 a day, with a hostel bed, market food and one paid attraction.
  • Mid-range: around €180 to €260, with a decent hotel, restaurant dinners and museums.
  • Comfortable: €300 and up, in a canal-side hotel with good dinners and private tours.

Museum tickets are typically €20 to €30 each. A Museumkaart pays off only if you plan several visits in a short time. A beer in a bar is around €5 to €7, and a coffee €3 to €4.

Frequently asked questions

Is Amsterdam worth visiting for a weekend?

Yes. The centre is compact, so two full days will cover the major museums, a canal walk and a couple of good meals. Book museum tickets ahead.

Do I need to book Anne Frank Huis in advance?

Yes. Tickets are sold online for specific time slots and often sell out well ahead. Check the official site as soon as your dates are fixed.

Is Amsterdam safe for tourists?

Generally yes. The main risks are pickpocketing in crowded areas and bike theft. Keep an eye on your bag around Centraal Station and De Wallen, and stay out of the cycle lanes.

Can I get by with English?

Easily. Almost everyone speaks excellent English. A simple "dank je" (thank you) is still appreciated.

What is the cheapest time to fly to Amsterdam?

Generally January to March and November, excluding holidays. Midweek flights are often cheaper than weekends, and hotel prices drop in the same months.
