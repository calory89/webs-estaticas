Episode 1: The Day You Stop Waiting

Most people don't fail because they can't. They fail because they wait too long to start. Episode 1 of the book I'm writing, and one action to take in the next 24 hours.

September 29, 2026 · 13 min read · Updated October 11, 2026
Leer en español →

Chapter 1 of the book I am writing

There is a moment in life that almost nobody remembers exactly.

It has no date circled on the calendar. There are no photos. Nobody congratulates you. You probably don’t even notice it is happening.

It is the moment you start waiting.

You wait to have more money.

You wait to feel ready.

You wait to find the right job.

You wait for someone to believe in you.

You wait to have more time.

You wait for Monday, for January, for next year, for a better situation.

And while you wait, something extraordinarily dangerous happens:

your life keeps going.

The days don’t wait with you. Your body gets older. Your kids grow up. Your parents get older. Opportunities show up and disappear.

People with less talent than you start the projects you spent years imagining.

Other people build the companies you were going to build. Other people write the book you kept saying you would write one day. Other people take that trip, change careers, learn the language, start training.

Not necessarily because they are better.

Very often they just started earlier.

This is one of the first truths we have to accept if we want to grow, personally and professionally:

Most people don’t fail because they are incapable. They fail because they wait too long to start.

The myth of the perfect moment

We were taught to prepare. Study. Learn. Plan. Analyse. Ask. Compare.

And all of that is useful.

The problem starts when preparation becomes a hiding place.

There is an extremely elegant form of fear: endless planning.

It doesn’t look like fear. It looks like responsibility. It looks like intelligence. It looks like prudence.

But very often it simply means:

“I’m not ready to risk finding out whether I can do it.”

You can spend three months designing the perfect business without selling a thing.

You can read twenty productivity books without changing a single hour of your day.

You can watch hundreds of videos about investing without investing a euro.

You can spend years analysing whether to change jobs.

You can research how to write a book without ever writing chapter one.

Preparing feels good because it imitates progress. But preparation and progress are not the same thing.

You can run for hours on a treadmill and end up exactly where you started. You can also work incredibly hard and not move forward.

That is why one of the most important questions you can ask yourself is:

Am I moving forward, or am I just busy?

The answer can hurt. And that is exactly why it can change you.

Nobody is coming to rescue you

There is another uncomfortable idea that can give you enormous freedom: probably nobody is coming to save you.

Your boss may never spontaneously discover how talented you are. The ideal client may never turn up by chance. The investor you need may not call tomorrow. The perfect motivation may never arrive. Your circumstances may never be ideal.

And that is not a tragedy. It is an advantage.

Because once you understand that nobody is obliged to build your life for you, you get back something you had handed over without noticing:

control.

For years, a lot of people live reacting. To the boss, the market, their partner, the economy, the bills, the news, the circumstances.

But there is a huge difference between reacting to your life and running it.

People who build extraordinary lives don’t control everything that happens. Nobody does. But they tend to take responsibility for something far more important: their next move.

And that may be the smallest unit of change. Not your whole life. Not the next ten years. Not your destiny.

Your next move.

What can you do today?

Which conversation have you been avoiding for months?

Which email do you need to send?

Which skill do you need to start learning?

Which expense do you need to cut?

Who should you call?

Which habit is slowly draining your energy?

Which idea have you been sitting on for too long?

A life can look too big to change. The next move never is.

The distance between the life you have and the life you want

Try an exercise. Picture two versions of yourself.

The first is you today. Your income, your body, your relationships, your work, your habits, your energy, what you know, your confidence, the people around you.

Now picture yourself five years from now, if things go reasonably well. It doesn’t have to be a perfect life. Just a life that makes you proud.

Maybe you have a company. Maybe you got promoted. Maybe you work fewer hours. Maybe you are financially free. Maybe you are in better shape. Maybe you have an extraordinary relationship with your family.

Maybe you have learned to say no.

Maybe you have stopped trying to impress people you don’t even admire.

There is a distance between those two people. But that distance is rarely made of one big event. It is made of thousands of small decisions.

Getting up. Training. Reading. Calling. Asking. Saving. Investing. Learning. Sleeping. Saying no. Trying. Trying again.

Transformation is usually boring while it is happening. That is something social media almost never shows.

We see the founder after the company is built. The athlete after the body. The writer after the book. The investor after the money.

We rarely see the hundreds of ordinary mornings. The Fridays spent working when nobody is watching. The awkward conversations. The mediocre workouts. The projects that didn’t work. The calls nobody returned. The first clients. The doubts. The exhaustion.

Real personal growth rarely looks heroic. Most of the time it just looks like repetition.

Discipline is memory

Most people think discipline means forcing yourself to do things you don’t want to do. I prefer to see it another way:

Discipline is remembering what you want when your emotions are trying to make you forget.

At night you want to be fit. In the morning you want to keep sleeping.

On Sunday you want to build a company. On Monday you want to avoid the difficult call.

When you do your budget you want to save. When you see something you like you want to buy it.

When you imagine your future you want freedom. When some instant comfort shows up you want to skip the sacrifice.

That is where the real fight happens. Not between you and the world. Between two versions of you: the one that wants comfort now and the one that wants a better life later.

Both live inside you. And every day you vote for one of them.

Every workout is a vote. Every wasted hour is a vote. Every book you read is a vote. Every promise you keep to yourself is a vote. Every time you give up, you are voting too.

You don’t build your identity through big declarations. You build it through evidence.

If you say “I’m disciplined” but never do what you promised, your brain stops believing you.

If you say “I’m going to do it” and you do it, again and again, something powerful happens: you start trusting yourself.

Self-trust doesn’t come from looking in the mirror and repeating phrases. It comes from piling up proof.

You trust yourself when you become someone who keeps their word, especially the word you gave yourself.

Your biggest competitor is not another person

Comparison is an industry.

You will always find someone richer, younger, better looking, more famous, with a bigger company, a better house, more followers. And you will always find someone with less.

Comparison never ends because you can always move the reference point. That is why constantly competing with other people is a race with no finish line.

There is a much more useful competition: you against your potential.

The question stops being “am I doing better than him?” and becomes “am I using what I have?”

Because two people can get completely different results and both be succeeding.

One person can make ten million euros and be wasting enormous potential. Another can earn thirty thousand and have completely transformed their life.

Success shouldn’t be measured only by the size of the result. It should also be measured by the distance travelled.

You don’t need to become the best person in the world. You need to avoid becoming a mediocre version of yourself.

The invisible price of not deciding

Making decisions has a cost. So does not making them. That cost is just harder to see.

Imagine you have spent five years thinking about starting a business. You haven’t lost money. You haven’t shut down a company. You haven’t been through failure. It feels like nothing happened.

But something did.

You lost five years of learning. Maybe five years of clients. Five years of contacts. Five years of mistakes. Five years of experience. Five years of growth.

Inaction pays interest too. And it can be devastating.

When you put off a decision for too long, your life doesn’t freeze. You are quietly making another decision: to carry on exactly as you are.

This changes how you think about risk. We always ask, “what if I try and it goes wrong?” Very few people ask the opposite question:

What if I don’t try?

Where will you be in five years? And in ten? How much will it cost to change then?

Some decisions look risky in the short term and are extremely safe in the long term. And some look comfortable today and are dangerous for decades.

You don’t need a new life

One of the great traps of personal development is thinking you need to transform everything at once. New routine, new diet, new job, new company, new habits, new personality.

That rarely lasts. Because you are trying to build ten new people at the same time.

There is a far more powerful strategy:

improve one thing that improves several things.

Sleeping better can improve your energy, your decisions, your training and your work.

Cutting one distraction can win back hours every week.

Learning to sell can transform your career and your business.

Training three times a week can change your body and also how you see yourself.

Cutting expenses can give you the freedom to take professional risks.

Choosing the people around you well can change almost every decision you make from here on.

Look for levers, not movement. A small lever can move an enormous weight.

Your environment is writing your future

For one week, pay attention to your conversations. What do the people you spend the most time with talk about? Ideas, problems, business, opportunities? Or complaints, TV and other people?

The conversations you hear over and over slowly become the limits of what you consider normal.

If everyone you know thinks starting a business is crazy, you will need enormous courage to start one. If everyone trains, training feels normal. If everyone reads, reading feels normal. If everyone spends every euro they earn, saving feels strange.

Your environment doesn’t fully decide your future. But it negotiates with you every single day.

That is why growing sometimes feels strange. You start feeling uncomfortable in places where you used to feel at home.

It doesn’t always mean arrogance. Sometimes it just means your ambitions are changing.

One rule for the next twelve months

I want to suggest one rule. A very simple one.

For the next twelve months, shrink the distance between thinking and doing. When a good idea shows up, take one small action immediately.

  • Want to learn something? Find the course.
  • Want to meet someone? Send the message.
  • Want to get in shape? Do the first workout today.
  • Want to start a business? Talk to one potential client today.
  • Want to write? Write the first page.

You don’t need to finish the journey. You need to prove to yourself that you are someone who starts.

Because starting creates information. Information creates better decisions. Better decisions create progress. And progress creates motivation.

A lot of people wait for motivation to start. Very often it works exactly the other way round.

You start, you see progress, and then the motivation shows up.

Life doesn’t owe you time

There is a line worth remembering when you start putting things off too much:

someday is not a day of the week.

“Someday I’ll travel.” “Someday I’ll build something of my own.” “Someday I’ll spend more time with my kids.” “Someday I’ll take care of my health.” “Someday I’ll write.” “Someday I’ll change.”

The problem is that someday can slowly turn into never.

And maybe the biggest tragedy isn’t failing while trying to build the life you wanted. Maybe it is getting to the end knowing you never even found out what you were capable of.

You don’t need to live anxious. You don’t need to rush all the time. You don’t need to turn every second into productivity. A good life also needs rest, play, family, friends, silence and days with no goals at all.

But there is a huge difference between resting after living and using rest to avoid living.

Only you know which one you are doing.

The contract

Before you go on with this book, I want to ask you for something.

Don’t read the next pages as entertainment. Don’t collect ideas. Don’t underline thirty sentences only to forget twenty-nine of them tomorrow.

Do something.

This book shouldn’t change your life.

You should change your life using a few ideas from this book.

That is a huge difference.

Books don’t do push-ups for you. They don’t call clients. They don’t save money. They don’t apologise. They don’t walk away from destructive relationships. They don’t build companies. They don’t learn languages. They don’t raise your kids. They don’t make decisions.

You do.

So make a contract with yourself. You don’t need to write it down. Just accept one rule:

every chapter has to produce at least one action.

One. Not ten. One.

After this first chapter, ask yourself:

What have I been waiting too long to start?

Write the answer down.

Now shrink that answer until you find one action you can take in less than twenty-four hours.

Not when you feel ready. Not when you feel like it. Not on Monday.

Today.

Because there is a truth you will find again and again in these pages:

Your life rarely changes on the day you achieve something big. It starts changing on the day you decide you have waited long enough.

And maybe that day is today.

Your turn. Tell me in the comments what you have been waiting too long to start, and what your under-24-hours action is. Writing it down in front of someone makes it harder to skip.
